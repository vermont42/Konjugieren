# A4 · final verdict: partly (finding restored, prose replaced) · final grade: needs-hedging

The skeptic's kill does not hold. The finding's diagnosis and its grade are correct and should
reach Phase 4. The finding's *replacement prose* is wrong, and the skeptic is right about that, so
I have written new prose.

The sentence at issue, essay line 126, second sentence:

> Equipped with horses, wheeled carts, and perhaps bronze weapons, they moved westward in
> successive waves.

## What the skeptic got right

Three things, and they matter.

**Phase 1's replacement prose must not ship.** Its added sentence, "Widespread horse-based mobility
came later, arising across Eurasia around 2200 BC," is the title claim of Librado et al. 2024
restated as settled fact. That is the exact proposition Anthony, Trautmann and Heyd set out to
demolish in May 2026, and their abstract says the opposite in as many words: domestication's
"transformative effects on human mobility and social organization began as early as the fourth, if
not the fifth millennium BCE." Phase 1 proposed to cure an unhedged claim about a contested
question by installing a second unhedged claim about the same contested question, pointing the
other way. The skeptic caught this, and it is the single most valuable thing in either prior report.

**Phase 1's "what is actually true" overstates.** "The westward waves were wagon-borne, not
horse-borne" is one camp's position written as though the matter were closed. It is not closed.

**The citation error is real.** Phase 1 attached DOI `sciadv.ady7336` to "Trautmann, M. et al.
(2023)". That DOI is the 2026 Anthony/Trautmann/Heyd rebuttal; the 2023 Trautmann paper is
`sciadv.ade2451`. I confirmed both independently through the Europe PMC REST API. Phase 1's
bibliography is sloppy.

**And the 2026 paper is real.** I did not take either agent's word for it. Europe PMC returns
PMID 42127202 / PMC13170673, Anthony D, Trautmann M, Heyd V, *Science Advances* 12(20), May 2026,
open access, and the abstract text matches the skeptic's quotation word for word. The skeptic
fabricated nothing.

## Where the skeptic is wrong, or is not

The refutation rests on one load-bearing sentence: *"an essay is not obliged to hedge a sentence
that is true under both resolutions, and this one is."* Everything else in the verdict is support
for that sentence. It is false, and it is false in a way that the primary literature settles
directly rather than by argument.

**1. The contested proposition is not "the Yamnaya rode," it is "the Yamnaya spread horses
westward," and line 126 asserts the second one.** The skeptic quoted only Librado et al. 2021's
abstract, where the rejection is phrased as a causal claim about riding *driving* the expansion,
and reasoned from that phrasing that line 126, which makes no causal claim, is untouched. But the
paper's main text states the conclusion in a form that has no causal element at all. I pulled it
from the open-access text myself; neither prior report quotes it:

> "Yamnaya pastoralism did not spread horses far outside their native range."

That is the negation of "equipped with horses, they moved westward." Librado et al. 2024 then make
it sharper still. Anthony et al. quote them at page 821 excluding Yamnaya horses from "…any
significant involvement…in the Yamnaya-related or earlier human migrations from the steppe," and
concluding that "Yamnaya-related steppe migrations and the spread of DOM2 horses are, thus,
chronologically incompatible." The skeptic's leg 1 does not survive the sentence the source
actually wrote.

**2. "True under both resolutions" fails, and it fails on the pro-horse side too.** Check each
camp against the essay's sentence:

- Under Librado 2021 and 2024: explicitly denied, in the words just quoted.
- Under Anthony, Trautmann and Heyd 2026: *not asserted either.* Their claim, in full, is that
  "The Yamnaya migrations into Southeast Europe that began ∼3100 BCE were compatible with the
  spread of DOM2-clade horses." Compatible with. That is a statement that the genomic data fail to
  exclude the possibility, not a statement that it happened. Their own review of the direct
  evidence concedes how thin it is: "no Yamnaya, CWC, or Afanasievo grave has yielded useful aDNA
  except for one Afanasievo coat color allele," and horse bones in five Yamnaya kurgan fillings in
  Southeast Europe, "none … analyzed for aDNA."

So there is no live position in the July 2026 literature under which "equipped with horses, they
moved westward" is established. One camp denies it. The other defends it as *possible* and says
the sample is too small to conclude anything. The essay states it flat, in a bare participial
phrase, with no hedge at all. That is more confidence than either camp claims. A sentence that
outruns the strongest of its own supporting camps is the paradigm case of needs-hedging.

**3. The "already hedged at line 86" argument is a hedge on a different proposition.** Line 86
reads: "The Yamnaya may have been among the first to ride the horse, a reading some of their
skeletons support and other specialists dispute, and whether or not they rode, the recently
invented wheel gave them a mobility no earlier people had enjoyed." That hedges *riding*. It says
nothing about whether steppe horses travelled west, and it is forty lines and four section
headings earlier, in a section about the Yamnaya homeland rather than the migration. Line 86 is
correct as written and I would not touch it. But a reader who has been told "they may have ridden,
and the wheel is what made them mobile" has been given no reason to doubt, on reaching line 126,
that the horses came along. The skeptic calls a second hedge "redundancy, not accuracy." It would
be redundant if the two sentences made the same claim. They do not.

**4. The decisive error: the 2026 rebuttal makes the topic *more* contested, not less.** This is
the failure mode where a skeptic kills a hedge finding by winning an argument about the underlying
fact. The skeptic's third leg says the Librado rejection "is no longer the field's settled
position," having been rebutted. Correct, and I verified the rebuttal. But the finding was never
"Librado is right, so delete the horse." The finding was graded **needs-hedging**, and Phase 1 said
so explicitly, giving the 2026 material as its reason: "the horse question is genuinely live …
which is why the verdict is needs-hedging rather than factual error." A live dispute is the
*premise* of a hedging finding, not a refutation of it. The skeptic used evidence that the question
is open to justify leaving it stated as closed. That inverts the argument.

Incidentally, this also corrects the skeptic's charge that Phase 1 "never reckoned with what the
2026 paper actually concludes." Phase 1's Maier detail, six of ten models admitting steppe
admixture, comes from the 2026 paper, and Phase 1 used it precisely to hold the grade down from
factual-error to needs-hedging. Wrong year on the citation, right use of the content. Sloppy
sourcing is not the same as a wrong finding, and only the second decides a verdict.

**5. One point neither agent raised.** The essay puts horses first in a list whose next item is
"wheeled carts." Both camps agree, without qualification, that Yamnaya vehicles were ox-drawn.
Anthony et al. 2026: "When horse riding was combined with ox-drawn wagons ∼3200 BCE, the domestic
unit became mobile." Riding and traction were separate technologies in every account on offer. A
general reader meeting "horses, wheeled carts" in one breath will supply horse traction, which
nobody defends. Naming the oxen costs one word, is uncontested on all sides, and removes the
misreading at its source. That is a strict accuracy gain independent of how the horse dispute
resolves.

## Final disposition, and what Phase 4 should do

**Restore the finding. Grade needs-hedging, unchanged from Phase 1. Discard Phase 1's replacement
prose and use the prose below.**

In terms a reader who has seen neither prior report can follow: the essay tells its reader, flatly,
that the Yamnaya moved west equipped with horses. Whether steppe horses actually travelled west
with those migrations is one of the live disputes in Bronze Age archaeology right now. One side, on
horse genomes, says flatly that they did not. The other side, in a rebuttal published two months
ago, says the genomic sample is far too small to conclude that, and that horses spreading west is
"compatible with" the evidence. Neither side claims it is established. The essay should not claim
it either. The fix is one hedging word and one clarifying word, and it leaves the sentence true
whichever way the dispute goes.

Two notes for Phase 4:

  the English, since both localizations ship as translated.
- Do not touch line 86. It hedges riding correctly, it is compatible with both camps, and it is a
  different proposition from this one.

## Replacement prose

English, `docs/verb_history.txt` line 126, replacing the second sentence only:

> Equipped with ox-drawn carts and perhaps with bronze weapons and horses of their own, they moved
> westward in successive waves.

Twenty words against the original's seventeen. No markup delimiters, so no crash risk. No em dash,
no new parenthetical. "Horses of their own" is the precise hedge: Europe already had horses, and
what is disputed is whether the steppe animals came along. It preserves the essay's existing
"perhaps" rather than adding a new hedging construction, and it reads as one clause, not as a
correction.

German, `docs/verb_history_de.txt` line 92, replacing the second sentence only:

> Ausgestattet mit ochsengezogenen Karren und vielleicht mit Bronzewaffen und eigenen Pferden zogen
> sie in aufeinanderfolgenden Wellen westwärts.

If Phase 4 judges "ox-drawn" to be scope creep beyond the horse question, the minimum viable
edit is to move "perhaps" leftward and leave the carts alone: "Equipped with wheeled carts and
perhaps with bronze weapons and horses of their own, they moved westward in successive waves." I
recommend against this. The ox is uncontested, it costs a word, and once the horses are hedged the
unmodified "wheeled carts" is the remaining invitation to imagine a horse in harness.
