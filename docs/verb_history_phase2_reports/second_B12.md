# B12 · final verdict: partly · final grade: needs-hedging

The finding survives, but not as either prior agent framed it. Phase 1 was right that the
sentence needs rewriting and wrong that its three legs are the same error. The skeptic was
right to refuse factual-error and wrong about why, wrong about which leg is defective, and one
notch too low on the grade. The skeptic's central argument is refuted by the essay's own use of
the same verb 23 lines later, which neither prior agent checked.

The sentence, essay line 133:

> This had profound linguistic consequences: the Germanic languages developed free from the
> Romanization that transformed Gaulish into French, Iberian languages into Spanish and
> Portuguese, and Dacian into Romanian.

## What the skeptic got right

Three things, and they are not small.

It refused Phase 1's grade, and the refusal was correct. Phase 1 argued that because the essay
is about descent, a reader will read "transformed Gaulish into French" as descent and acquire a
false family tree, and that this makes the sentence a factual error. But the sentence does have
a true reading available, and Phase 1 conceded as much in its own text. A fact-check that
grades a sentence by its worst available reading, when a good reading exists and the essay
supplies corrective material two sentences later, is grading the reader rather than the text.

It noticed that Phase 1's replacement prose does not fix what Phase 1 itself identified. Phase 1
raised Basque as a reason not to write of Iberian languages turning into Spanish and Portuguese,
then produced a replacement containing "the languages of Iberia," which asserts the same thing
Basque contradicts. That is a real catch.

It was honest about its sources, and correctly noted that the source-quality problem is not
where the verdict is decided. That is the right instinct: a weak source and a wrong finding are
different things, and the skeptic did not confuse them.

## Where the skeptic is wrong, or is not

**Its load-bearing argument fails against the essay's own usage.** The skeptic's case rests on a
claim about sentence shape: "A descent claim does not name an external imperial process as the
thing that did the transforming," and, in its self-assessment, "the protocol the essay taught has
no external agent in it; every genuine descent sentence in the essay has a language or a stage as
its subject." Both prior agents argued this from general English usage. Neither checked how this
essay uses the verb. It uses it once more, at line 156:

> This series of sound changes transformed voiceless stops into fricatives or affricates
> (p → pf or ff; t → ts or s; k → ch)

That is an external process as grammatical subject, "transformed X into Y" as the frame, and a
pure continuity claim as the meaning: the pf is what the p became. Line 178 does it again, "the
system has been transformed by millennia of change," again meaning the same system persisting
through change. So within this essay, an agentive subject on "transform" does not signal
replacement. It signals exactly the descent reading the skeptic said it ruled out. The essay
teaches the reader that protocol in the section on the High German consonant shift, and the
reader applies it at line 133.

The general-usage point runs the same way. "Transform X into Y" predicates identity across the
change: the thing that was X is the thing that is now Y. Naming who did it says who did it, not
that X survived. The test that settles it is the object, not the subject. Swap the language for
the region and the sentence is true and idiomatic: "the Romanization that transformed Gaul into
France." The essay wrote the language name. That is where the false implicature comes from, and
the skeptic's grammatical defense never touches it.

**It upheld the weakest leg and dismissed the strongest.** The skeptic kept "Iberian languages"
as the one genuinely defective leg and gave two reasons. The first, that the phrase parses as
"Romanization transformed Spanish and Portuguese into Spanish and Portuguese," requires lifting
the phrase out of a three-item parallel whose other two items are Gaulish and Dacian. In that
slot no reader reaches Ibero-Romance. That is the uncharitable-lift failure mode. The second
reason, Basque, is true but thin: the sentence quantifies over nothing, and my own research cuts
against the skeptic here rather than for it. The Paleohispanic languages actually were plural
and actually were replaced by Latin, Iberian and Celtiberian and Lusitanian and Tartessian
among them, all of them gone by roughly the first or second century AD. So the plural "Iberian
languages" is apt for a reason the skeptic did not credit, and Basque is a footnote-sized
exception to a sentence that never claimed universality. This leg is a nitpick and the skeptic
graded the whole finding by it.

**Neither agent checked the Dacian leg, and it is the one that is genuinely overstated.** Phase 1
flattened all three legs into "one error stated three times." The skeptic accepted the flattening
and answered it in kind, "repeating a looseness, not an error." The brief had flagged Dacian as
the odd leg; Phase 1 rejected that and the skeptic never revisited it. Both were wrong, and the
brief's instinct was right. Gaulish and Iberian are settled cases of language shift in a
territory that stayed under Rome. Dacia is not. Rome held it for about 165 years and abandoned
it around 271, and where Romanian actually formed is one of the live unresolved questions in
Romance linguistics. The Cambridge History of the Romance Languages says the available
historical, archaeological, and linguistic data are not adequate to settle it; the Oxford Guide
to the Romance Languages repeats that the location and extent of the territory where
Daco-Romance originated is uncertain. On top of that, the identity of the Romanian substrate is
itself disputed among Dacian, Thraco-Dacian, and Illyrian, a point both prior agents reported
and then set aside as immaterial. It is not immaterial: it is half of what the leg asserts.

So the sentence does not contain one error three times. It contains a loose descent implicature
three times, plus, in the Dacian leg only, a flat statement of something two standard handbooks
decline to state.

**One argument-from-absence to name.** The skeptic's third defense was that nothing downstream
builds on the false reading, "so the misreading has nowhere to go and nothing to reinforce it."
Nothing downstream reinforces it, true. Nothing downstream retracts it either. An absence of
downstream traffic is not evidence that the reader did not take the wrong turn; it only means
the essay never checks.

## My own research

Five retrievals, independent of both prior agents' source lists.

The result that moved me is the handbook pair on Romanian, which neither prior agent consulted.
Both the Cambridge History (2013) and the Oxford Guide (2016) treat the origin territory of
Daco-Romance as unsettled. That converts "the Romanization that transformed Dacian into
Romanian" from loose phrasing into a knowledge-state claim the essay is not entitled to make
flatly, in either language, since both localizations ship as translated.

I then tried to refute my own new angle, which is the check that matters most. Popular reference
works do state the Dacia account flatly: the standard encyclopedia framing has Romanian evolving
from the Latin of the province, with Trajan's colonists and intense Romanization. So the essay
is repeating the mainstream popular account, not inventing anything. That is exactly why this is
not a factual error. It is also why it is not nothing, because the essay's subject is genealogy
and the popular account is the one the handbooks flag.

On Iberia the research went against the skeptic, as described above: Latin replaced the
Paleohispanic languages, Proto-Basque excepted, so the plural is right and Basque is a small
exception rather than a counterexample to the claim as written.

## Final disposition, and what Phase 4 should do

**Restored in substance, regraded, and relocated.** Not Phase 1's factual-error and not the
skeptic's nitpick.

Not factual-error, because grading it so would require certifying that the descent reading is the
only reading, and it is not: "Romanization" as agent leaves a true reading open, and line 135
names adoption of Vulgar Latin as the mechanism for exactly these peoples. Not nitpick, because
grading it so would require certifying that the descent reading is unavailable, and line 156
proves the essay itself uses "transformed X into Y" to mean continuity, so the reading is not
merely available but trained. Needs-hedging is the grade that is honest about both, and it is
also the grade the Dacian leg earns on its own terms: the essay states as settled what the
standard handbooks say cannot be settled.

Phase 4 should replace the final sentence of line 133 and its counterpart in
`docs/verb_history_de.txt` line 99. The fix costs five words fewer than the original and gives
back three things at once: Latin as the ancestor, regions rather than languages as the objects
so the descent implicature disappears, and no commitment on where Romanian formed. Nothing else
in the section changes, and B9's attribution of the outcome to the halt in Roman expansion is
untouched.

## Replacement prose

English, replacing the final sentence of line 133:

> This had profound linguistic consequences: the Germanic languages developed free from the
> Romanization that replaced the languages of Gaul, Iberia, and the Danube provinces with the
> Latin that became French, Spanish, Portuguese, and Romanian.

29 words after the colon against the original's 34. Regions as objects, so no language is said to
have turned into another. "The Latin that became" puts the ancestor where the descent actually
starts and keeps the essay's own descent verb doing honest work. "The Danube provinces" rather
than "Dacia" sidesteps the unresolved question entirely while staying true: the Latin that became
Romanian came from that Roman world, whichever bank of the river it was spoken on. Order is
preserved, so the reader can still zip Gaul to French, Iberia to Spanish and Portuguese, the
Danube provinces to Romanian. No markup characters, no asterisk, no em dash, no new
parenthetical, and the sentence sits mid-paragraph so no heading spacing is involved. The
`~limes~` pair earlier in line 133 is not touched.

German, replacing the final sentence of `docs/verb_history_de.txt` line 99:

> Dies hatte tiefgreifende sprachliche Konsequenzen: Die germanischen Sprachen entwickelten sich
> frei von der Romanisierung, die in Gallien, Iberien und den Donauprovinzen die dortigen
> Sprachen durch das Latein ersetzte, aus dem Französisch, Spanisch, Portugiesisch und Rumänisch
> wurden.

"Romanisierung" stays as the agent, matching the English. "Ersetzen durch" carries replacement,
and "aus dem ... wurden" carries descent from Latin, so the German makes the same two moves the
English does. "Iberische Sprachen" and "Dakisch" both go, for the reasons above.

If Josh wants to keep the dead languages named, this is the best version that does so, and it is
second best:

> This had profound linguistic consequences: the Germanic languages developed free from the
> Romanization that displaced Gaulish, the languages of Iberia, and Dacian in favor of the Latin
> that became French, Spanish, Portuguese, and Romanian.

It fixes the descent implicature and keeps the roll call. It is weaker because the parallel still
invites the reader to pair Dacian with Romanian, which is the pairing the handbooks will not
endorse. Prefer the first.
