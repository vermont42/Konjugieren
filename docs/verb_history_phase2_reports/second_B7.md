# B7 · line 131 · final verdict: **partly upheld** (finding restored) · grade: **nitpick**

The skeptic's kill does not hold. The finding goes back on the list at the grade Phase 1
originally gave it, with new prose. Phase 1's own replacement is not the fix.

## What the skeptic got right

Three things, and they are the reason this stays a nitpick rather than becoming anything larger.

1. **The sentence asserts nothing false.** `Arminius (Hermann)` is an alternate-name gloss.
   Germans have called this man Hermann for roughly five hundred years, the monument above
   Detmold is the Hermannsdenkmal, and popular German writing still says Hermann der Cherusker.
   Nothing in the sentence states that Hermann is his Germanic name, his earlier name, or the
   source of the Latin one. Phase 1 conceded this itself. Any grade above nitpick would be wrong,
   and the skeptic was right to refuse an upgrade.
2. **The gloss earns its place.** This is an app for English speakers learning German. The
   paragraph is the essay's only bridge to Hermannsdenkmal, Kleist's Hermannsschlacht, and
   Hermann der Cherusker. Deleting the German name would be a net loss. Any fix has to keep it.
3. **Phase 1's replacement prose is genuinely bad.** "whom Germans have called Hermann since
   the sixteenth century" reads as unbroken present usage when German scholarship and much
   current popular writing have gone back to Arminius; it hangs a relative clause off "Cherusci"
   that has to be parsed back to "Arminius"; and it moves the scannable German name to the tail
   of a sentence that already carries a parenthetical and two passives. The skeptic's objections
   here are correct and I adopt them.

The skeptic also got the underlying history right, and its `Heer` citation from the
Etymologisches Wörterbuch des Deutschen is better evidence than anything Phase 1 produced.

## Where the skeptic is wrong, or is not

The kill rests on three legs. Leg one, "no false assertion", holds. The other two do not, and
they fail in the same way: each quietly assumes the very name-equivalence that does not exist.

**Leg two, the essay's internal parenthetical convention, is backwards.** The skeptic argued that
line 142 ("Wōðanaz (later Wōden (Old English) and Óðinn (Old Norse)), Þunaraz (Thunor and Þórr),
Tīwaz (Tiw and Týr)") establishes a habit of older-form-head, later-form-parenthesis, so a reader
carrying that habit to line 131 lands on the right chronology.

Two problems.

- *It is eleven lines too late.* Line 142 comes after line 131. A reader moving through the essay
  linearly cannot have internalized a convention the essay has not yet shown him. The skeptic's
  argument requires reading backwards.
- *The convention is not about chronology, it is about descent.* Every parenthesized name at
  line 142 is a genuine etymological reflex of its head: Wōðanaz really does give Wōden and
  Óðinn, Þunaraz really does give Thunor and Þórr, Tīwaz really does give Tiw and Týr. That is
  the only thing those parentheses do. Back-apply that convention to `Arminius (Hermann)` and it
  delivers "Hermann is the later German reflex of Arminius", which is exactly the false etymology
  the finding is worried about. The essay's one internal signal, if a reader uses it at all,
  points toward the folk reading, not away from it.

I checked this rather than assuming it: a scan of the essay for `Name (Name)` glosses returns
line 131 and the three god names at line 142, and nothing else. The god list is the sole precedent,
and it is a descent list.

**Leg three, the Charlemagne analogy, begs the question.** The skeptic wrote that condemning this
parenthesis "would condemn every 'Charlemagne (Karl der Große)' and 'Confucius (Kongzi)' in print".
But Charlemagne and Karl der Große *are* the same name in two languages, both from Carolus Magnus;
Confucius *is* a Latinization of Kong Fuzi. Both analogies are cases where the equation in the
parenthesis is real. The disputed case is the one where it is not: Hermann is a sixteenth-century
humanist substitution built on a false etymology, Old High German *heri* plus *man*, from a language
that did not exist in 9 AD, and it is not descended from, cognate with, or a translation of
Arminius. The analogy holds only if you first grant the thing in dispute.

**Where the skeptic is right against Phase 1.** Phase 1 claimed the surrounding sentence
"invites" the wrong reading by calling him a Romanized chieftain. That is overstated. "Romanized"
in that sentence is doing work about citizenship, equestrian rank, and auxiliary command; it is
not a claim about names. The finding's diagnosis survives, but this particular premise of it does
not, which is part of why my verdict is "partly" and not a clean restoration.

**The category error that produced the kill.** The skeptic titled its report "refuted" and argued
at length that the essay does not assert that Arminius is a Latinization of Hermann. Phase 1 never
said it did. Phase 1 said the parenthesis is an unglossed apposition whose two available readings
are both live, graded it a nitpick for exactly that reason, and proposed a cheap fix. Refuting an
upgrade nobody proposed is not refuting the finding. A nitpick is by construction a small,
low-cost improvement, and "there is no false assertion here" is an argument against promoting it,
not against doing it. The skeptic's own remaining objection, that the fix costs more than it buys,
was true of Phase 1's prose and is not true of a three-word fix.

## My own research

Five searches plus six fetches, taking neither prior agent's sources on trust. Handbook-level
material on this specific point is mostly paywalled (Benario 2004 in *Greece & Rome*, Winkler's
*Arminius the Liberator*, OUP 2016), so I could not read the primary scholarship directly and do
not pretend otherwise. What I could establish:

- **The Hermann name is a sixteenth-century humanist coinage, not a survival.** A German
  scholarly-society treatment states it flatly: the humanist reception revived interest in the
  historical Cheruscan, and "auf diese Zeit, genauer auf das beginnende 16. Jahrhundert, geht
  auch der neue Name Hermann für den historischen Arminius zurück". It also notes that Luther is
  *frequently* named as the coiner, which is weaker than Phase 1's flat attribution and weaker
  than the popular sources.
- **The two names are not etymologically connected, and Arminius's Germanic name is unrecorded.**
  German-language summaries state that the meaning of Arminius is still undetermined, that it has
  little connection to Germanic and certainly none to Hermann, and that no Cheruscan name for him
  is transmitted. Hermann's first element is Old High German *heri*, first attested in the eighth
  century.
- **Winkler's chapter two is described in reviews as covering "Arminius' renaming to 'Hermann'
  and associated false etymologies".** That phrase, from the secondary literature on the standard
  monograph, is the closest I got to a handbook framing, and it labels the equation a false
  etymology rather than an alternate name.
- **The folk equation is still what a reader will find if he goes looking.** Both of my searches
  returned, near the top and stated flatly, "the name Arminius was identified as a Latinized form
  of the German name Hermann" and "Hermann is the German equivalent of the Latin name Arminius".
  This corroborates the skeptic's own stated weakest point: the wrong reading is not the reader's
  private invention, it is the ambient one. The skeptic identified this and then dismissed it with
  the question-begging analogy above.

Net effect on the facts: both prior agents were substantially right, the skeptic more precisely so.
Neither agent's factual account changes the disposition. What changes it is that the skeptic's two
supporting arguments do not survive inspection.

## Final disposition, and what Phase 4 should do

**Restore the finding at grade nitpick.** Not a factual error: the essay makes no false assertion,
and Phase 4 should not treat it as one. Not "none" either: a bare two-word parenthesis after a
personal name, in a paragraph that has just called the man Romanized, in an essay whose only other
name-parenthesis is a descent list, sits one word away from an ambiguity that a century of
reference works actively taught readers to resolve wrongly.

Apply the prose below to `docs/verb_history.txt` line 131 and `docs/verb_history_de.txt` line 97.
It keeps the German name where it is and scannable, adds "later" to close the false reading, and
carries no markup, so it cannot break the Info screen. It adds no em dash and no new parenthetical:
the parenthesis is already there and is being expanded, not introduced. If Phase 4 is triaging by
cost, this is a five-word edit in each language and should be near the front of the cheap pile.

## Replacement prose

**English**, `docs/verb_history.txt` line 131:

> Three Roman legions under the command of Publius Quinctilius Varus (perhaps 20,000 soldiers)
> were ambushed and annihilated by an alliance of Germanic tribes led by Arminius (known to later
> Germans as Hermann), a Romanized chieftain of the Cherusci.

**German**, `docs/verb_history_de.txt` line 97:

> Drei römische Legionen unter dem Kommando von Publius Quinctilius Varus (vielleicht 20.000
> Soldaten) wurden von einer Allianz germanischer Stämme unter der Führung von Arminius (späteren
> Deutschen als Hermann bekannt), einem romanisierten Häuptling der Cherusker, überfallen und
> vernichtet.

Why this shape and not Phase 1's. The essay already uses this exact construction one line earlier,
at line 130: "These Germanic-speaking peoples (known to the Romans as ~Germani~)", and the German
translation matches it at line 96, "(den Römern als ~Germani~ bekannt)". So the fix is not a new
habit imposed on the essay, it is the essay's own nearest parenthetical form reused. "later" is the
essay's own word for this relation, from line 142's "Wōðanaz (later Wōden...)". Nothing is dated,
nothing is moved to the tail, no relative clause dangles, and the German name stays where a reader
scanning for it will find it.
