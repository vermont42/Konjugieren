# C1 · final verdict: partly upheld · final grade: nitpick · the skeptic's kill does not hold

Phase 1 proposed a nitpick against line 136. The Phase 2 skeptic refuted it at high confidence and
graded it none. I am restoring the finding at Phase 1's original grade, nitpick, with prose of my
own rather than Phase 1's. The skeptic's factual defense of the essay is sound as far as it goes,
but the single number it rests on was computed for a different part of Europe than the one the
essay is talking about, and the skeptic reached that number by silently replacing the essay's own
definition of "northern Europe" with a narrower one that made its source apply.

## What the skeptic got right

Three things, and they matter.

**The essay is not making a false statement.** Northern Europe at 9 AD was not a cleared
countryside with woodlots. It was a mosaic, and woodland was a large part of it. Phase 1's phrase
"the settled zones were largely open" is true of the settled zones and is not a description of the
region. The skeptic is right that a sentence about where people lived can legitimately name the
region rather than the farmyard.

**Feddersen Wierde was a bad exhibit and deserved the beating it got.** Phase 1 cited it, from
Wikipedia, as a settlement with no trees on it. A tidal salt marsh has no trees because it is a
tidal salt marsh. It is evidence about tides, not about clearance, and it was the weakest of
Phase 1's four examples. The skeptic caught that cleanly.

**Two of the three objections to Phase 1's replacement prose land.** "In a landscape of cleared
fields, pasture, and coastal marsh as much as of forest" asserts rough parity between cleared and
wooded land at 9 AD, which no source Phase 1 cited establishes, and it promotes the North Sea marsh
strip to one of three defining features of northern Europe on the strength of one site. The third
objection, that a three-item list sits badly in front of the paragraph's four-cereal and
four-livestock lists, is a fair voice complaint. I am not adopting Phase 1's sentence.

## Where the skeptic is wrong, or is not

### The ~60% figure is for a different region than the skeptic used it for

This is the failure that decides the finding. The skeptic's whole case is one number: "still ~60%
forest cover at 550-750 CE" in north central Europe, therefore the north German interior was
majority wooded at 9 AD, therefore "scattered through the forests" is archaeology and not Tacitus.

I pulled the Roberts et al. 2018 full text and read the paragraph the number comes from. The number
is not a regional average that Roberts et al. computed for a region they defined. It is a
cross-comparison figure computed to match the footprint of a 1952 historical map:

> "For example, Schlüter used a variety of historical evidence to map the area of forested land
> across north central Europe prior to Mediaeval clearances and also for 1900 CE. … While Schlüter
> used 900 CE as a starting date, our pollen-based land-cover reconstructions indicate that forest
> clearance in north central Europe had already begun by 850 ± 100 CE (i.e. during Carolingian
> times), and we therefore use 550–750 CE as a pre-Mediaeval baseline for comparison. **According
> to the PBMsc, forest cover at this time was ~60% for the same area**, and declined to 45% for the
> most recent time window (post-1750 CE)."

"The same area" is Schlüter's area. Reference 48 in the same paper names it: *Atlas Östliches
Mitteleuropa*, the atlas of **eastern** Central Europe. So the skeptic's number is a pollen-based
estimate reprojected onto a map of the lands from roughly the Elbe eastward, made for the purpose
of checking a palynological reconstruction against a historical-geographic one at the eve of the
medieval clearances. Transporting it to northwestern Germany, the North Sea coast, Jutland, and the
Baltic littoral is not something the paper licenses. The skeptic did that transport in one
unremarked step, and everything else in the refutation is built on it.

### The skeptic replaced the essay's geography with one that suited its source

The essay defines its own "northern Europe" six lines earlier, at line 130:

> "By the first millennium BC, a recognizable Proto-Germanic language had emerged in southern
> Scandinavia and along the North Sea and Baltic coasts. These Germanic-speaking peoples (known to
> the Romans as ~Germani~) developed a culture adapted to the forests and coastlines of northern
> Europe."

Southern Scandinavia, the North Sea coast, the Baltic coast. Now read the sentence in Roberts et
al. that the skeptic quoted, in full, and notice that it has three buckets, not two:

> "…in particular between north central Europe, where the majority of forests remained intact until
> Mediaeval times, and **north western Europe where most forests had already been cleared in Bronze
> and Iron Age times. Some other areas, such as coastal regions of northern Europe (e.g. southern
> Scandinavia) also experienced substantial forest loss during later prehistory.**"

The essay's stated Germanic homeland is distributed across the second and third buckets, the
early-cleared ones. The skeptic assigned it to the first, on the ground that "the battle in the
essay's own preceding paragraph took place" in the interior. Kalkriese, the candidate Teutoburg
site, is near Osnabrück on the northern edge of the North German lowland, not in the Mittelgebirge
core that carries Roberts' late-clearance signal. And the skeptic then dismissed the coastal and
Scandinavian zone as "a Danish and Frisian regional footnote". It is not a footnote to this essay.
It is the essay's own answer to the question of where these people lived, written two paragraphs
above the sentence under review.

This is the uncharitable-reading failure mode running in the other direction than usual: not
reading the essay's sentence out of its paragraph, but reading its *source* out of the essay's
paragraph.

### The date the number was measured at is the wrong date, and the skeptic knew it

The skeptic quoted, in its own sources section, the sentence immediately preceding the one it built
on:

> "The decline in mid-latitude forest cover continued through Iron Age and Roman times, but was
> halted or reversed in the post-Roman Migration period, around 1700–1300 yr BP. … Pollen data
> therefore confirm that the post-Roman decline in rural settlement was accompanied by re-growth of
> secondary forests in many regions."

550 to 750 CE is 1400 to 1200 BP. It sits at the far end of that regrowth window. So the ~60% is a
post-regeneration value, and 9 AD sits on the declining limb that ran down to a minimum around
250 CE. Roberts et al. describe the regrowth as stabilization on one method and "a slight increase"
on another, so I do not claim this flips the number outright. What it does do is dispose of the
skeptic's rhetorical framing: "still ~60% … at 550-750 CE" invites the reader to treat 60% as a
floor for 9 AD, when the paper's own trajectory makes the Roman Iron Age the *least* wooded moment
in temperate Europe before the medieval clearances. That is the opposite of the emphasis the
skeptic drew from it, and it points at Phase 1.

### The number chosen is the most forested of the three the paper offers

Roberts et al. compare three methods and say plainly that the two the skeptic quoted read high:
"PBM and PFT reconstructions may under-estimate the true amplitude of forest cover change when
compared with REVEALS". Their own worked example: for the most recent window, PBMsc gives 45% and
REVEALS gives 41%. The skeptic picked the PBMsc figure without noting that the paper flags it as
the conservative one.

### What does not count against the skeptic

Its "the next clause supplies the open ground" argument is rhetoric, not evidence, and it is
faintly self-undermining, since if the cereals and livestock are what rescue the forest clause then
the forest clause was not carrying its own weight. But this is a small point and I do not rest
anything on it. Its reading of "scattered through" as "within a wooded region" rather than "in the
trees" is a fair reading of the English, and I accept it. That is why this stays a nitpick and does
not become a factual error.

## My own research

Five searches, plus direct retrieval and local text extraction of the Roberts et al. PDF so I could
read the disputed paragraph rather than a quotation of it. I took neither prior agent's sources on
trust and re-derived the Roberts quotations from the full text.

1. **Roberts et al. 2018, full text.** The skeptic's own key source, and it does not say what the
   skeptic needs it to say. Details above: the ~60% is Schlüter's eastern-Central-European
   footprint; the three-bucket sentence puts the coasts and southern Scandinavia in the early
   clearance group; Iron Age and Roman times are on the declining limb.
2. **Serge et al. 2022, the European REVEALS land-cover dataset paper (ESSD, open access).** This
   is the current handbook-grade gridded reconstruction and neither prior agent consulted it. It
   reports that "At 2700–3200 BP the United Kingdom, France, Germany and the Mediterranean region
   have grid cells recording OL values >70%", OL being open land. Grid cells in Germany at over 70
   percent open land three centuries *before* the Iron Age begins is not compatible with the
   skeptic's picture of a majority-wooded north German landscape whose woodland "was not
   substantially removed until the medieval clearances". It is compatible with a mosaic.
3. **Nielsen et al. 2012, the REVEALS openness reconstruction whose region actually is northern
   Germany and Denmark.** Its abstract places the large-scale opening "in the north-western part of
   the region", with "development of arable areas, grasslands and of heathlands". The skeptic cited
   this paper and read that qualifier as excluding the north German interior. Read against the
   essay's stated geography rather than against the skeptic's substituted geography, it says the
   opposite of what the skeptic used it for: the north-western part of northern Germany and Denmark
   is the Germanic North Sea zone.
4. **Northwest German landscape history (Behre and the heathland literature).** Heaths in Lower
   Saxony were established and grazed before the Bronze Age and expanded through the Iron Age as
   cutting, grazing and shifting cultivation degraded forest soils. The open ground in this region
   is old, and it is anthropogenic, which is exactly Phase 1's point stripped of its bad exhibit.
5. **A check on the post-Roman revival literature.** The 2020 *Scientific Reports* forest-revival
   paper is Brittany-focused and does not bear on Germania; I set it aside rather than press it into
   service, and note it here only so the next reader does not repeat the search.

What all of this converges on: the region was a mosaic of long-established woodland, long-cleared
arable, pasture, heath, and coastal marsh, varying sharply by sub-region, and the Roman Iron Age
was the most open moment temperate Europe had yet seen. Neither "the forests" alone nor "cleared
fields as much as forest" is the right summary. Both prior agents wrote a sentence that picked one
side of a mosaic.

## Final disposition, and what Phase 4 should do

**Restore the finding at grade nitpick.** Not a factual error: the essay does not assert anything
demonstrably false, and a reader who pictures wooded country containing farms has not been
seriously misled. Not none either: "the forests of northern Europe", standing alone, names the one
element of the landscape that Roman-period farmers had spent a thousand years reducing, and does so
in a paragraph that is otherwise reporting archaeology. The word carries a whiff of Tacitus's
*silvis horrida* into a sentence that is supposed to be reporting excavations.

Phase 4 should make one edit to each localization, adding a two-word pairing and changing nothing
else. This preserves everything the skeptic correctly defended, since the forest stays and stays
first, while removing the implication that the cleared ground was incidental. It does not assert
parity, does not promote the marsh, and does not add a list.

Both target lines are free of `` ` ``, `~`, `$`, `‡`, and `^`, so no markup balance is at risk, and
neither edit touches a heading boundary.
