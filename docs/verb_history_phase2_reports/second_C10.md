# C10 · final verdict: partly · final grade: nitpick

Essay line 140 (English) / line 106 (German):

> They committed little to writing, though they did develop the runic alphabet for short
> inscriptions and magical purposes.

> Sie hinterließen wenig Schriftliches, obwohl sie das Runenalphabet für kurze Inschriften und
> magische Zwecke entwickelt hatten.

Phase 1 filed two charges, chronology and magic, and graded the row needs-hedging. The Phase 2
skeptic refuted both and graded it none. My finding: the magic charge is dead and the skeptic
killed it correctly. The chronology charge survives, but only in the German, only at nitpick
strength, and not for the reason Phase 1 gave. Phase 1's replacement prose stays rejected.

## What the skeptic got right

**Phase 1's central move is genuinely wrong, and I verified it against the primary source.** Phase 1
treated the date of the oldest surviving inscription as the date the alphabet was developed, which
is how it got "about a century and a half later." Runology refuses that equation, and Düwel's
Runenkunde separates the two in consecutive sentences. I extracted the publisher's reading sample
myself with pdftotext rather than trusting the skeptic's transcription, and the sentences are there
verbatim: "Die Entstehung der Runenschrift dürfte spätestens im Laufe des 1. Jhs. n.Chr. erfolgt
sein. Die ältesten Denkmäler aus der Zeit um 200 n.Chr. …" On the standard German handbook's own
dating, runic writing existed within the century of Teutoburg. Phase 1's "century and a half" gap
is not a fact about the script, it is an artifact of conflating attestation with invention.

**Phase 1 misread Barnes, and the skeptic caught it.** Barnes's claim is that there is little
evidence the *runes themselves* were held to be imbued with magic power. That is a claim about
character-mysticism, not about whether runic inscriptions were used for magical ends. The essay
makes the second claim. Phase 1 quoted the sentence and drew the other conclusion from it.

**The handbooks do affirm the dual function.** I extracted Nedoma's Vienna introduction and confirm
the skeptic's key quotation word for word: "Soweit sich aus den Inschriften erkennen läßt, haben die
Runen sowohl der profanen zwischenmenschlichen Kommunikation als auch der Kommunikation mit der
'anderen' Welt (der übernatürlichen Sphäre) gedient." Düwel's parallel sentence is also verbatim as
quoted: a general characterization of older-futhark inscriptions that foregrounds either the magical
and sometimes cultic aspect or the profane content of the messages "läßt sich nur schwer
durchführen." The essay's two-item split is the split the handbooks make. Charge two is dead.

**Phase 1's replacement prose is bad**, on all three of the skeptic's counts. I checked the third
independently: "only about a century and a half later" has no antecedent inside its own paragraph,
whose nearest temporal anchor is the section-opening battle, so the sentence would read as a century
and a half after 9 AD, which is the very proposition the handbooks do not support. It swaps an
undated sentence for a dated and wrong one.

**Source quality genuinely improved.** Phase 1 rested on three Wikipedia articles and a book review.
The skeptic reached two handbooks and a 2025 Antiquity paper. That is the upgrade this pass is for.

## Where the skeptic is wrong, or is not

Four problems. The first two are overreach in the argument; the third and fourth are gaps.

**1. The Svingerud plank does not say what the skeptic says it says.** The skeptic cited the 2025
Antiquity radiocarbon range of 50 BC to AD 275 as evidence that runic writing may reach back to the
turn of the era, and leaned on it as one of three decisive answers. The range dates grave A4367,
which contained fragment Hole 2, not the stone and not the carving. The paper's own framing runs the
other way: "Among the earliest certain runic finds are inscriptions on portable objects from Denmark
such as a bone comb from Vimose, archaeologically dated to AD 160," and the Hole stone is offered as
"the earliest known archaeologically dated rune-stone," not as an early-first-century object. Nedoma,
reading the stone as a runologist, dates it "aus dem 2. oder 3. Jahrhundert n. Chr." The skeptic
converted the extreme lower bound of a context range into evidence about the script. Strike the
plank.

**2. Nedoma was quoted selectively, and his own chronology was not reported.** The skeptic quoted
Nedoma where he affirms the supernatural function and where he calls Meldorf first-century, and did
not report that Nedoma's section actually headed "Zeitlicher und räumlicher Rahmen" gives no
creation date at all. It says the origin is Mediterranean and undeterminable, then opens the
chronology at the Funen knife of about 150 and the Svingerud stone of the second or third century,
with Vimose and Øvre Stabu in the second half of the second century. Meldorf appears as an
unresolved aside: "Es bleibt unklar, ob es sich um vier Runen hiwi … oder um eine lateinische
Buchstaben-folge IDIṆ … handelt." The skeptic also omitted the passage where Nedoma says the
cultic-magical reading of the futhark belongs "vor allem in der älteren Forschung, aber auch in der
Esoterik neuerer Prägung" and is "indessen problematisch." That passage is about the rune *names*,
so it does not overturn the affirmative sentence, and I am not reviving Phase 1's charge on its
strength. But it means the skeptic's conclusion that "the handbook framing does not support even a
hedge" overstates its own sources. Nedoma flags the extent of the magical horizon as an old
Streitfrage "auch heutzutage noch kontrovers diskutiert." So: runes existing at 9 AD is Düwel's
outer bound, not a handbook consensus, and the Barnes review the skeptic itself quotes says "there
is little certainty about the place, time, or reason for the development of this system."

**3. The "the section's frame is loose" argument is backwards.** The skeptic's proof that the
Teutoburg section is not a 9 AD snapshot was that it glosses Wōðanaz with Old Norse Óðinn two
paragraphs later. But the essay writes "Wōðanaz (later Wōden (Old English) and Óðinn (Old Norse))".
It flags the forward reach with the word *later*. The one place the essay reaches forward is the one
place it tells the reader it is doing so, which is evidence the section is careful about temporal
reach, not evidence it floats free. Meanwhile the runic sentence sits in a paragraph of three flat
statements about that society at that time: no states or cities, no stone monuments, little writing.
The skeptic's charitable reading of the essay was charitable in the right direction but rested on
the wrong exhibit.

**4. The skeptic never looked at the German, and the German is where the finding lives.** The whole
of the skeptic's chronology defense is "the essay asserts no date at all," which is true of the
English concessive: "though they did develop" can be read as "though they did eventually develop."
The German ships a pluperfect inside a past-tense frame that the paragraph explicitly anchors at
"Zur Zeit der Schlacht im Teutoburger Wald": "obwohl sie das Runenalphabet … entwickelt hatten." In
German that reads as "although by then they had developed it." The atemporal escape hatch the
skeptic relied on does not exist in the shipped German. Both localizations are marked translated, so
German readers get the flat version of a claim no handbook affirms.

## My own research

Six web operations, plus local pdftotext extraction of two PDFs so I was reading the sources rather
than the skeptic's transcription of them. I confirmed both of the skeptic's load-bearing German
quotations are verbatim and in context, which is worth saying plainly: the skeptic did not
fabricate or shade them. What I found beyond that:

- Düwel puts the emergence of runic writing "spätestens im Laufe des 1. Jhs. n.Chr.", hedged with
  "dürfte", and the oldest monuments "aus der Zeit um 200 n.Chr." The two dates are separate claims
  and Phase 1 collapsed them.
- Nedoma's chronological section declines to date the invention and starts the record at about 150
  AD. Two standard handbooks, two different framings, and neither one affirms runes at 9 AD.
- The Antiquity 2025 radiocarbon range applies to a grave, not a carving, and the authors name the
  Vimose comb of AD 160 as among the earliest certain runic finds.
- General-survey framing agrees that most runologists favour a first-century CE date for when runes
  were devised while the earliest unambiguous inscriptions are Vimose and Øvre Stabu at about 160.

Net: the proposition "the Germanic peoples had the runic alphabet by 9 AD" is not false. It is
unprovable, it sits at the early edge of the range one handbook allows and outside the frame another
one draws, and no source asserts it. That is a hedging-shaped problem, not an error, and it only
reaches the reader in the German.

Incidental, outside this row: the same paragraph's "They built no stone monuments" is now in mild
tension with the Svingerud find, an inscribed standing sandstone slab in an Iron Age grave field.
Worth a glance if another row covers that sentence. Not my remit and not a C10 fix.

## Final disposition, and what Phase 4 should do

Verdict **partly**. Grade **nitpick**, down from Phase 1's needs-hedging and up from the skeptic's
none.

- **Magic half: kill confirmed.** Do not touch "for short inscriptions and magical purposes" in
  either language. The handbooks assert the dual function, Phase 1 misread Barnes to argue
  otherwise, and the only residue is a proportion quibble that misleads nobody.
- **Chronology half: survives, minimally.** The problem is not that the essay is wrong about when
  runes were invented. It is that the German pluperfect quietly places the alphabet inside the 9 AD
  frame, asserting flatly what the field declines to assert. Fix it with the essay's own narrative
  future, the "would" / "sollten" device it already uses eleven and three times respectively. That
  removes the temporal claim without inserting a competing one, which is exactly where Phase 1 went
  wrong.
- **Reject Phase 1's replacement prose.** It asserts a development date the handbooks contradict, it
  dangles "later" without an antecedent, and its "some argue" demotes what the handbooks affirm.

If Phase 4 is triaging for cost and only actioning findings above nitpick, the German-only edit
below is still a two-word change with no downside. If Phase 4 prefers a single-language edit, do the
German alone; the English is defensible as it stands.

## Replacement prose

**English, line 140** (optional, for parallelism with the German fix):

> They committed little to writing, though they would develop the runic alphabet for short
> inscriptions and magical purposes.

**German, line 106** (the change that matters):

> Sie hinterließen wenig Schriftliches, sollten aber das Runenalphabet für kurze Inschriften und
> magische Zwecke entwickeln.

Both sentences carry no markup, so there is no fatalError risk. "Would" and "sollten" are the
essay's established narrative future, already used for "the Romans would launch punitive
expeditions" and "den ~limes~ … errichten sollten." Neither version asserts a date, neither adds a
parenthetical, and length is unchanged.
