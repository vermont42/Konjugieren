# C13 · second opinion · final verdict: **upheld (kill confirmed)** · final grade: **none**

Essay line 142, English: "worshipped in sacred groves rather than temples."
German line 108: "die in heiligen Hainen statt in Tempeln verehrt wurden."

Phase 1 graded this **needs-hedging**. The Phase 2 skeptic returned **refuted / none**. I confirm
the kill. The skeptic reached the right answer, and on the decisive point it reached it by a sound
route. Two of its supporting arguments are weak enough that I would not want Phase 4 to rely on
them, and one of its quoted sources appears to be misquoted. Neither defect touches the outcome.

## What the skeptic got right

**The dateline is real, not a rescue.** The sentence is the fourth paragraph of a section that opens
"At the time of the battle at Teutoburg," and the three paragraphs before it make period-bound
claims in the same flat register: no centralized states or cities, no stone monuments, little
committed to writing. Reading the religion sentence as a timeless claim about Germanic paganism in
general would be reading it out of its paragraph. That is exactly the uncharitable-reading failure
mode I was told to watch for in skeptics, and the skeptic did not commit it here; the finding
arguably did.

**The finding's central evidence concedes itself away.** Uppåkra, Tissø, Ranheim and Ose are the
weight-bearing items, and Phase 1 writes that they "are third century and later, so they do not bear
directly on 9 AD and I am not claiming they do." A hedge is a claim that the essay's sentence
overstates for the moment it describes. Evidence stipulated not to bear on that moment cannot
establish it.

**The dating is not an argument from absence.** This is where I most expected the skeptic to be
vulnerable, and it is not. "No cult buildings before AD 200" could be mere silence in the record.
It is not: the specialist literature makes the *positive* claim that the dedicated cult building is
an innovation of about AD 200, and one recent monograph on Gudme argues it was inspired by Roman
temple practice met along the Rhine. A claim that the building type begins at a date entails its
absence before that date. My own searching, independent of the skeptic's, returned the same picture
from the Uppåkra literature: Gudme, Uppåkra and probably Sorte Muld are "among the earliest known
cult structures in the region," erected at roughly the same time.

**The replacement prose really would have introduced an error.** "Archaeology has since found roofed
cult houses," dropped undated into a paragraph governed by "At the time of the battle at Teutoburg,"
tells the reader roofed cult houses belong to the world of 9 AD. That is the one thing Phase 1
explicitly says it is not claiming. The proposed fix would have shipped the misreading the finding
was written to prevent.

## Where the skeptic is wrong, or is not

**The Lewis and Short argument is a bad argument for a right conclusion.** The skeptic writes that
"templum in classical Latin is first of all a marked-out consecrated space, not a roofed building,"
and supports this by noting that Lewis and Short give the augural sense first. Dictionary sense
order in L&S is etymological and historical, not frequency-ranked. In Tacitus's own ordinary usage
templum means a temple building far more often than it means an augural precinct, so "L&S lists it
first" proves nothing about what Tacitus meant in *Annals* 1.51. The conclusion nevertheless stands,
on two better grounds the skeptic also cites but leans on less: Romans did apply templum to
consecrated groves and other sacred places, and the same author's *Germania* 9 makes the precinct
reading the economical one here. This is the standard scholarly resolution of the tension, not an
apologetic invented for this review. Reasoning docked, verdict unaffected.

**The Oberdorla evidence is misreported, and it was the skeptic's own exemplar.** The skeptic says
Oberdorla "has no permanent roofed ritual structure at all" and puts in quotation marks that the
site "remains an open-air sanctuary 'without evidence of permanent roofed ritual structures.'" I
fetched the cited article and could not find that phrase; what it does contain is "evidence of huts
which may have sheltered the cult images." German site descriptions go further: in the third century
a separate sanctuary was built there "das Züge römischer Umgangstempel trägt," carrying features of
a Roman ambulatory temple, with Gallo-Roman influence. So Oberdorla is not the clean open-air
counter-exemplar the skeptic made of it. It is still evidence *for* the essay's sentence at 9 AD,
because the temple-like structure is third century, but a Phase 4 reader who checks that citation
will find it does not say what the report says it says.

**The LEIZA survey is off-target.** It covers sanctuaries in the Roman province of Upper Germania,
whose pre-Roman sanctuaries are largely Celtic and whose relevance to the free Germani east of the
Rhine is analogical. It corroborates; it does not settle. The Gudme and Uppåkra material does the
actual work.

**Did the skeptic kill a hedge finding because the underlying fact checks out?** I tested this
specifically, since it is the classic way a refutation goes wrong. The answer is no, because here
the hedge and the fact are the same question. The finding's thesis is that the exclusive "rather
than" is Tacitus's rhetorical opposition to Roman practice rather than an observed fact. If
independent archaeology of the right period and region supports the exclusion, the thesis fails on
its own terms, and it does. That the *Germania* 9 passage is also a Herodotean ethnographic topos,
which it is, and which I confirmed independently, does not by itself make its content false; a topos
can be reached for precisely because it happens to fit.

**The residual quibble the skeptic raised and dismissed is the better finding.** For exactly this
period and region, the richest cult evidence is wetland: Oberdorla is a bog and lake sanctuary,
and Thorsberg, Vimose, Illerup and the bog bodies are Iron Age deposits. "Groves" is a synecdoche
for open-air cult sites generally. I weighed widening the phrase and rejected it, for reasons under
disposition.

**One thing to discard.** A search summary I ran asserted that Tacitus "mentions a temple to Nerthus
(Germania 50)." The *Germania* has 46 chapters, the Nerthus passage is chapter 40, and its word is
*castum nemus*, a chaste grove. There is no second contemporaneous templum. Whatever Phase 4 does,
it should not repeat that.

## My own research

Eight operations, six productive: two failed (the Woolf chapter came back as unparsed PDF binary,
and a German Wikipedia URL 404'd).

1. Aniconism and the "no temples" motif in *Germania* 9. Confirmed as an ethnographic topos with a
   direct Herodotean model, Persians at *Histories* 1.131. This is real and Phase 1 was right about
   it, but it establishes the framing, not the falsity.
2. *Templum Tanfanae*. The tension with *Germania* 9 is genuine and noticed; the standard resolution
   is the one the skeptic gives, that a consecrated grove or other sacred place could be called
   templum. No excavated identification of the site exists.
3. Earliest Germanic cult buildings. Gudme about AD 200, Uppåkra and probably Sorte Muld at roughly
   the same time, Uppåkra's building rebuilt seven times across some seven hundred years. Earliest
   securely attested roofed Germanic cult structure postdates Teutoburg by roughly two centuries.
4. Germania Magna sanctuaries of the Roman Iron Age. Oberdorla flourishes after AD 58, some
   sixty idols across the first three centuries AD, some standing in circular open enclosures; the
   temple-like roofed structure is third century and shows Gallo-Roman influence.
5. and 6. Direct fetches of the Oberdorla descriptions, English and German, reported above.

The convergence matters more than any single item: three independent lines, Scandinavian cult-house
chronology, Thuringian sanctuary chronology, and the specialist reading of the cult building as a
Roman-contact innovation, all put roofed Germanic cult architecture after the essay's stated moment.

## Final disposition, and what Phase 4 should do

**Confirm the kill. Grade none. Change nothing at line 142, and nothing at German line 108.**

The sentence says that at the time of Teutoburg the Germanic gods were worshipped in sacred groves
rather than temples. That is what the source says, what the archaeology of that period shows, and
what the paragraph's own dateline scopes it to. The hedge Phase 1 wanted would attach a
historiographical caveat to one clause of a paragraph that states cattle, kinship, runes and stone
monuments flatly, and the specific caveat proposed would have imported an anachronism.

I considered and rejected a smaller change, and record it so Phase 4 does not re-open it: widening
"sacred groves" to "sacred groves and other open-air places" to cover the bog and lake sanctuaries
that dominate the period's evidence. It is defensible on accuracy and I still say no. It lengthens a
sentence already trailing a four-name parenthesis, the following sentence about sacrifice already
carries the reader to the practices in question, and no reader is misled by the narrower word, which
is the word the ancient source uses. Accuracy gain near zero, prose cost real.

The Óðinn and Þórr forms in the parenthesis are glossed in place as later reflexes, "later Wōden
(Old English) and Óðinn (Old Norse)." They name the gods across time; they do not invite the reader
to carry first-century cult practice into the Viking Age.
