# C6 · final verdict: refuted · grade: none (kill confirmed, with one rider Phase 4 should carry)

Essay line 138: "Society was organized around kinship groups and tribal affiliations, led by chieftains
whose authority derived from military prowess, generosity, and noble lineage. The warrior aristocracy
formed a ~comitatus~, a band of followers bound to their lord by oaths of loyalty."

I confirm the skeptic's kill. The essay's sentence contains no factual error, and Phase 1's proposed
replacement would introduce one. But the skeptic reached the right verdict partly by an argument that
is demonstrably false about this essay, and it punted a real defect sitting on the German line it was
reading. Both are recorded below so that Phase 4 does not inherit the bad reasoning along with the
good conclusion.

## What the skeptic got right

**The substantive charge, and it is decisive.** Phase 1 wrote that "Germania 14 grounds the bond in
shame, 'infame in omnem vitam ac probrosum superstitem principi suo ex acie recessisse', not in a
sworn oath." I pulled the Latin independently, from Perseus (text 2007.01.0090), not from the Latin
Library that both prior agents used. The clause Phase 1 quotes and the clause about the oath are
punctuated as a single sentence:

> iam vero infame in omnem vitam ac probrosum superstitem principi suo ex acie recessisse: illum
> defendere, tueri, sua quoque fortia facta gloriae eius adsignare praecipuum sacramentum est

Phase 1 quoted the first colon-clause and asserted that the second does not exist. It does, and its
head noun is *sacramentum*, the Roman word for the military oath of allegiance. The essay's "oaths of
loyalty" is not an invention imposed on Tacitus. It is the one word in the passage that names the
bond. Phase 1's "What is actually true" section states the opposite of what its own cited chapter
says, which is the strongest possible ground for a kill.

**The corroboration is not just translators.** I checked the German-language scholarly summary
tradition, which neither prior agent touched and which matters here because the shipped German
localization is what German readers get. The standard German framing of the Tacitean Gefolgschaft is
"eine freiwillige, durch Treueid gefestigte Vereinigung erprobter Männer und wehrfähiger Jünglinge um
einen charismatischen oder berühmten Führer", a voluntary association consolidated by an oath of
loyalty. *Treueid* is precisely the word the shipped German uses on line 104. So the German
localization is not shipping a flat version of a hedged English claim. It is shipping the standard
German handbook phrasing verbatim. That closes the one asymmetry this project worries about most.

**Phase 1's replacement prose is worse than what it replaces, and the skeptic's four objections all
land.** I would add a fifth. "bound to their lord by loyalty and by the disgrace of outliving him"
takes the sanction for breaking the bond and installs it as the bond itself, so the sentence now
claims the followers were held together by a shame that only exists once the lord is already dead.
That is not a stylistic quibble. It is a worse account of the institution than the sentence it was
meant to correct.

**The Gefolgschaft answer holds.** I pushed on the strongest version of the skepticism I could reach:
that "many scholars today consider the Roman era report of these warbands as more of a literary
trope", with Morillo cited for the comitatus as "a literary ideal rather than a historical reality".
That is real, but it is aimed at the heroic comitatus ideal and at the continuity thesis running from
Tacitus to medieval lordship, and the scholars carrying it work mostly on Anglo-Saxon England. It does
not reach a sentence that says first-century Germanic war-bands existed and were bound to a lord.
Phase 1 conceded in its own text that "warrior retinues themselves are not in doubt" and that the
graded weapon deposits corroborate them. A finding cannot demand a hedge for a fact it concedes.
Note also that Phase 1 graded this nitpick, not needs-hedging. Phase 1 did not itself think a hedge
was required, so this is not a case of a hedge finding being killed because the underlying fact
checks out. The hedge was never the finding's spine.

## Where the skeptic is wrong, or is not

**The `~…~` argument is false as stated, and it was the skeptic's charge-one rebuttal.** The skeptic
wrote that "the essay's ~…~ span demonstrably marks outsiders' terms (~Germani~, ~limes~, ~kurgans~),
not self-designations", and built a whole paragraph on "Every member of the set Phase 1 invokes as the
comparison class is an outsider's term." I extracted all 59 emphasis spans in the essay body. The
convention is nothing of the kind. It marks grammatical terms of art (~Aspect~, ~Optative~,
~e-grade~, ~preterite-presents~), emphasized clauses (~collapse of the aspect system~, ~Verb classes
proliferated based on the structure of the present stem~), and cited word-forms in the languages under
discussion, most of which are native self-designations: ~Pfund~, ~Wasser~, ~machen~, ~haben~, ~sein~,
~werden~, ~würde~, ~können~, ~müssen~, and the English ~pound~, ~water~, ~make~. The skeptic sampled
the three examples Phase 1 happened to name and generalized from a set of three to a set of 59.

This does not rescue the finding, and I want to be exact about why. The correct reading is that the
span is provenance-neutral. It says "this is a cited or technical term" and asserts nothing about who
coined it. Phase 1's inference, that a reader "will take it for what the Germanic warriors called
their own institution", is therefore unsupported, and so is the skeptic's counter-inference. "The
warrior aristocracy formed a ~comitatus~" reads as "formed what is called a comitatus", which is true.
Right conclusion, wrong argument. Phase 4 should not repeat the skeptic's version of it anywhere.

**The underlying fact behind charge one is better than either agent said, and is worth knowing.**
There is a reconstructed Germanic self-designation for this institution: *druhtiz, surviving as Old
English *dryht* and *druht*. So Phase 1 was right that *comitatus* is the outsider's word for a thing
whose insiders had their own, and the skeptic's "the standard name for the institution" is a fact
about modern cataloguing rather than an answer. It still does not make the essay wrong, because the
essay never claims otherwise. But if Josh ever wants a sentence here, *druhtiz is the etymological
thread, not an attribution to Tacitus, and it connects to the Old English material the essay already
cares about.

**The skeptic punted a real defect it had already found.** Its closing observation, that the German
line 104 reads "eine ~comitatus~" with feminine agreement on a Latin fourth-declension masculine, was
correct and was then dismissed as "a translation-quality question for whoever owns the German pass".
There is no German pass after this one. I am the last voice on line 104. Verified: German takes this
loan as masculine, "der lateinische Begriff comitatus", consistent with every German Latin loan in
*-us* (der Modus, der Kasus, der Status). *Bilden* takes the accusative, so the correct article is
*einen*. This is a grammar error in text shipping to German readers in the "translated" state. It is
not the C6 claim and it does not revive C6, but letting it die on a jurisdictional technicality would
be the wrong outcome of a process whose whole point is that this line got looked at three times.

**Charge three is correctly dismissed.** Germania 14.2 has "plerique nobilium adulescentium", most of
the noble youths, seeking out warring nations, and 13 confers a *princeps*'s standing on the very
young by "insignis nobilitas aut magna patrum merita". Coupling the retinue to the warrior elite is
what the source does. The essay's preceding clause already grounds authority in prowess, generosity,
and lineage rather than in closed heredity. Nothing to fix.

## My own research

Nine calls, deliberately routed around both prior agents' sources.

1. Latin text of Germania 14 from Perseus rather than The Latin Library, to check whether the two
   clauses really sit in one sentence and whether *praecipuum sacramentum est* is textually secure. It
   does and it is. This alone settles charge two against Phase 1.
2. German-language scholarship on the Gefolgschaft, which neither agent consulted, to test the
   shipped German rather than the shipped English. It returned *Treueid*, the exact word on line 104.
3. The strongest available form of the comitatus skepticism, including Morillo's "literary ideal
   rather than a historical reality", to see whether the skeptic had understated it. It had understated
   it slightly, but the skepticism targets the heroic ideal and the continuity thesis, and Phase 1
   conceded the war-bands.
4. The Germanic term *druhtiz, which reframes charge one as an etymological opportunity rather than
   an error.
5. Direct extraction of all 59 `~…~` spans from the essay, which is the check that falsified the
   skeptic's central rhetorical move and which neither prior agent ran.
6. Rives's Clarendon commentary remains unreachable in full text, as the skeptic reported. It does
   not matter. Phase 1 needed the oath clause to be absent, and it is present in every text of the
   chapter.

## Final disposition, and what Phase 4 should do

**C6 is refuted, grade none.** Confirming the skeptic's kill, not landing somewhere new on the
English. Phase 4 should make no change to `docs/verb_history.txt` line 138. It is accurate, it tracks
its source on the one point that was actually contested, and both of the specific rewrites offered to
it would make it worse.

**Two things Phase 4 should carry forward anyway.**

1. Fix the German article on `docs/verb_history_de.txt` line 104. One word, markup untouched, balanced,
   no crash risk. This is a grammar defect, independent of C6's merits, found by the skeptic and then
   dropped.
2. Do not reuse the skeptic's "the ~…~ span marks outsiders' terms" claim in any other verdict. If it
   appears in a sibling finding about ~Germani~, ~limes~, ~kurgans~, or ~ablaut~, that finding's
   reasoning needs rechecking against the 59-span inventory above. The span is provenance-neutral.

## Replacement prose

**English: none.** Line 138 stands unchanged. Neither prior agent's rewrite should be applied.

**German, line 104, article only:**

> Die Kriegeraristokratie bildete einen ~comitatus~, eine Schar von Gefolgsleuten, die durch Treueide an ihren Herrn gebunden waren.

The only change is *eine* to *einen*. The rest of the line, including *Treueide*, is correct and
matches the standard German scholarly phrasing for this institution.
