# C9 · final verdict: upheld · grade: factual-error · replacement prose rewritten

Essay line 140: "They built no stone monuments." German counterpart, `docs/verb_history_de.txt`
line 106: "Sie errichteten keine Steinmonumente."

The finding stands, and the grade stands. The skeptic's verdict survives, but two of its three
supports are weaker than it presented them, and the one thing it certified without testing, Phase 1's
replacement sentence, is the part that actually needed work. I am replacing that sentence.

## What the skeptic got right

Three things, and they are the load-bearing ones.

It refused the easy kill. The obvious move here is to say that line 140 is a compression of Tacitus,
*Germania* 16 and 27, sitting in a paragraph assembled entirely out of *Germania* material, and that
"stone monuments" therefore silently means "monumental masonry in the Roman manner." The skeptic
raised that defense against itself in full strength and then answered it rather than quietly
declining to raise it. That is the right shape for a Phase 2 report.

It read the paragraph in context rather than lifting the sentence out, which is the first failure
mode on the list, and it got the context right. Line 130 defines the subject population as
Proto-Germanic speakers "in southern Scandinavia and along the North Sea and Baltic coasts," and the
Lifeways paragraph at 136 describes those people. The sentence at 140 cannot be quietly narrowed to
the Cherusci without contradicting the essay ten lines earlier.

It graded honestly against its own side. It docked Phase 1 for resting a high-confidence
factual-error on two Wikipedia articles and an academia.edu paper, and it discarded Phase 1's
runestone and picture-stone counterexamples as four centuries too late to bear on a claim framed at
AD 9. Both corrections are right, and both weaken the finding's original presentation while leaving
the finding itself intact. That is the distinction the brief asks about, kept correctly: a weak
source and a wrong claim are different things.

## Where the skeptic is wrong, or is not

**Its decisive evidence is not quite decisive, and it is not peer-reviewed.** The skeptic staked the
verdict on the Wielbark stone circles, calling them "Germanic, continental, and contemporary with
Teutoburg," and said "there is no reading of the essay's sentence narrow enough to survive that."
Two problems. First, chronology. The Wielbark stone constructions appear "during the second half of
the 1st century" on the skeptic's own cited source; the Grzybnica necropolis is founded around AD 80
to 120 with its heyday ending around 160 to 200; Odry is dated to the 1st and 2nd centuries. Teutoburg
is AD 9. These circles begin two to four generations after the battle the paragraph anchors itself
to. That is close, and for a paragraph this loosely framed it is close enough to matter, but it is
not the airtight contemporaneity the skeptic claimed. Second, sources. The skeptic's two Wielbark
citations are a university thesaurus page and the UNESCO astronomy-heritage portal. Neither is
peer-reviewed. I went looking for a handbook and did not find one on the open web; the fullest
dedicated study I could reach, Pachulska 2021, is a licenciate thesis. So the skeptic scolded Phase 1
for tertiary sourcing and then rested its own kill-shot on comparable ground. The verdict is not
affected, because the underlying fact is not contested anywhere I looked, but the certainty was
oversold.

**The finding does not need the Wielbark material at all, and the skeptic misidentified which leg it
stands on.** The clean support is the essay's own geography plus the Scandinavian stone circles,
which run roughly 500 BC to AD 400 and so straddle AD 9 directly rather than trailing it. Line 130
sites the homeland in southern Scandinavia. Line 140's subject is "The Germanic peoples," the
essay's widest term, the same one it uses at 135 to reach forward to German, English, Dutch and the
Scandinavian languages. A sentence that broad, about a homeland the essay itself has just placed in
southern Scandinavia, cannot deny in its own voice the single most conspicuous class of thing those
people left standing above ground. Wielbark is a bonus, not the hinge.

**The one soft spot: it certified Phase 1's replacement without testing its positive clause.** Phase
1 proposed "They built nothing in dressed stone, though standing stones and stone settings marked
their graves." The skeptic called it "sound," offered a vocabulary preference, and moved on. But that
sentence's second half is a universal positive about "the Germanic peoples," and it is not true of
the population the paragraph actually foregrounds. In the Rhine-Weser and Elbe interior where
Teutoburg sits, the Pre-Roman and Roman Iron Age rite is cremation with the remains urned in flat
cemeteries, grave goods scarce, stone constructions present but largely in or over the grave pit
rather than raised as a marker; and where Iron Age burials in northern Germany do sit under something
visible, the recurring pattern in the recent literature is insertion into Neolithic and Bronze Age
mounds that were already ancient, that is, appropriation rather than construction. So Phase 1's fix
swaps a false universal negative for a shaky universal positive, and a German reader of line 106 in
Lower Saxony would be told that standing stones marked graves in a landscape where they did not. The
repair is cheap: name the region, which the essay has already named at line 130.

**Failure modes checked and cleared.** No argument from absence is doing any work here; there is
positive evidence on both sides. No hedge is being confused with a fact: nobody in this chain killed
or upheld anything on hedging grounds, and I confirm below that hedging is not the right grade.

## My own research

Five searches plus four fetches, deliberately aimed away from both prior agents' sources.

On the Wielbark question I went for peer review and mostly failed to get it, which is itself the
finding. The *Antiquity* article on the eponymous Wielbark site, which is genuinely peer-reviewed,
confirms the culture's span, "between the beginning of the Roman period (first century AD) and the
migration period (fifth century AD)," and confirms the Gothic association while flagging that the
research team wants to "carefully modify or redefine" it. It says nothing about stone circles. The
dedicated study I reached, Pachulska's 2021 Jagiellonian thesis, is squarely on the subject and
concludes the Pomeranian circles reflect "a certain wider socio-political and religious
organism...which would be the Gothic community." Independent site literature gives Grzybnica at
c. AD 80 to 200 and Odry as the largest Wielbark concentration in Pomerania with at least 602 burials.
Convergent, uncontested, but not handbook-grade on the open web. Verdict on the fact: true. Verdict
on the skeptic's confidence in it: half a notch too high.

On northern Germany I looked specifically for what the skeptic had not: whether the interior, the
essay's foreground, supports the *positive* half of the proposed replacement. It does not, cleanly.
The Pre-Roman and Roman Iron Age record there is urnfield cremation, "cemeteries of up to several
hundred urns," grave goods "generally relatively scarce," graves that "partly feature stone
constructions" and sometimes enclosing ditches, with the rite continuous from Pre-Roman into Roman
Iron Age. Separately there is an active line of research on Iron Age reuse of much older mounds in
northern Germany. Neither of those is "standing stones marked their graves." This is the check that
changed my output.

On Scandinavia I confirmed the chronology that does the real work: Iron Age stone circles c. 500 BC to
c. 400 AD, a burial custom of southern Scandinavia, concentrated on Gotland and in Götaland, mostly
Pre-Roman and Roman Iron Age, excavation showing charcoal at the centre and the circles now read as
cremation graves. This is the same Wikipedia-level source Phase 1 used and I could not better it
quickly, but unlike the Wielbark point it is not disputed by anyone and it is the leg the finding
should have been standing on from the start.

## Final disposition, and what Phase 4 should do

**Upheld, at factual-error, with the replacement prose rewritten.**

Why the finding survives the register defense, stated for a reader who has seen neither prior report:
the sentence sits in a run of three, "no centralized states or cities," "no stone monuments," "committed
little to writing." The first and third are true on any reading. The second is not, and that
asymmetry is the whole case. "Stone monument" is not loose popular shorthand that only a pedant would
take literally; it is the term archaeologists apply to exactly the objects at issue, and those objects
are the ordinary grave form across the region and centuries this paragraph is about. A reader who
walks into a boulder ring in Skåne or Kashubia has walked into something the essay says was never
built.

Why factual-error and not needs-hedging: no adverb fixes it. "They built few stone monuments" or
"They built almost no stone monuments" would still be wrong about southern Scandinavia. The repair
has to change what the sentence asserts, which is the line between the two grades.

Phase 4 should replace line 140's second sentence with the prose below, and relocalize line 106 of
`docs/verb_history_de.txt` to match. It should not use Phase 1's sentence or the skeptic's variant of
it; both assert grave-marking stones for the whole Germanic world, which is not true of the interior.
Nothing else in the paragraph moves. Line 142's "worshipped in sacred groves rather than temples" sits
comfortably beside the new sentence and still carries the no-monumental-architecture point.

## Replacement prose

English, line 140, replacing "They built no stone monuments.":

> They built nothing in cut stone, though standing stones and stone circles marked their graves in Scandinavia and along the Baltic.

It keeps the contrast with Rome that the original sentence existed to make, states the true version of
the negative, and confines the positive to the region where it is true, which is the region the essay
itself named at line 130. No backtick, tilde, dollar sign, double dagger or caret, so no delimiter can
unbalance. No asterisk. No em dash, no parenthetical.

"Cut stone" over Phase 1's "dressed stone" because it is the plainer word for a general reader and
carries the same content. "Stone circles" over Phase 1's "stone settings" for the same reason; the
skeptic reached this preference too and was right about it.

German, `docs/verb_history_de.txt` line 106, replacing "Sie errichteten keine Steinmonumente." Phase 4
owns the translation; offered as a starting point:

> Sie bauten nichts aus behauenem Stein, doch in Skandinavien und entlang der Ostsee kennzeichneten Bautasteine und Steinkreise ihre Gräber.

What the German must carry, whatever wording Phase 4 lands on: the concessive shape, a term for worked
or quarried building stone rather than for stone in general, the geographic restriction, and *errichten*
moved out of the negated clause. Negating *errichten*, the exact verb for raising a bautasten, is what
makes the current German read worse than the current English.
