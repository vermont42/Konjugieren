# D17 · final verdict: partly (skeptic's kill does not fully hold) · final grade: nitpick

The finding is restored on one of its two legs, with new replacement prose. The skeptic was
right about the other leg and right to reject Phase 1's fix.

## What the skeptic got right

**Leg two of the finding is dead, and the skeptic killed it cleanly.** Phase 1 objected that
"crew is not simply gone," so "later became" overstates an incomplete replacement. But the essay
never asserts that *crew* is extinct. It says the conjugation "later became 'crowed'", which is a
statement about the standard outcome, and the standard outcome is exactly that. A reader who takes
away "modern English says crowed" has taken away something true. Nothing in the paragraph is
damaged by the residual survival of *crew*. This is the classic argument-to-an-unmade-claim, and
the skeptic identified it correctly.

**The rejection of Phase 1's replacement prose is also correct, and I confirm it independently.**
Phase 1 wanted "the old strong preterite survives only in literary use." My own checks say that is
a narrower claim than the record supports. Merriam-Webster's label is *chiefly British*, which is
regional, not literary. Dictionary.com lists *crew* among the verb's inflected past forms with no
label at all. So "only in literary use" would trade an unfalsifiable flourish for a falsifiable
and slightly wrong lexicographic assertion, which is the wrong direction of trade in a fact-check.
The skeptic's point 3, that the Phase 1 sentence reads as a correction of the King James rather
than a continuation of the argument, is a good observation about the paragraph's rhetoric.

**The skeptic also disclosed its own weak point honestly**, which is more than the argument
required of it. That disclosure is what decides this review, below.

## Where the skeptic is wrong, or is not

**Its load-bearing argument against leg one is itself a substitution.** The skeptic writes: "the
essay does not claim that speakers recognize *crew* as a preterite. It claims they know the modern
conjugation is *crowed*, which every competent speaker of Modern English does know." That is not a
reading of the sentence. The sentence is "Speakers of Modern English are aware that the conjugation
later became 'crowed'." The words *later became* are the predicate of the awareness clause, and the
skeptic's paraphrase deletes them. What the sentence ascribes to speakers is knowledge of a change,
not knowledge of a current form. So the skeptic accused Phase 1 of swapping in a different
proposition while swapping in a different proposition of its own, and that swap is the whole of its
first argument.

**Its fallback is a good argument that stops one step short.** The fallback is that the clause is a
register marker of the "as we all know" kind, carrying no informational payload, so no reader
finishes the sentence holding a false belief. For the English text I accept that. But the skeptic
then reasoned from harmlessness to *none*, and harmlessness is the bar for **factual-error**, not
the bar for **nitpick**. A nitpick is precisely a sentence that says something that is not so while
misleading nobody about the essay's subject. The skeptic conceded in its own words that the
proposition is "probably false of the population it names," identified the exact minimal fix,
deleting the appeal to speakers, and then declined to recommend it as "a copy-edit, not a
fact-check finding." A conceded false ascription about a real population, whose repair is deleting
seven words that carry no payload, is not a copy-edit. It is the smallest thing this pass exists to
catch.

**And the register-marker defense does not survive translation, which the skeptic looked at and
misread.** The skeptic quoted the German line only to confirm the two localizations match, and
concluded "no localization asymmetry exists and no German edit is required." Matching text does not
mean matching effect. The English clause works, to whatever extent it works, because it addresses
the reader as a fellow speaker of Modern English: an inclusive nudge, not a report. The German line,
"Sprecher des modernen Englisch wissen, dass die Konjugation später zu 'crowed' wurde," addresses a
reader who is by construction *not* a member of the group it names. For that reader it is a flat
third-person report about a foreign speech community, of the sort a reader takes at face value, and
there is no inclusive reading available to defuse it. Both localizations ship as "translated," so
the one sentence in the essay whose defense depends on the reader being an English speaker ships
verbatim to readers who are not. The asymmetry is not in the text. It is in the audience, and it
runs against the skeptic's conclusion rather than for it.

**One smaller overreach.** The skeptic wrote that "two major dictionaries have dropped the form the
finding wants protected," presented as though it refuted the finding's factual claim. It does not.
Phase 1's claim was that dictionaries still list *crew* as a British past tense, and that claim is
true: Merriam-Webster does, Collins does, Dictionary.com does. That some other dictionaries omit it
makes Phase 1's statement incomplete, not false. This does not change the verdict on leg two, since
the essay makes no claim either way, but it is the "the source is weak, therefore the finding is
wrong" move, and those are different claims.

## My own research

I did not take either agent's lexicography on trust, and I looked for a handbook rather than a
dictionary where I could.

On the history, all three of us agree and the agreement is well founded: *crow* is from Old English
*crāwan*, a class VII strong verb with preterite *crēow*, and it is a standard textbook instance of
the drift of English strong verbs into the weak class. Krygier's monograph on the disintegration of
the English strong verb system is the study of exactly this drift, and class VII is where it bit
hardest. The essay's historical direction is correct and was never in dispute.

On the lexicography, the picture is more mixed than either prior report allowed. Dictionary.com,
which follows the Random House Unabridged, prints *crew* among the verb's inflected past forms with
no archaic, regional, or literary label. Merriam-Webster prints it labelled *chiefly British*, and
only for the cock-crowing sense. Collins likewise carries it. Etymonline gives only "Related:
Crowed; crowing," and Oxford Advanced Learner's, as the skeptic found, omits *crew* entirely. So
neither "dictionaries still list it" nor "major dictionaries have dropped it" is the whole truth.
Both are true of different dictionaries. What every source agrees on is that *crowed* is the
standard modern past in both British and American English, which is all the essay's sentence needs
and all it delivers.

That settles leg two against Phase 1 on the merits, not merely on the technicality that the essay
did not claim extinction. It leaves leg one untouched, because leg one was never a lexicographic
question. No dictionary can tell you what speakers of Modern English are aware of.

I also checked whether "later" is safe relative to 1611, since weak *crowed* coexisted with *crew*
well before the King James. The Middle English Dictionary is behind a 403 and I could not date the
first weak attestation directly. It does not matter: "later became" describes when *crowed* became
the standard, not when it was first written, and no source disputes that the King James form was
the older one. I raise it only to record that I looked and found nothing that would upgrade this
finding.

## Final disposition, and what Phase 4 should do

**Restore the finding, at grade nitpick, on leg one only, with prose neither prior agent wrote.**

The essay tells readers that speakers of Modern English are aware of a change in this verb's
conjugation. Most are not, as the skeptic itself conceded. The clause does no work: strip it and
the reader keeps the fact the paragraph wants them to keep. So the repair costs nothing and removes
an assertion that is not true. The paragraph's argument, the King James example, and the direction
of the change all stand exactly as they are.

Phase 4 should replace the final sentence of the paragraph in both localizations. Do **not** apply
Phase 1's version: "survives only in literary use" is a new checkable claim that the dictionaries do
not support. My version adds no new claim at all; it uses the strong/weak vocabulary the paragraph
has already introduced, which strengthens the illustration rather than qualifying it.

Markup notes for the edit: there is no `$…$`, `~…~`, or `‡…‡` in this sentence, so nothing can go
unbalanced. In both files the sentence's final period is immediately followed by a backtick
subheading with no whitespace between them, and that must stay true after the swap or the rendered
view gains a blank line.

## Replacement prose

English, `docs/verb_history.txt` line 155. Replace:

> Speakers of Modern English are aware that the conjugation later became "crowed".

with:

> The strong preterite later gave way to the weak "crowed".

German, `docs/verb_history_de.txt` line 121. Replace:
