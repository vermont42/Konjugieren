# E12 · final verdict: partly · grade: needs-hedging

The skeptic's kill does not survive. The finding is restored, but not in the shape Phase 1 gave
it, and not for Phase 1's reasons. Phase 1 was right by accident, on bad evidence; the skeptic
demolished the bad evidence and then mistook a live specialist disagreement for settled consensus.

Essay line 157 (English), `docs/verb_history_de.txt` line 123 (German).

> Today, German strong verbs must largely be memorized individually, their ablaut patterns, while
> still systematic, are no longer predictable from the infinitive.

## What the skeptic got right

A great deal, and it should be said plainly, because most of Phase 1's case really was rotten.

1. **The Mailhammer quotation is real and verbatim.** I verified the *Morphology* 17 (2007)
   abstract independently. It says what the skeptic says it says, including "predictability is no
   longer based on the citation form stored in the mental lexicon" and the "remnants of the old
   system" clause. The skeptic did not massage it.
2. **Phase 1's central factual claim is false.** Phase 1 wrote that "the ie verbs take o just as
   reliably (biegen, fliegen, ziehen)." *liegen* gives *lag* / *gelegen*, and it is among the most
   frequent verbs in the language. The skeptic caught this. A finding that rests on a false premise
   deserves to be attacked.
3. **Phase 1's own example refutes Phase 1.** Phase 1 offered *leiden* / *litt* against *meiden* /
   *mied* as a "genuinely unpredictable pocket." Those two infinitives are segmentally identical
   from the stressed vowel onward, so the pair is evidence that the infinitive does not determine
   the pattern. It argues for the essay's sentence. The skeptic was right to say so, and I can add
   a second pair it missed: *scheiden* / *schied* against *schneiden* / *schnitt*, likewise
   identical from the diphthong onward. The skeptic's case against the K₁eiK₂en rule was available
   in a stronger form than it used.
4. **Phase 1's grading rationale is incoherent.** Phase 1 graded the finding a nitpick because "the
   two halves argue with each other." They do not. Systematicity and predictability are distinct
   properties, and a system can be organized into recurring series while requiring each lexeme's
   class to be stored. The skeptic's analysis here is correct and is the best paragraph in either
   prior report.
5. **Phase 1's cited source does not support Phase 1's claim.** Wikipedia's "coherence of the strong
   verb system is still present in modern German" is about coherence, which the essay already
   concedes.

Had I only these two reports to go on, I would have confirmed the kill.

## Where the skeptic is wrong, or is not

**The load-bearing error: the skeptic treated one side of a live scholarly disagreement as "the
standard scholarly summary."**

The skeptic's verdict rests on a single proposition: that the essay's sentence is a near-paraphrase
of settled specialist consensus, so nothing needs hedging. It quoted one source for that, ran four
searches, and never found the opposing literature.

It exists, it is peer-reviewed, it is by a career morphologist at the IDS Mannheim, and it is
addressed to precisely the synchronic question the essay's "Today, ..." sentence asks. Bernd Wiese,
"Form and function of verbal ablaut in contemporary standard German" (2008), concludes that **"for
the majority of strong verbs, membership in these classes (and thus ablaut) is predictable,"** with
the classes "defined in strictly synchronic terms" on a classification of stem forms by vowel
quality, complexity, and quantity.

That is the negation of the essay's second clause, scoped exactly as the essay scopes it, to strong
verbs. So Mailhammer 2007 and Wiese 2008, published a year apart, disagree. The essay picks one
side and states it flatly, in both localizations. That is what "needs hedging" means.

Two further defects follow from this one.

**The skeptic dismissed a tertiary source without checking whether a primary source said the same
thing.** It nominated German Wikipedia's K₁eiK₂en observation as its own weakest point, then
discounted it as tertiary and as describing "learned sub-regularities." But that Wikipedia passage
is a folk echo of Wiese's position, and Wiese is not tertiary and not describing folk intuition.
This is the failure mode of conflating "the source is weak" with "the claim is wrong." The skeptic
answered the source it found instead of the claim that source was gesturing at.

**The skeptic used its own key distinction only when the distinction helped it.** Its strongest
move against Phase 1 was that systematicity and predictability are different properties. Its
closing defense of the sentence was that "the neighboring 'while still systematic' is there to
prevent" a reader from hearing "random," so "no reader reaching the end of it is misinformed." That
defense requires "still systematic" to soften the predictability claim. If the two properties are
genuinely distinct, as the skeptic insisted when it suited the argument, then the in-sentence hedge
cannot reach the contested clause at all. The essay hedges systematicity and leaves predictability
bare, and predictability is the disputed one. The skeptic's best analytical insight defeats its own
conclusion.

**One scoping complaint, smaller.** The skeptic's "fact 1" is that strength itself is unpredictable
(*kaufen* / *laufen* / *saufen*, *reisen* / *reiten*), and it called this the thing any
infinitive-keyed rule "has to survive first." That is true and important, but the essay's sentence
takes "German strong verbs" as its subject, so it presupposes strength and fact 1 is not strictly
on point against a rule scoped the same way. The skeptic's fact 2, the resident ambiguities, is on
point and does the real work. I note this not to overturn anything but because fact 1 turns out to
belong in the replacement prose rather than in the argument, for reasons below.

**Where the skeptic was right and I am not overturning it:** the essay's sentence is *not false*,
Mailhammer genuinely supports it, and the diachronic frame of the paragraph, whose previous sentence
is "The original phonological conditioning that determined class membership became opaque," makes
the charitable reading the natural one. This is not a factual error. It is an unhedged statement of
a contested claim.

## My own research

Six searches, four fetches, taking neither prior agent's sources on trust.

- **Verified the skeptic's source rather than assuming it.** The Mailhammer *Morphology* abstract
  came back verbatim as quoted. The skeptic reported reaching it through a Springer cookie
  redirect; the text is genuine.
- **Chased a promising counter-source and killed it myself.** A search surfaced nonce-verb results
  reporting that "strong inflection makes up 35% of the elicited nonce forms" with acceptability at
  4.2 on a 7-point scale. Nonce-verb data would be the ideal test here, since it removes exactly
  the presupposition the skeptic leaned on, namely that the speaker already knows the verb is
  strong. On following it up, the study is **Frisian, not German**. It must not be cited in this
  finding. Recording it so that no later pass rediscovers the snippet and misattributes it.
- **Checked the Journal of Linguistics hit** the search engine kept surfacing as if it were modern
  experimental work. It is Barbour 1982, and the accessible text does not bear on predictability
  from the infinitive. Also not usable.
- **Found the decisive source neither prior agent reached**, Wiese 2008, and confirmed it from the
  IDS institutional repository rather than from a search snippet, because the first search summary
  visibly blended Wiese's abstract with Mailhammer's and I did not trust it.

Honest caveat on Wiese, since I am overturning a kill on his authority: the abstract states that
class membership is predictable for the majority of strong verbs but does not spell out in the
abstract itself what the prediction takes as input, beyond the classification of stem forms. The
present stem is what an infinitive gives you, so the natural reading is predictability from the
infinitive's stem. A less favorable reading is that Wiese is making a claim about descriptive
economy for the analyst rather than about what a speaker can derive, while Mailhammer's framing is
explicitly about the mental lexicon. Those two could partly coexist. But "partly reconcilable under
a careful reading" is not the same as "settled," and it is not what an unhedged sentence in a
learner's app conveys. Note also that Wiese says "majority," not "all," so the ambiguous pairs
(*leiden* / *meiden*, *scheiden* / *schneiden*, *biegen* / *liegen*, *beißen* / *heißen*) are
consistent with him. They show why the right word is "most," which is precisely the hedge at issue.

## Final disposition, and what Phase 4 should do

**Restore the finding, regrade it from nitpick to needs-hedging, and replace the prose with neither
prior agent's version.**

- Not `none`. The essay states one side of a documented specialist disagreement as flat fact, and
  the hedge already in the sentence covers a different property than the contested one.
- Not `factual-error`. Mailhammer supports the sentence. It is defensible, not false.
- Not `nitpick`. Phase 1's reason for nitpick was wrong, and the real reason for concern is
  stronger than Phase 1 knew. Both localizations ship it flat, so German readers get it flat too.

Phase 1's replacement should not be adopted as written. The skeptic's second objection to it is
sound: "the shape of the infinitive often points to the pattern" sits oddly beside "must largely be
memorized individually," and a reader is entitled to ask why memorization is needed if the shape
points the way. The fact that reconciles the two is the one both prior agents identified and
neither put into prose: an infinitive does not tell you whether the verb is strong in the first
place. My replacement carries that fact, which lets the sentence hedge the contested claim and stay
coherent at the same time.

Mechanical notes for Phase 4:

- Neither the current sentence nor the replacement contains any markup, in either localization.
  Nothing to balance, no crash risk.
- **The sentence is immediately followed by the backtick heading** `` `Development of the Perfect
  Tense` `` in English and `` `Entwicklung des Perfekts` `` in German, with no intervening
  whitespace. Per CLAUDE.md, headings concatenate directly. The splice must leave "...without
  settling it." adjacent to the opening backtick with no newline added.
- The German currently joins the clauses with a colon where the English uses a comma splice. Both
  replacements use two sentences, which incidentally retires the splice. The splice itself was
  correctly excluded from scope by both prior agents; this is a side effect, not the motive.

## Replacement prose

**English**, line 157, replacing the final sentence only:

> Today, German strong verbs must largely be memorized individually. No infinitive reveals whether
> a verb is strong at all, and among those that are, the shape of the stem still points to an
> ablaut pattern in most cases without settling it.

**German**, `docs/verb_history_de.txt` line 123, replacing the final sentence only:

> Heute müssen deutsche starke Verben größtenteils einzeln auswendig gelernt werden. Kein Infinitiv
> verrät, ob ein Verb überhaupt stark ist, und bei den starken Verben weist die Stammform in den
> meisten Fällen noch auf ein Ablautmuster hin, ohne es festzulegen.

"In most cases ... without settling it" is Wiese's "majority ... predictable" and Mailhammer's loss
of determinacy at the same time, which is the honest position given that the two disagree. Dropping
the abstract word "systematic" costs nothing: "still points to an ablaut pattern in most cases" is
the same claim made concrete, and it is the claim the sources actually support.
