# E3 · final verdict: partly · final grade: nitpick · the skeptic does not hold

Phase 1 graded E3 a nitpick. The Phase 2 skeptic killed it outright at grade `none`. I restore it
at Phase 1's grade, on reasoning Phase 1 did not give, with prose neither agent wrote, and with one
of Phase 1's supporting claims struck as false. The skeptic reached a defensible destination by a
route that had the decisive handbook fact backwards, and it applied a decision rule that would
delete the `nitpick` and `needs-hedging` grades from the rubric entirely.

## What the skeptic got right

Quite a lot, and it should be said plainly before the disagreement.

1. **It did not conflate weak sources with a wrong finding.** Phase 1 rested on Wikipedia and
   Citizendium. The skeptic noticed, went and got better sources, confirmed the underlying facts
   from them, and *still* refuted, explicitly separating "the finding is factually sound" from
   "the finding describes a defect." That is the right methodology and it is the failure mode this
   pass most often commits. The skeptic avoided it.
2. **The scope argument is real and has handbook backing.** Braune calls the voiceless stops the
   part the shift hits hardest: "Die hd. Lautverschiebung trifft am intensivsten die harten
   Verschlußlaute, weniger die weichen Laute unter b)". An essay that gives the headline of a
   phonological change it mentions in passing has genuinely not erred.
3. **Its critique of Phase 1's prose is correct on both counts.** "A second phase" is the wrong
   ordinal against standard enumerations, and the second "German … where English has …" clause
   twelve words after the first is flat cadence. Both objections stand and I have honored them.
4. **It found its own weakest point and stated it accurately**, which is more than most refutations
   manage. The *dō-* / *tun* bridge at line 155 is the strongest case for the finding, and the
   skeptic identified it unprompted.

## Where the skeptic is wrong, or is not

**Wrong, and this is the load-bearing one: it rescued Phase 1's worst claim instead of striking
it.** Phase 1 argued that the *d* > *t* phase "is the most widespread part of the whole shift,
reaching even the northernmost High German dialects." The skeptic flagged the Citizendium sentence
behind that as "the one link in its evidence that a skeptic could have pulled on," then declined to
pull, explaining away Ripuarian *Dür* as a later West Central German lenition of an already-shifted
*t*. Braune, the standard Old High German grammar, says otherwise (§ 23):

> Westgerm.-as. *d* ist ahd. im Oberd. und Ostfränkischen stets zu *t* geworden (dd > tt). Dagegen
> kommt im Mfränk. und Rheinfr. *t* statt *d* nur oft im Auslaut vor, während an- und inlautend *d*
> geblieben ist.

No lenition is needed. Middle Franconian and Rhine Franconian simply never shifted initial or medial
*d* at all: Braune's own paradigm line is `rheinfr. dohter, bindan, biodan` against
`ostfr./obd. tohter, bintan, biotan`. And the relation is the exact inverse of what Phase 1
asserted, because Braune describes the *voiceless* shift, not the voiced one, as the change with
total reach: germ. *t* spirantizes "über das ganze hochd. Gebiet sich erstreckend" (§ 20), germ. *p*
likewise "über das ganze hochd. Gebiet reichend" (§ 21). So *d* > *t* is not the most widespread
part of the shift. It is one of the *less* widespread parts. Phase 1's central justification is
false, Lewandowski's "im gesamten hd. Gebiet" is a simplification the skeptic accepted as
"the decisive source," and the skeptic spent its wrinkle-checking budget manufacturing a defense of
the claim it should have deleted. A kill reached without the strongest counter-fact on the table is
not a kill I can certify, even where I end up agreeing with parts of it.

**Wrong on method: "nothing in this sentence is false" cannot be near-dispositive in a rubric that
grades `needs-hedging` and `nitpick`.** The skeptic wrote that Phase 1's concession "is close to
dispositive" in a fact-check. But those two grades exist precisely for text that is true and
under-hedged, or true and incomplete. If "not false" settles the question, both grades are dead
letters and the rubric collapses to `factual-error` / `none`. That is the fourth failure mode on my
list, arriving in a slightly disguised form: the skeptic did not kill a hedge finding by checking
the fact, it killed an incompleteness finding by checking falsity, which is the same move.

**Not wrong, but supplied rather than found: the "scoped to voiceless stops" premise.** The
skeptic's whole first argument rests on the sentence's grammatical scope being voiceless stops. Read
in the paragraph, that scoping is the skeptic's, not the essay's. The preceding clause names the
object and states its significance: Old High German was "distinguished from other Germanic languages
by the ~High German consonant shift~." The next sentence begins "This series of sound changes," with
the whole named shift as its subject, and then defines it. Nothing marks the definition as partial:
no "chiefly," no "most conspicuously." Braune's definition of the same named object covers both
series. So the essay defines a thing more narrowly than the handbook defines it, with the narrowing
unmarked. That is not an error. It is the textbook shape of a nitpick.

**Not wrong in general, but wrong here: "no reader is misled by an absence they cannot detect."**
This would be a strong answer in most essays. It fails in this one because the essay itself makes
the absence detectable, two paragraphs up and in its own subject matter. Line 153: the weak verb
"added a dental suffix (*-d- or *-t-)", giving the "-te" ending in German (machte, sagte, spielte)
and the "-ed" ending in English. Line 155: the suffix comes from the verb "to do" (*dō-). Line 156 is
the paragraph where a reader holding German *-te* against English *-ed*, and German *tun* against
English *do*, would expect to be told why, and it is the one paragraph in the essay that could tell
them. The skeptic classed this as a declined pleasure. In a general essay it would be. In a history
of the German verb system, the voiced-stop phase is not a footnote to the shift; it is the part of
the shift that shaped a verb ending the essay has already put in front of the reader.

That last point is what separates this from "this passage could say more," the reductio the skeptic
correctly fears. The test that keeps the reductio away is not "would more be nice" but "does the
essay's own argument raise a question that this passage is the place to answer, and doesn't." Very
few omissions pass that test. This one does.

## My own research

Four searches plus five fetches, two of which produced the evidence. I took nothing from either
prior agent: neither had reached a handbook, and the Salmons volume the skeptic wanted was 403. I
routed to Braune/Ebbinghaus, *Abriß der althochdeutschen Grammatik*, which is the reference grammar
for exactly this period and which turned out to have a real text layer.

Three things came out of it, in descending order of how much they change the picture.

1. **The dialect geography is the reverse of Phase 1's claim.** Documented above: *p* and *t*
   spirantize across the entire High German area; *d* > *t* holds in Upper German and East
   Franconian and fails initially and medially in Middle and Rhine Franconian. Any prose that ships
   must not say "everywhere in High German."
2. **The Medienverschiebung is nonetheless inside the definition, not adjacent to it.** Braune § 18
   lists the pre-shift inventory in three groups and assigns the shift to two of them: hard stops
   most intensively, the voiced group less so, the hard fricatives not at all. So the essay's
   sentence is narrower than the handbook's definition of the same named change. This is what makes
   the omission a nitpick rather than nothing, and it is also why the skeptic's centrality argument
   survives in part: "less intensively" is not "not at all," but it is also not "equally."
3. **The verb link is confirmed at handbook level, which neither prior agent established.** Braune,
   introducing the weak verbs: they form the preterite "durch Antritt eines Dentalsuffixes (ahd.
   -ta, as. -da, -ta)." His paradigms carry it through: OHG *frumita* against Old Saxon *frumida*,
   OHG *denita* against OS *thenida*, OHG *zalta* against OS *talda*, OHG *legita* against OS
   *legda*. Old High German *t* against Old Saxon *d* in the same morpheme is the *d* > *t* shift,
   and that morpheme is the direct ancestor of the German "-te" the essay names at line 153. So the
   omitted phase is not a noun-flavored curiosity like *Tag* / *day*. It is the change that gave the
   German weak preterite its shape. Phase 1 never argued this and the skeptic never weighed it.

Incidental corroboration of content the essay already has, worth recording since nobody has checked
it: Braune § 20 says the *t* spirantization reaches the whole High German area "nur mfränk. (thür.?)
unterschoben die Neutra der Pron. (that, it, wat)." The essay's Kölsch *et* example is precisely
this, and it is right down to being the one documented exception.

## Final disposition, and what Phase 4 should do

**Restore the finding at grade `nitpick`, with a corrected basis and new prose.** Concretely:

1. **Strike Phase 1's rationale.** Do not carry forward "the most widespread part of the whole
   shift" or "reaching even the northernmost High German dialects." Both are false per Braune § 23,
   and the skeptic's lenition defense of them is also false. If any downstream text is generated
   from Phase 1's note, this is the sentence that must not survive contact with a reader who owns a
   grammar.
2. **Keep the finding, on the verb ground.** The essay's account of the High German consonant shift
   omits the phase that produced the German weak preterite suffix, in an essay about the German
   verb system, one paragraph after introducing that suffix and the verb it came from.
3. **Ship the two sentences below if Phase 4 is landing improvements; skip them if it is landing
   only corrections.** Nothing in the essay is wrong, and nitpick means optional. My recommendation
   is to land it, because this is the single omission in the passage whose repair pays off in the
   essay's own subject rather than in general erudition, and because ~machen~ is already sitting in
   the sentence as the *k* → *ch* example, so ~machte~ two clauses later shows one verb carrying
   both phases of the shift. That is the kind of buried connection this essay elsewhere goes out of
   its way to surface.
4. **Do not touch the existing parenthesis.** The "k → ch after a vowel" qualifier that Phase 1
   wanted and the skeptic defended belongs to E5 and E6, not here. My prose is purely additive and
   leaves that fight where it is.

## Replacement prose

Additive. Insert after the ~make~ clause and before "The effects of the shift were not uniform."

**English**, `docs/verb_history.txt` line 156:

> A later stage of the same shift turned Germanic d into t across much of the High German area. It
> is why ~machen~ builds its preterite as ~machte~ where English ~played~ keeps the older d, and why
> *dō- itself is ~tun~ in modern German.

**German**, `docs/verb_history_de.txt` line 122, appended after "…wo das Englische ~pound~,
~water~ und ~make~ hat.":

> In einer späteren Stufe verschob sich germanisches d in weiten Teilen des hochdeutschen Gebiets zu
> t. Daher bildet ~machen~ sein Präteritum als ~machte~, wo das Englische in ~played~ das ältere d
> bewahrt, und daher heißt *dō- im heutigen Deutsch ~tun~.

Notes on the drafting, since three constraints shaped it:

- **"across much of the High German area", not "everywhere".** This is the correction Braune forces
  and the reason neither prior agent's prose is usable. It also pre-echoes the next sentence,
  "The effects of the shift were not uniform," instead of fighting it.
- **It does not re-assert the debated etymology.** Line 155 hedges the *dō-* origin as "the most
  widely accepted theory." An earlier draft of mine read "*dō-, the verb behind that suffix," which
  would have flattened that hedge into a fact one paragraph later, and in German it would have
  shipped flat. "*dō- itself is ~tun~ in modern German" states only the uncontroversial sound
  correspondence and lets the reader make the join.
- **~played~ rather than a new example.** It is already in the essay at line 153, and English *-ed*
  after a voiced stem is the cleanest surviving *d* to hold German *-te* against.

Markup check: four `~…~` pairs in each language, all balanced; `*dō-` unpaired as required; no
`$…$`, no ` `` `, no `‡…‡`; no em dashes; no new parenthetical asides.
