# F8 · second opinion · final verdict: **upheld** · final grade: **factual-error**

Essay line 164. Phase 1 graded this needs-hedging. The Phase 2 skeptic returned partly and
downgraded to nitpick. I uphold the finding and grade it **above** what Phase 1 asked for. The
skeptic's verdict does not survive: its central premise is factually wrong, and its grade
contradicts its own reasoning.

The runbook flagged F8 in advance as likely to fall, on the ground that "a finding about an
omission has to show the omission misleads, not merely that more could be said." That is the right
test. The finding passes it. The essay's account of how Middle High German expressed future time is
not merely incomplete, it is wrong in the direction that matters, and the essay's timeline gives the
reader nowhere to put the correction.

## What the skeptic got right

Four things, and they are not small.

1. **Phase 1's category was wrong.** "needs-hedging" is defined in the runbook as "the question is
   contested and the essay states it flatly." The terminus Phase 1 wants added, Early New High
   German with a sixteenth-century culmination, is not contested; every source I reached says the
   same thing. Omitting a settled endpoint is not an unhedged contested claim. The skeptic is right
   that Phase 1 reached for the wrong label.
2. **"a true future auxiliary" overshoots.** The skeptic is right that the modal-versus-temporal
   status of *werden* + infinitive is live in the literature, and Joshua Booth's Oxford Middle High
   German syntax notes independently confirm the point for the earlier stages: the modal periphrases
   are what MHG actually used, and the *werden* pattern inherits their ambiguity. Any fix should say
   "the regular future auxiliary" and not "a true future auxiliary." I adopt this.
3. **An undated Early New High German half-fixes the problem.** The essay dates Old High German
   (750–1050) and Middle High German (1050–1350) at first mention. Introducing a third period with
   no dates, in a correction whose whole purpose is to let the reader place the change on a
   timeline, is a miss. I adopt this too.
4. **The source attribution correction is right.** The 143-against-39 ReM figures and the
   sixteenth-century explosion belong to Jäger 2024, not to a 2021 Hartmann article. My own search
   returns those numbers attached to the Jäger abstract.

So the skeptic did real work on Phase 1's prose. Its work on Phase 1's substance is where it fails.

## Where the skeptic is wrong, or is not

**Its central premise is false.** The skeptic's whole case rests on one sentence: "Beginning in
Middle High German" is an onset claim "and is correct under every account of the werden future."
That is not true, and it is not true in both directions at once.

- Concu 2022 puts *werden* + infinitive in **Old High German** and calls it well established in the
  first two centuries of MHG. Onset earlier than the essay says.
- **Paul's *Mittelhochdeutsche Grammatik*, the standard reference grammar of the period, gets
  *werden* + INF from the second half of the fourteenth century**, which is *after* the 1350 boundary
  the essay itself assigns to Middle High German. Onset later than the essay says.
- Bech's older account put the origin in the thirteenth century, inside MHG.

The proposed onsets span roughly five centuries and the essay picks one flatly. The skeptic could
not reach Paul, said so honestly, and treated the gap as immaterial because "the two peer-reviewed
articles above already settle the chronology." They settle the *terminus*. Paul is the source that
would have shown the *onset* is contested, which is the one thing the skeptic asserted was not.
That inverts the skeptic's argument: on its own definition of needs-hedging, the sentence contains a
flatly stated contested claim after all.

**It stopped reading at the colon.** The skeptic accused Phase 1 of lifting the sentence out of its
paragraph, then quoted the paragraph, then quoted only the sentence *before* the one under review.
The sentence after the example, line 168, is:

> This created a three-way temporal system (present, preterite/perfect, future) from the original
> two-tense Proto-Germanic system

"This" is anaphoric on the *werden* grammaticalization. "Created" is a completion verb. So the
section does report a completed change, in its own closing sentence, and the only period the section
names is Middle High German. The skeptic's defense, that the essay "pointedly does not write 'By
Middle High German German had a future'", is answered by the essay four lines later, where it says
in effect exactly that. This is the same failure mode the skeptic charged Phase 1 with, run in the
opposite direction: over-charitable reading produced by cutting the paragraph short.

**Its grade contradicts its own reasoning.** The runbook defines nitpick as "a specialist's quibble
that misleads nobody." The skeptic's own text concedes "a soft misdirection," concedes that a reader
trained on line 158's two-stage template "walks away placing the arrival of the German future
roughly two centuries early," and then grades the finding under a label defined by the absence of
misdirection. Both cannot hold. Either the reader is misled, in which case nitpick is unavailable,
or the reader is not, in which case the finding should have been refuted outright. The skeptic
picked the one grade its own paragraph rules out.

**It framed the defect too narrowly.** The skeptic treats the whole matter as an omitted terminus.
The larger defect is that the essay's positive picture of Middle High German is wrong. Booth,
reporting the handbooks: *werden* + infinitive is "**very rare (in both OHG and MHG)**" and
"ultimately wins out over the modal verbs, but **only firmly established by C16th**." Middle High
German marked futurity with the simple present and with *sol*, *wil* and *muoz* plus infinitive. The
essay hands that whole inventory to Old High German in the preceding sentence, "Old High German
expressed future time primarily through the present tense with temporal adverbs," and then opens the
next sentence with a contrastive "Beginning in Middle High German." A contrast structure asserts a
change of state at the boundary. No such change occurred at that boundary. The reader is not
underinformed about MHG, the reader is misinformed about it.

**Argument from absence, used the wrong way round.** The skeptic argued that the words licensing the
misreading "are the ones the essay conspicuously does not use." But the licence is not lexical, it is
structural. I checked: `Early New High German` and `Frühneuhochdeutsch` appear **nowhere** in either
localization, and the only dated periods in either file are 750–1050 and 1050–1350. The essay's
timeline stops at 1350 and resumes at "modern German." A reader who wanted to place the completion
later has no slot to put it in. The absence of Early New High German from the essay is not neutral
context, it is the mechanism of the error.

Where the skeptic is *not* wrong: it was right to test whether any handbook dates the establishment
to MHG, right that none does, and right that this therefore cannot be refuted outright. Its
instincts were sound. Its reach was short by one book.

## My own research

Six searches, three useful fetches, two blocked hosts routed around.

The decisive new source is a specialist Middle High German syntax lecture by Joshua Booth of Oxford
Linguistics, which synthesises Wright (1955), Paul (2007) and Jones & Jones (2019, 2024) and which I
extracted from the PDF locally after the fetch tool returned binary. It states the handbook position
on exactly the point at issue, and it supplies Paul's date, the thing the previous skeptic recorded
as an unreached source. Its four relevant claims, in its own words:

- "Gmc: Just uses the present tense. OHG: Develops periphrastic constructions with modal verbs …
  MHG: Lots of variation—various modal verbs competing. Can still simply use the present."
- "However, the modal strategy had become very successful. Competing forms: sol + infinitive
  (oldest: appears in OHG) … muoʒ + infinitive (rarer …) … wil + infinitive."
- "Very rare (in both OHG and MHG) is werden + infinitive. Ultimately wins out over the modal verbs,
  but only firmly established by C16th."
- "Paul (2007): Get werden + INF from 2nd half of C14th."

I verified Concu 2022 verbatim myself from the publisher rather than taking either prior agent's
quotation: "Old High German data also show the use of werden + infinitive, which suggests that it
was already well established in the first two centuries of the Middle High German period," and the
grammaticalization "culminated in the 16th century," in Early New High German. Jäger 2024 gives the
corpus shape: *werden* + Partizip I outnumbers *werden* + Infinitiv in thirteenth-century MHG, 143
to 39 in the ReM corpus, while *werden* + Infinitiv "increases explosively" in the sixteenth century
and displaces the modal-based future periphrases from then on.

Three independent lines converge on the same chronology, and none of them supports a Middle High
German arrival:

| | onset of *werden* + inf | status in MHG | regular future auxiliary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Concu 2022 | Old High German | in use, entrenched as a construction | ENHG, culminating 16c |
| Jäger 2024 | 13c, minority variant | outnumbered 39 to 143 by *werden* + Part. I | 16c, "explosively" |
| Paul via Booth | 2nd half of 14c | "very rare" | "only firmly established by C16th" |

The onset column disagrees with itself across five centuries. The last column does not disagree at
all. That is the exact opposite of the picture the skeptic worked from, in which the onset was
settled and only the terminus was missing.

## Final disposition, and what Phase 4 should do

**Restore the finding, at factual-error.** Neither prior grade fits:

- *needs-hedging* is Phase 1's label and the skeptic is right to reject the reasoning behind it,
  though for the wrong reason. The onset genuinely is contested and stated flatly, so the label is
  not empty. It is just not the main injury.
- *nitpick* is unavailable, because the skeptic itself concedes the reader is misdirected, and the
  grade is defined as misleading nobody.
- *factual-error* is defined by reader outcome, "a reader would be actively misinformed," not by
  whether an individual sentence is literally false. The belief this section reliably induces is
  that German acquired its *werden* future somewhere between 1050 and 1350. That belief is false by
  about two centuries, and the essay supplies no later period in which the reader could lodge the
  correction. The section also misdescribes MHG itself, which used the simple present and the modal
  periphrases for futurity while the construction the essay credits to it was very rare.

Phase 4 should replace the second sentence of line 164 with the prose below, and the corresponding
sentence in `docs/verb_history_de.txt` line 130 with its German counterpart. Three notes for that
document:

1. **Leave line 168 alone.** "This created a three-way temporal system" becomes true once the
   preceding sentence names the period in which the creation finished. Fixing one sentence fixes
   both.
2. **Leave the Old High German sentence alone.** Row F7 is owned separately and confirmed. The
   replacement repairs the false contrast by stating what MHG did, without re-opening F7.
3. **This is the essay's first and only mention of Early New High German**, so it carries dates at
   first mention, matching what the essay does for the other two periods at lines 156 and 158.

## Replacement prose

English, replacing only the second sentence of line 164 and keeping the colon that leads into the
`🇩🇪 Ich werde singen (I will sing)` example:

> Beginning in Middle High German, the verb ~werden~ (to become) was paired with an infinitive,
> though the construction stayed rare there beside the simple present and periphrases with ~sollen~
> and ~wollen~; only in Early New High German (1350–1650) was ~werden~ grammaticalized as the
> regular future auxiliary, a change that culminated in the sixteenth century:

It keeps the essay's own onset hedge and its own word "grammaticalized," moves that word to the
period the literature assigns it, states the rarity that makes the correction more than an added
date, names the competitors MHG actually used, and dates the new period the way the essay dates the
other two. Length is comparable to the two-stage perfect sentence at line 158. Markup: four `~…~`
pairs, eight tildes, balanced, no other markers, no em dashes, no new parenthetical aside. The
`(to become)` gloss and the `(1350–1650)` period gloss are both forms the essay already uses.

German, `docs/verb_history_de.txt` line 130, second sentence. The German file keeps the English
glosses, `(to have)` and `(to be)` at line 124, so `(to become)` stays as it is:

> Beginnend im Mittelhochdeutschen wurde das Verb ~werden~ (to become) mit einem Infinitiv
> verbunden, doch blieb die Konstruktion dort selten neben dem einfachen Präsens und Umschreibungen
> mit ~sollen~ und ~wollen~; erst im Frühneuhochdeutschen (1350–1650) wurde ~werden~ zum regulären
> Futurauxiliar grammatikalisiert, ein Wandel, der im 16. Jahrhundert seinen Abschluss fand:

Same four `~…~` pairs, same hedge strength, same dates, so neither localization ships a flatter
