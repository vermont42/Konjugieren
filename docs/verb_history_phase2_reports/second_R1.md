# R1 — final verdict: **upheld** (grade: **nitpick**). The skeptic's kill does not survive.

Essay line 90 (English), `docs/verb_history_de.txt` line 56 (German).

The sentence pair under review:

> The Yamnaya spoke what linguists reconstruct as ~Proto-Indo-European~ (PIE), the ancestor of languages spoken by nearly half of humans alive today. These languages include German, English, Ukrainian, Hindi, Persian, Cornish, Greek, and Latin.

Phase 1 said: "These languages" points back at a set defined as *spoken today*, and Latin is not in that set. Grade nitpick. The Phase 2 skeptic said: refuted, grade none. I find the skeptic's two load-bearing arguments both fail, one on semantics and one on its own cited authority, and I restore the finding at Phase 1's grade. I reject Phase 1's replacement prose and supply my own.

## What the skeptic got right

Four things, and they are not trivial.

**It quoted the right unit.** Phase 1's write-up leads with the second sentence alone. The skeptic restored the pair and analyzed both, which is the correct unit of analysis and the first thing an over-eager finding usually gets wrong. That check had to be run.

**Cornish is settled and settled in Phase 1's favor.** Both agents agree Cornish survives the essay's framing, and the skeptic strengthened the evidence: 563 people gave Cornish as their main language in the 2021 census of England and Wales, against 557 in 2011. So if there is a sore thumb in the list, it is Latin alone. Phase 4 should not touch Cornish.

**"Dead language" is a coarse instrument, and the skeptic was right to say so.** Latin retains real institutional and second-language use. Benedict XVI's *Latina Lingua* (2012) is a primary document showing it, and the skeptic found it. This matters for the grade: it is part of why the defect is venial.

**Phase 1's replacement prose is the wrong fix.** "Its descendants include German, English, …" makes the pronoun reach back over an intervening plural noun phrase to find PIE, and it trades the essay's tie between the family and living humanity for a bare genealogical list. The skeptic identified both costs correctly. I am not adopting that sentence either.

## Where the skeptic is wrong, or is not

### The collective-modifier argument is a non sequitur

This is the skeptic's first and main argument, and everything else it says is a fallback. Reconstructed:

1. No single language is spoken by nearly half of humanity.
2. Therefore "spoken by nearly half of humans alive today" cannot distribute over the members.
3. Therefore it is a property of the set taken together, which is to say a characterization of the Indo-European family.
4. Therefore "These languages" denotes the family, of which Latin is a member, and the mismatch evaporates.

Step 2 is correct. Step 3 does not follow from it, and it is where the argument breaks.

The modifier is a **cumulative** predication, not a collective one, and the two behave differently. In a cumulative reading, what aggregates is the *cardinality*; the relation itself still holds of each member. The textbook case is Scha's "600 Dutch firms use 5,000 American computers": the numbers aggregate, but the sentence still entails that each of those firms uses computers and each of those computers is used. Cumulativity does not make the verb vacuous on the parts. So "languages spoken by nearly half of humans alive today" denotes a plurality of languages each of which is spoken, today, by some subset of humanity, the subsets summing to nearly half. A language spoken by nobody contributes nothing to the sum and is not in the plurality. The head noun's modifier has not been suspended; only the headcount has been pooled.

The skeptic's own rebuttal to its own strongest objection depends on the same slip. It offered the parallel "Old Norse is the ancestor of languages spoken across Scandinavia today. These languages include Danish, Swedish, Norwegian, and Old Gutnish", then dismissed it on the ground that "spoken across Scandinavia today" distributes while "spoken by nearly half of humans alive today" cannot. That misdescribes the contrast. In both sentences the participle *spoken* distributes over the languages; only the measure phrase differs in whether it pools. Repair the parallel so the measure pools too, and the jar is exactly as loud:

> Old Norse is the ancestor of languages spoken by nearly one percent of humans alive today. These languages include Danish, Swedish, Norwegian, and Old Gutnish.

Nobody hears that as a claim about the North Germanic family that happens to include an extinct member. It reads as a list with a dead language smuggled into it. The skeptic disarmed its own counterexample by changing a variable that was not doing the work.

The bridging reading the skeptic wants, "These languages" = "the Indo-European languages", is genuinely available to a charitable reader. Loose anaphora of that kind is common in expository prose and no reader will be harmed. That is a reason to grade this a nitpick. It is not a reason to say the strict parse is unavailable, which is what the skeptic argued.

### The Ethnologue rescue is refuted by Ethnologue's own registration authority

The skeptic's fallback was that even on the strict parse nothing false is said, because Ethnologue lists Latin as a language of Vatican City used as a second language, so "Latin is spoken today" is "not a clean fact".

That citation is real but it is a sub-entry, and the parent registry settles the question the other way. SIL International is the ISO 639-3 registration authority and publishes Ethnologue off the same inventory. The registry's entry for `lat` records **Language Type: Historical**, not Living. The registry's own criterion for Living is explicit and is a first-language criterion: "A language is listed as *living* when there are people still living who learned it as a first language." So the authority the skeptic invoked classifies Latin as not living, by exactly the test Phase 1 applied. An Ethnologue country row saying Latin is used in Vatican City as an L2 is a statement about institutional use, not a promotion to living status, and it cannot be set against the type field that the same organization assigns.

The second half of this is arithmetical and, I think, decisive. Cash out the essay's own statistic. The "nearly half of humans alive today" figure is Ethnologue's: roughly 46 percent of the world's population, about 3.2 billion people, speaking an Indo-European language **as a first language**, distributed over about **445 living** Indo-European languages. The set the essay's first sentence names is therefore not a vague penumbra. It is the set of living Indo-European languages, and it is constituted by an L1 headcount to which Latin contributes exactly zero. The skeptic's restricted-L2-use defense does not touch that number, because L2 use is not what the number counts. On the essay's own figure, Latin is outside the set, and the membership test is not "not cleanly failed" but cleanly failed.

### Two procedural defects

**"The finding grades itself out of existence."** The skeptic quoted Phase 1's own "no reader will conclude Latin is a living language" and treated it as self-refutation. That collapses the grade scale. *Nitpick* is a grade in this taxonomy; it exists precisely for defects that are real, small, and cheap to fix. A finding that correctly identifies itself as a nitpick has done its job, not disqualified itself. If low stated harm zeroes a finding, no nitpick can ever survive and the grade is decorative.

**"Its two supporting citations are both Wikipedia, which is thin ground."** This is the conflation of *weakly sourced* with *wrong*, and it is doing rhetorical work in the verdict that it cannot support. Only the second decides anything, and the skeptic conceded every fact those citations were cited for: Latin has no L1 speakers and has had none for many centuries; Cornish died as a community language, was revived from Jenner's 1904 handbook, was reclassified by UNESCO in 2010, and is spoken today. A finding whose facts the refuter affirms has not been refuted by its bibliography. Phase 1's sourcing was indeed thin. That is a note for Phase 1, not a verdict.

### Where the skeptic was right against Phase 1

One correction of Phase 1 stands, partially. Phase 1 wrote flatly that Latin "is not a member" of the set of languages spoken today, on the ground that it has no native speakers. Read as a general claim about Latin's status in the world, that is too flat, and the skeptic's *Latina Lingua* citation shows why. Read as a claim about the essay's set, which is what the finding needs, it is right for the reason given above: the set is fixed by an L1 count. So Phase 1 reached a correct conclusion by a slightly blunter route than the one that actually works. That is worth recording, and it changes nothing about the disposition.

## My own research

Six operations, run independently of both prior agents and deliberately aimed at primary rather than tertiary sources, since both earlier reports rested on Wikipedia or on search-extracted snippets of pages that return 403.

- Two direct fetches of SIL's ISO 639-3 registry, which is the primary authority behind Ethnologue's inventory: the code page for `lat` and the "Types of Languages" definitions page. These are the strongest sources in this thread and neither prior agent reached them. They give Latin's type as Historical and give the Living criterion as first-language acquisition.
- A search on how the standard reference figure is constructed, confirming the 46 percent / 3.2 billion figure is an **L1** count over roughly 445 **living** Indo-European languages, sourced to Simons and Fennig's Ethnologue. This is the datum that decides the case, because it fixes what the essay's own set is.
- A search for how handbook treatments phrase the family, confirming the standard framing: eight branches with living members, six further subdivisions extinct. Every reference treatment separates "the family" from "the living members", which is the distinction the essay's second sentence elides. I could not reach Fortson's text directly.
- A search on Ethnologue's Latin entry, reproducing what the skeptic found, so I could check it against the registry rather than take it on trust.

I did not reopen "nearly half of humans alive today", which the brief records as dismissed twice, and I did not reopen Cornish.

## Final disposition, and what Phase 4 should do

**Restore the finding. Grade: nitpick.** The skeptic's kill is reversed.

In plain terms, for a reader who has seen neither prior report: the essay's first sentence describes a set of languages by saying they are spoken today by nearly half of humanity. The next sentence says "These languages include …" and ends the list with Latin. Latin has had no native speakers for many centuries, and the "nearly half" figure counts native speakers of living languages only, so Latin is not in the set the previous sentence just defined. Nothing false is asserted about Latin, and no reader will come away believing Latin is alive, which is why this is a nitpick and not an error. But the sentence is one phrase away from being exactly right, and the fix costs nothing.

Phase 4 should replace the **second** sentence only, in both languages, and leave the first sentence untouched. Leaving sentence one alone matters: it carries the tie between the family and living humanity that gives the paragraph its lift into the *tewtéh₂* passage, and the skeptic was right that a fix which sacrifices that tie is a bad trade.

Do **not** adopt Phase 1's "Its descendants include …". Use the prose below. It repairs the reference by naming the family instead of pointing at a set, and it earns its place independently: the essay has said "Proto-Indo-European" three times by this point but has not yet named the family itself, which does not appear unprefixed until line 128. Naming it here is where a reader wants it.

The sentence carries no markup in either language, so there is nothing to balance and no crash risk. No em dashes, no new parentheticals, and the replacement is the same length as what it replaces.

## Replacement prose

**English**, `docs/verb_history.txt` line 90, second sentence. Replace:

> These languages include German, English, Ukrainian, Hindi, Persian, Cornish, Greek, and Latin.

with:

> The Indo-European family includes German, English, Ukrainian, Hindi, Persian, Cornish, Greek, and Latin.

**German**, `docs/verb_history_de.txt` line 56, second sentence. Replace:

> Zu diesen Sprachen gehören Deutsch, Englisch, Ukrainisch, Hindi, Persisch, Kornisch, Griechisch und Latein.

with:

> Zur indoeuropäischen Sprachfamilie gehören Deutsch, Englisch, Ukrainisch, Hindi, Persisch, Kornisch, Griechisch und Latein.

The German keeps the existing `gehören` construction verbatim and only swaps the dative complement, so the rhythm is unchanged. Lowercase adjectival `indoeuropäischen` matches the essay's own usage at lines 59, 94, and 151; the capitalized `Indoeuropäische` forms elsewhere are the nominalized language name, not the adjective.
