# R5 · second opinion · final verdict: refuted · final grade: none (kill confirmed)

Essay line 110, English:

> ~Voice~ distinguished active and middle (the latter indicating action affecting the subject or done in the subject's interest), with a developing passive.

German, `docs/verb_history_de.txt` line 76:

> ~Diathese~ unterschied Aktiv und Medium (letzteres zeigte Handlung an, die das Subjekt betrifft oder im Interesse des Subjekts geschieht), mit einem sich entwickelnden Passiv.

Phase 1 graded this a nitpick and proposed replacing the coda. The Phase 2 skeptic refuted it and
graded it none. I ran the question a third time, off both agents' sources, and the kill stands.

## What the skeptic got right

Three things, and they are the load-bearing ones.

**It read the sentence in place rather than lifting it.** The finding's whole case is that the coda
"reads as a third item in a list of PIE features." The skeptic checked the actual syntax: the verb
"distinguished" governs two nouns, active and middle, and the passive sits outside that scope in an
absolute phrase. It then went further and steel-manned the finding's case anyway, by noting that the
paragraph sits in a run of parallel category entries (Aspect, Tense, Mood), two of them with bulleted
lists, so the list rhythm could bleed into the coda. Building the finding's best argument for it and
then answering it is what this pass is supposed to look like.

**It caught that Phase 1 quoted its own source selectively.** Phase 1 rests on the Folia Linguistica
abstract for "specialized markers of the passive are lacking," which is true, and then writes a
conclusion the same literature does not support: "the passives of the daughter languages are
independent later creations rather than a shared inheritance in progress." That is true of the
*morphology* and false of the *function*. Kulikov and Lavidas, whom Phase 1 cites, posit the passive
syntactic pattern for PIE despite the absence of a passive voice sensu stricto. The skeptic caught a
real overreach in the finding's "What is actually true" section, which is the section Phase 4 would
have acted on.

**It checked the German.** `verb_history_de.txt` line 76 carries "mit einem sich entwickelnden
Passiv," the identical participial hedge. I verified this in the file. There is no
English-hedged, German-flat asymmetry, which is the one thing that could have upgraded a nitpick.

## Where the skeptic is wrong, or is not

I looked hardest at the fourth failure mode on my list, killing a hedge finding because the
underlying fact checks out. This finding is genuinely hedge-shaped: Phase 1 does not say PIE had no
passive function, it says the coda "claims slightly more than the handbooks grant." A skeptic could
have dodged that by proving the fact and declaring victory. This one did not dodge. It argued the
hedge directly, that "developing" is a participle which cannot assert an existing category, and then
argued that the literature itself frames the situation as a development in progress. Both halves are
answers to the hedge complaint, not substitutes for one.

One place the skeptic is uncharitable, though it does not move the verdict. It reads Phase 1's
replacement, "the middle carried the work a passive would later do," as one that "pushes all passive
work into the daughters." It does not. The sentence says the middle did that work, in PIE, and that a
passive later took it over, which is close to what Kulikov and Lavidas describe. Phase 1's prose is
not inaccurate. The skeptic's *other* objection to it is the correct one and is sufficient on its
own: it is a coordinated clause where the original has a five-word coda, it repeats "the middle"
which the parenthesis has just glossed, and it buys no accuracy for the length. Not-an-improvement is
the right ground to decline prose on. Not-accurate was not.

One methodological weakness worth naming. The skeptic's most quotable support is Grestenberger 2018,
a DiGS conference handout, which is grey literature rather than a handbook, and it self-rated its
sources "mixed." That matters for how much weight the *verdict* can bear, so I went looking for
handbook-level confirmation independently. I found it, below, and it points the same way, so the
weakness is real but harmless here.

## My own research

Five searches plus four fetch attempts, deliberately aimed at handbooks and peer-reviewed work rather
than at either agent's citations. Three of the fetches were blocked (De Gruyter 405, Penn 403, Brill
403), one PDF was unreadable, so two of my quotations below are search-surfaced snippets of the Folia
Linguistica article rather than text I pulled from the page myself. I flag that rather than hide it.
The picture converges from four independent directions:

**The count of voices.** Two, active and middle or mediopassive. Nobody reconstructs three. The essay
does not claim three: "distinguished" takes exactly two objects.

**Whether a passive is reconstructible.** Not as morphology. Apart from *-i̯(e/o)-, for which a
passive function cannot be reconstructed for the proto-language, there is no specialized passive
marking, and the dedicated formations of the daughters (Greek -ē-/-thē-, Indo-Aryan -ya-) are
independent post-breakup innovations that are not cognate. Phase 1 has this exactly right, and the
essay does not contradict it.

**Whether a passive was developing.** This is the whole question, and here the literature uses the
essay's own vocabulary. The standard account is that the middle acquired passive function, that this
"is usually held to have taken place at a late stage of PIE, or possibly later, in the individual
languages," and that the development "must be traced back to some late stage of PIE in which the
middle voice sporadically began to be interpreted as a passive, depending on the context." A passive
that has begun to be interpreted out of the middle, sporadically and contextually, at a late stage of
the parent language, is a developing passive. The essay's coda is not leaning past the handbooks. It
is a four-word compression of them.

**The Germanic continuation, which the essay is heading toward anyway.** Gothic is the only Germanic
language to inherit finite middle forms with passive function, and they are restricted to the present
indicative and optative; the other Germanic languages lost the inflected middle entirely and rebuilt
the passive periphrastically with the participle plus an auxiliary. So the development the coda
gestures at does not stop at the PIE boundary. It runs straight into the German *werden* passive a
reader of this app already uses. If anything, "developing" is the historically apt word precisely
because the thing was still in motion for another three thousand years.

The one thing I could not settle at handbook level is the minority position, that the passive use of
the middle is itself a branch innovation rather than late-PIE. That view exists in the literature and
the productivity and properties of the middle passive are explicitly described as debated. But note
which way that cuts: on the minority view "developing" is *more* cautious than the mainstream, not
less. The essay survives either horn.

## Final disposition, and what Phase 4 should do

**Kill confirmed. Verdict refuted, grade none. Phase 4 should change nothing at line 110, and nothing
at `verb_history_de.txt` line 76.**

For a reader who has seen neither prior report: the essay says PIE had two voices and that a passive
was on the way. That is what the field says. The finding's objection was not that this is false but
that a reader might misparse the four-word coda as announcing a third voice. The word "developing"
prevents that parse, since a category described as developing is by definition not asserted to exist,
and even a reader who did misparse it would land on something the literature supports, because a
passive function really was emerging from the middle in late PIE. The finding's own summary of the
truth, meanwhile, contains a claim the essay does not make and the sources do not support, namely
that the daughters' passives are independent creations "rather than a shared inheritance in progress."
The passive *use of the middle* is a shared inheritance; only the dedicated affixes are independent.
Acting on the finding would have moved the essay away from the literature, not toward it.

I considered and rejected a middle landing that neither prior agent proposed, namely keeping the
sentence but naming the mechanism, "with a passive developing out of it." It is marginally more
explanatory and marginally longer, and it is the kind of edit that looks free but is not: this
sentence is one line of a four-entry parallel run, the German has to move with it, and both
localizations ship as translated. A rewrite whose only benefit is closing a misreading that the
existing participle already closes is not worth two file edits and a relocalization.

## Replacement prose

None. Neither Phase 1's replacement nor any of my own should be applied.
