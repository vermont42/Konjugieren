# R8 · second opinion · verdict: partly · grade: nitpick

Line under review, `docs/verb_history.txt` line 122, `docs/verb_history_de.txt` line 88, and twice in
`Konjugieren/Assets/Localizable.xcstrings` (lines 3072 and 3078):

> 🇩🇪 nehmen, $nahm$, $genOMmen$ (take, $tOOk$, taken)

Phase 1 graded this a nitpick and proposed swapping the row for `brechen / break`. The skeptic
refuted it outright and graded it none. My disposition: the skeptic wins the argument it actually
picked, loses two of the arguments it used to win it, and overshoots on the grade. The finding is
restored at nitpick, and I found a separate defect on the same line that neither agent saw and that
Phase 4 should fix regardless of what it does with the cognate question.

## What the skeptic got right

The central reading is correct, and the finding as Phase 1 wrote it does depend on the reading
being wrong. Phase 1 says the row "marks both sets of vowels red as though they illustrated the
same inheritance," which treats the parenthesis as a claim of correspondence between the German
lexeme and the English one. The essay's convention will not carry that. A parenthesis after a 🇩🇪
bullet in this document is an English rendering of what precedes it, and the essay is happy to put
non-cognate English in that slot.

The skeptic's own best evidence is evidence it did not cite. Line 166, `🇩🇪 Ich werde singen (I will
sing)`, and line 175, `🇩🇪 Modern alternative: Wenn ich kommen $wÜrde$... (If I $wOUld$ come...)`,
are the identical construction: 🇩🇪 bullet, German material, parenthesis. `werden` is cognate with
English `worth` as in "woe worth the day," not with `will`; `würde` and `would` are likewise
unrelated, `würde` continuing `werden` and `would` continuing `willan`. Both parentheses are plain
translations and nobody would read them otherwise. Add the German localization, where line 88 is
byte-identical to the English: for a German reader the parenthesis can only be the English
rendering. That settles the convention from both sides.

The skeptic is also right that the framing sentence at line 119 scopes itself to "the vowel
alternations we still see in ~German strong verbs~ today," and that `nehmen / nahm / genommen` is a
flawless class IV German paradigm, which is what that sentence promises. Nothing in the German
column is wrong, and Phase 1 never said it was.

## Where the skeptic is wrong, or is not

**Its "decisive instance" is not one.** The skeptic rests its convention argument on line 153:

> the "-te" ending in German preterites (machte, sagte, spielte) and the "-ed" ending in English
> ($mAde$, $saId$, played)

and says line 153 "does with `played` precisely what line 122 does with `take`." It does not. Read
the syntax: the second parenthesis attaches to "the '-ed' ending in English" and lists English
examples of an English ending. It is not glossing the German list; it is a second list of its own.
That `spielte` happens to sit third in one list and `played` third in the other is an artifact of an
author picking three parallel examples, not a translation slot. So the one construction the skeptic
called decisive is structurally unlike line 122, while the two constructions that really are like
line 122 went uncited. The conclusion survives; the argument offered for it does not.

**The `give` tu quoque is a false equivalence, and it is the skeptic's load-bearing rhetorical
move.** The skeptic argues that `give` is Norse-touched too, so "two of three rows are spoiled" and
Phase 1's criterion "was invented for the middle row rather than applied to the display." Etymonline,
checked directly, says the opposite of what that argument needs: `give` is Old English `giefan`,
inherited, and Old Norse `gefa` influenced only the initial consonant, arresting the regular
Middle English development to `yiven`. The lexeme is native; a consonant was preserved by contact.
`take` is a wholesale Norse loan that displaced the native cognate `niman` outright. And the
difference lands exactly where the essay is looking: the row reddens vowels, the paragraph is about
vowels, and `give / gave / given` has an unbrokenly inherited English vowel series while
`take / took / taken` has an imported one. Phase 1's criterion is applied consistently and `give`
passes it. The charge of a criterion invented for the occasion is unfounded.

**It answers the assertion charge and treats that as answering the implicature charge.** The
skeptic's closing line is "I cannot construct a reader who ends up misinformed by line 122." I can,
and it is this app's paradigm reader. Rows one and three are cognate pairs in matching classes,
`singen / sing` class III and `geben / give` class V. Two rows in a row train the eye to read the
column as correspondence, and the essay elsewhere does exactly that kind of correspondence in prose
at line 156, "giving German words like ~Pfund~, ~Wasser~, and ~machen~ their characteristic sounds
where English has ~pound~, ~water~, and ~make~." A reader who collects etymological connections,
which is precisely the reader this essay is written for, is the reader most likely to take
`nehmen / take` as one more of those and to walk away believing `take` is the English continuation
of `nehmen`. It is not. That belief is not asserted by the essay, which is why this is a nitpick and
not an error, but "the text does not assert it" is not the same as "no reader acquires it," and the
skeptic used the first as though it were the second.

**One more thing the parenthesis at 121 to 123 does that no other parenthesis in the essay does.**
Lines 166 and 175 translate a German sentence into an English sentence. Lines 121 to 123 render an
English paradigm in the app's ablaut notation, with red letters that mean "this letter is
irregular." That is a translation which has additionally been made into an exhibit. As an exhibit of
"English has ablaut too" it is fine and true. As an exhibit placed beside a German paradigm it does
invite the comparison Phase 1 objected to. The invitation is weak, the essay never accepts it, and
so nitpick is where this lands. But the skeptic's picture of a pure translation slot understates
what the markup is doing.

## My own research

Six lookups, independent of both prior agents.

**`take` and `niman`.** Confirmed, and not in dispute between any of us. `take` is a Norse loan into
late Old English, entering as a class VI strong verb with `tōc` and `tacen`, competing with and then
displacing native `niman`, which is class IV and the true cognate of `nehmen`. A peer-reviewed
treatment of Norse loans in Middle English describes the first attestation of `tacan` as also the
first evidence of "the lexical and semantic rivalry with inherited `niman`, in which the inherited
verb is eventually superseded by the Norse loan."

**`give`.** Checked at source rather than taking the skeptic's characterization. Old English
`giefan`, native; Middle English `yiven`; the hard `g` owed to Norse `gefa` and Old Danish `givæ`.
Consonantal influence on an inherited lexeme, not a borrowed lexeme. See above.

**Class VI's Proto-Indo-European pedigree, which neither prior agent examined.** This is the one
place my research changes the picture rather than confirming it. Classes III, IV and V, which is
every verb in the German column, continue the ordinary PIE `e / o / zero` series that lines 113 to
117 have just laid out. Class VI, the series `take / took / taken` illustrates, is the outlier: the
Germanic `a` traces variously to `*h₂e`, to `*o`, or to an interconsonantal laryngeal, and on the
long vowel the literature says plainly that "it is not clear exactly how the ō is to be derived from
an earlier ablaut alternant in PIE," with the candidate accounts being reduplicant contraction in
`*h₂`-initial roots or o-grades of laryngeal roots. I could not reach Fulk or Mailhammer directly,
one 403 and one paywall, so I hold this at the confidence the accessible summaries support, which is
enough for the modest use I make of it: the sentence at line 125, "These vowel changes are direct
inheritances from Proto-Indo-European," is true of the German paradigms it is about, and the English
exhibit chosen for the middle row happens to be drawn from the one Germanic ablaut series whose
derivation from a PIE alternant is openly unsettled. That does not falsify anything. It does mean
the middle row's English exhibit is the weakest of the three at the job the paragraph hired it for,
and it is a second small reason to prefer a different row, independent of the cognate question.

**The defect nobody found.** I extracted every `$…$` form in the essay body mechanically. There are
27 in the English body and 30 in the German. `nahm` is the only one in either file with no uppercase
letter in it. The essay's convention is unambiguous elsewhere: forms with no change are left
undelimited, which is why line 123 writes bare `gegeben` and line 161 writes bare `gekommen` and
line 122 writes bare `taken`, since `take` to `taken` changes no vowel. `$nahm$` is delimited, so it
is declared to be a changed form, and then marks nothing. Since uppercase inside `$…$` is what
renders red, the row currently shows a red `A` in `$sAng$` above and a red `A` in `$gAb$` below and
nothing at all in the preterite between them, in a three-line display whose entire purpose is to
show which vowel changed. `nehmen` to `nahm` is an `e` to `a` alternation of exactly the kind the
rows above and below mark. The correct form is `$nAhm$`.

The skeptic defended this as a feature, writing that the row "removes the useful asymmetry, where
unmarked `taken` beside marked `$genOMmen$` shows the reader that the two languages did not preserve
the same amount." That conflates two different asymmetries. Unmarked `taken` is correct and
principled. Unmarked `$nahm$` is neither; it is the single exception in 57 marked forms across both
localizations, and it ships that way in both catalog entries.

## Final disposition, and what Phase 4 should do

Restore the finding at Phase 1's original grade of nitpick, with the reasoning changed. The essay
asserts nothing false and asserts no cognate parallel, so the skeptic is right that this is not an
error and Phase 1 was right to grade it low. But "none" is too strong: the display trains a
correspondence reading over two rows and then breaks it on the third, in an app whose reader is a
connection-hunter, and the row is additionally the weakest of the three as an illustration of
inherited PIE ablaut. That is a nitpick in the ordinary sense, a real if small improvement that
nobody should insist on.

Two actions, in priority order.

1. **Do this one.** Fix `$nahm$` to `$nAhm$`. Four sites: `docs/verb_history.txt` line 122,
   `docs/verb_history_de.txt` line 88, and `Konjugieren/Assets/Localizable.xcstrings` lines 3072 and
   3078. This is not the cognate question at all; it is a markup inconsistency on the same line, it
   is mechanically verifiable, and it costs nothing. Note the catalog edit touches no ASCII double
   quote, so the Edit tool is safe here per CLAUDE.md, and `git diff --stat` should show equal
   insertions and deletions with no reformatting.
2. **Josh's call.** Swap the row to `brechen / break` if the cognate parallel is worth the frequency
   loss. `brechen` and `break` are cognate, both continue Germanic `*brekan` from PIE `*bʰreg̑-`, and
   both are class IV, so all three rows would then be cognate pairs in matching classes. The cost is
   real and the skeptic named it correctly: `nehmen` is a top-tier German verb and `brechen` is not.
   My recommendation is to make the swap, because the display is teaching a pattern rather than
   vocabulary and the other two rows already set the pattern the middle row breaks. If the swap is
   made, action 1 is moot, since the row disappears.

Phase 1's replacement prose is sound as written and needs no rewriting from me. The `$` delimiters
balance at eight, the capitalization follows `$sAng$` and `$gAb$`, and both English past forms are
marked as line 121 marks both `$sAng$` and `$sUng$`.

## Replacement prose

Minimal fix, recommended, English `docs/verb_history.txt` line 122 and German
`docs/verb_history_de.txt` line 88, which are identical:

> 🇩🇪 nehmen, $nAhm$, $genOMmen$ (take, $tOOk$, taken)

Optional row swap, if the cognate parallel is wanted, same two lines:

> 🇩🇪 brechen, $brAch$, $gebrOchen$ (break, $brOke$, $brOken$)

Both are markup-safe. The first keeps the existing six `$` delimiters; the second has eight. Neither
nests a marker inside another, and neither introduces an asterisk.
