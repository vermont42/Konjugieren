# R9 · final verdict: partly · final grade: nitpick

The skeptic's kill was mostly right and decisively wrong on one word. Phase 1's `needs-hedging`
does not survive; the skeptic's `none` does not survive either. The finding lands as a `nitpick`
with a one-clause fix.

## What the skeptic got right

Three of its four arguments hold, and two of them hold completely.

**The antecedent argument is correct.** Line 125 says "These vowel changes," not "these vowels,"
and the only available referent is line 119's "the vowel alternations we still see in German strong
verbs today." That phrase is the direct object of a clause whose main assertion is that PIE *e and
*o "later developed into different vowels in the daughter languages." So the essay does say, one
sentence earlier and in the sentence that introduces the triads, that the vowel qualities changed.
Phase 1 quotes that lead-in and then argues as though it were absent. On the narrow question Phase
1 framed, whether the essay claims German inherited PIE's vowels, the skeptic is right and Phase 1
is wrong.

**The terminology argument is correct.** "Inherited" in historical linguistics contrasts with
"borrowed," and inherited material is exactly the material regular sound change operates on. A
sentence calling something a direct inheritance is not thereby claiming phonetic identity.

**The German check is correct, and I verified it.** German line 85 chains `die Ablautmuster` →
`den Vokalwechseln, die wir noch heute in deutschen starken Verben sehen`, and line 91 opens
`Diese Vokalwechsel`. `Vokalwechsel` is unambiguously "vowel alternation." Phase 1's standing worry
that a loose English claim ships flatter in German is inverted here.

**Its critique of Phase 1's replacement prose is right on both counts.** "Reshaped by five
millennia of sound change" names only sound change, when the levelling Phase 1 leans on is analogy,
not sound change. And "still recognizable through all of it" invites the very misreading Phase 1
set out to prevent.

It also avoided two of the failure modes I was told to look for: it did not argue from absence, and
it explicitly refused to conflate weak sourcing with wrongness ("The exposure is not in the sources
but in the inference drawn from them"). That is honest work.

## Where the skeptic is wrong, or is not

It fell into the fourth failure mode: it killed a hedge finding because the underlying fact checks
out, when the objection was to an intensifier the fact does not license.

**It defended "inherited" and then let "preserved" ride in for free.** The predicate on line 125 is
not "inherited." It is "direct inheritances ... preserved across five millennia of linguistic
evolution." The skeptic's terminology argument licenses "inheritance." It says nothing about
"preserved," which is a claim about continuity *without substantive alteration*. The skeptic saw
this. It wrote: "There is a residual softness in the word 'preserved,' which reads a shade more
static than 'transmitted.'" It then dismissed the softness by pointing at the trailing phrase: "a
sentence that ends 'across five millennia of linguistic evolution' is not inviting a reader to
think nothing happened."

That reading of the trailing phrase is backwards, and it is the same sin the skeptic charges Phase
1 with. "Preserved across five millennia of linguistic evolution" is concessive *in favor of*
preservation. It means preserved despite the evolution. Grammatically the phrase raises the stasis
claim rather than qualifying it. Read that way, the sentence's second half asserts more than its
first half, and the paragraph's earlier qualification does not reach it.

**It never tested the claim against the object the sentence actually points at.** "These vowel
changes" is deictic: it points at the three triads printed immediately above. So the question that
decides the finding is not "is Germanic ablaut inherited from PIE," which nobody disputes, but "is
the alternation displayed in those three triads a direct inheritance preserved." For two of the
three the answer is materially more complicated than the sentence allows, and neither prior agent
raised either point.

*singen, sang, gesungen* is a three-slot pattern. Old High German had a four-slot one, with the
preterite plural distinct from the preterite singular. Universität Zürich's Ad fontes, the
skeptic's own source, states it flatly: "Numerusunterscheidung zwischen Sg. und Pl. innerhalb des
Präteritums: ahd. *sang* 'ich sang' vs. *sungum* 'wir sangen'." Modern *sangen* is that plural
levelled to the singular vowel, part of the general "Formen- und damit ein Vokalausgleich besonders
im Präteritum" that reduced the surviving strong verbs to three principal parts on the way to New
High German. The alternation the reader is looking at is therefore not the inherited alternation.
It is what is left of it after a restructuring that is centuries old, not five millennia old.

*geben, gab, gegeben* is worse for the sentence. In Proto-Germanic classes 5 and 6 the past
participle carries the same vowel as the infinitive rather than a zero grade, because a zero grade
of a root like this one is unpronounceable. The *e* of *gegeben* is not a PIE grade preserved; that
slot was rebuilt inside Germanic.

So the paragraph corrects one of the two overstatements and not the other. Line 119 handles vowel
quality: the sounds changed. Nothing in the paragraph handles pattern continuity, and "preserved
across five millennia" is a pattern-continuity claim. The skeptic's antecedent argument establishes
that the sentence is about the pattern, and then treats "about the pattern" as equivalent to "safe."
About the pattern is where the sentence is weakest.

**On the grade, the skeptic is more right than Phase 1.** Its rubric point stands: `needs-hedging`
is for a contested question stated flatly, and that Germanic ablaut descends from PIE ablaut is not
contested by anyone. Ad fontes puts it in one sentence. Phase 1 picked the wrong grade for a real
observation. But `none` overshoots, because an unearned intensifier in the sentence that closes a
section is not nothing, and the skeptic's own stated fallback, `nitpick`, is where this belongs.

## My own research

I did not take either agent's sources. Nine research operations, four of them searches; three
fetches failed (Fulk's *Comparative Grammar* returned 403 on ResearchGate, two university PDFs came
back as undecoded binary).

What I could confirm at handbook or university level:

- Germanic ablaut is inherited from Indo-European ablaut. Uncontested, verbatim in Ad fontes. This
  is the essay's core proposition and it is correct.
- Germanic did not merely receive the system, it systematized it: the grades took on the job of
  marking tense, which is not what they did in PIE. Also Ad fontes.
- Old High German preserved a four-way distinction including a preterite plural vowel, *sang* vs.
  *sungum*. Ad fontes, quoted above.
- Middle High German still counted four Stammformen, and the transition to New High German brought
  a vowel levelling concentrated in the preterite across the roughly 210 surviving strong verbs.
  This is standard German-philology teaching material rather than a handbook I could open; I flag
  the source strength honestly, but the *sungum* → *sangen* levelling is not in dispute and Ad
  fontes independently establishes the Old High German end of it.
- Ringe (2017) is reported as stating that Proto-Germanic classes 5 and 6 had the same vowel in the
  infinitive and the past participle. I reached this through a search summary reporting Ringe, not
  through Ringe directly, so I weight it as support rather than proof. It is consistent with the
  standard account of why class V has no zero grade.

Both prior agents rested on Britannica, Wikipedia, and the Texas LRC grammar for phonology that
neither disputes. The phonology was never the issue. The issue is a judgment about what "preserved"
asserts, and on that the useful evidence is the Old High German paradigm, which Ad fontes supplies
and which the skeptic quoted from the same page without noticing that it cuts against the sentence
it was defending.

## Final disposition, and what Phase 4 should do

**Restore the finding at a reduced grade: `nitpick`, not `needs-hedging`, not `none`.**

For a reader who has seen neither prior report: the essay's sentence is not false and does not
misstate any contested science. Its first half, "direct inheritances from Proto-Indo-European," is
the standard handbook statement. Its second half, "preserved across five millennia of linguistic
evolution," asserts a stability that the German strong-verb paradigm does not have. The three-part
alternation printed directly above that sentence is a New High German reduction of an older
four-part one, and one of the three examples has a participle vowel that Germanic rebuilt. The fix
is one clause. Phase 4 should not rewrite the sentence, delete it, or touch line 119, which is
already correct.

Change the second half only, in both localizations. English line 125 and German line 91. Both
splice straight into the following heading with no newline; the replacements below preserve that.
Neither replacement touches `~`, `$`, `‡`, `^`, or heading backticks, and neither introduces an
asterisk. "Five millennia" is retained, so if G13 revises the figure this sentence tracks G13, as
Phase 1 noted.

Phase 1's replacement prose should not be used. The skeptic's objections to it are correct, and my
version fixes both: it names analogy alongside sound change, and it drops "recognizable."

## Replacement prose

**English, line 125.** Replaces "These vowel changes are direct inheritances from Proto-Indo-European,
preserved across five millennia of linguistic evolution."

> These vowel changes descend from Proto-Indo-European ablaut, carried across five millennia by sound change and analogy rather than preserved unchanged.`The Migration to Europe`

**German, line 91.** Replaces "Diese Vokalwechsel sind direkte Erbschaften aus dem
Proto-Indoeuropäischen, bewahrt über fünf Jahrtausende sprachlicher Evolution."

> Diese Vokalwechsel stammen vom proto-indoeuropäischen Ablaut ab, über fünf Jahrtausende durch Lautwandel und Analogie weitergetragen und nicht unverändert bewahrt.`Die Wanderung nach Europa`
