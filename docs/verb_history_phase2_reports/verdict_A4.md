# A4 · verdict: refuted · grade: none

## Why

Three things sink this finding, and any one of them would be enough.

**1. The essay does not make the claim that is contested.** Librado et al. 2021 reject a specific
proposition, and they state it in one sentence: "Our results reject the commonly held association
between horseback riding and the massive expansion of Yamnaya steppe pastoralists into Europe
around 3000 bc driving the spread of Indo-European languages." What is rejected is *horseback
riding as the mechanism of the expansion*. Line 126 says "Equipped with horses, wheeled carts, and
perhaps bronze weapons, they moved westward in successive waves." It asserts possession, not
traction and not propulsion. It does not say they rode into Europe, does not say the horses pulled
the carts, and does not say horses drove the migration or the spread of the languages. Phase 1
concedes in its own "Why" section that "Yamnaya horses existed as a genetic population, so
'equipped with horses' is not false of the Yamnaya in their homeland," and then grades the sentence
anyway on a reading it has to supply itself. Nobody on either side of this dispute denies that the
Yamnaya had horses. Librado's own dataset contains them.

**2. The hedge the finding wants is already in the essay, on the exact point, in the sentence that
introduces the Yamnaya and the horse.** Line 86: "The Yamnaya may have been among the first to ride
the horse, a reading some of their skeletons support and other specialists dispute, and whether or
not they rode, the recently invented wheel gave them a mobility no earlier people had enjoyed."
That sentence hedges the riding reading, names the dispute, and explicitly relocates the load onto
the wheel. Phase 1 takes S1 as given and undisturbed, which means it accepts that the reader of
line 126 has already been told the riding question is open. Hedging the same question a second time
forty lines later, in a sentence that never raised riding, is redundancy, not accuracy.

**3. Phase 1's "what is actually true" is a snapshot of one camp in a live dispute, and the other
camp published a point-by-point rebuttal in May 2026 that Phase 1 does not cite.** Anthony,
Trautmann and Heyd, *Science Advances* 12(20), 13 May 2026, "Horse genetics, archaeology, and the
beginning of riding," is aimed precisely at the two Librado papers Phase 1 rests on, and it takes
apart the very inference Phase 1 calls "direct rather than inferential." Their abstract: "Yamnaya
groups (~3200–2600 BCE) rode DOM2 horses—the direct ancestors of modern domestic stock—while
incorporating them into diets, rituals, and mobility systems." On the Corded Ware evidence
specifically: the four CWC horses come from a single anomalous ritual site in Upper Franconia with
more Neolithic LBK pottery than CWC pottery, and "Horses or their parts selected for a single
Franconian mountain ritual with Neolithic origins cannot represent all CWC horses." On the modeling:
"The topological features related to DOM2 ancestry in CWC horses varied between models, so strong
conclusions about this topic cannot be supported until a clearer signal can be generated from a
larger sample of CWC horses." And on the migration itself: "The Yamnaya migrations into Southeast
Europe that began ~3100 BCE were compatible with the spread of DOM2-clade horses."

That is a genuinely contested question. But an essay is not obliged to hedge a sentence that is
true under both resolutions, and this one is. Under Librado the Yamnaya had horses and did not ride
them west; under Anthony they had horses and some rode. "Equipped with horses" survives either way.

One incidental note on Phase 1's source list, which I flag only because it bears on how carefully
the ground was walked: the URL Phase 1 attaches to "Trautmann, M. et al. (2023) … Science Advances"
is `sciadv.ady7336`, which is not the 2023 Trautmann paper (`sciadv.ade2451`) but the 2026
Anthony–Trautmann–Heyd rebuttal. Phase 1 pulled the Maier re-analysis detail out of the 2026 paper
while citing it as the 2023 one, and so never reckoned with what the 2026 paper actually concludes.

## What is actually true

Horses were present in Yamnaya life throughout: as food, in ritual and prestige display, and in the
herd. That much is uncontested and is all line 126 asserts.

Whether the Yamnaya rode, and whether horses figured in the westward migrations, is open and
actively argued as of mid-2026.

- The genomics case (Librado et al. 2021, Nature 598; Librado et al. 2024, Nature 631) is that
  DOM2-mod, the lineage carrying the riding-associated GSDMC and ZFPM1 variants, does not appear
  before roughly 2200–2100 BCE and expands across Eurasia from about 2000 BC "synchronously with
  equestrian material culture, including Sintashta spoke-wheeled chariots," and that Corded Ware
  horses in Europe lack steppe ancestry, so the westward waves were not horse-driven.
- The archaeology case (Trautmann et al. 2023 on skeletal riding pathologies; Anthony, Trautmann
  and Heyd 2026) is that DOM2-mod postdates Yamnaya by definition and so proves nothing about what
  Yamnaya rode, that the CWC counter-evidence is four horses from one ritual site and does not
  survive re-modeling, and that riding plus ox-drawn wagons is what made the Yamnaya economy
  mobile in the first place: "When horse riding was combined with ox-drawn wagons ~3200 BCE, the
  domestic unit became mobile and an innovative kind of pastoral economy was invented … giving
  birth to the Yamnaya culture."

Phase 1's flat statement that "the westward waves were wagon-borne, not horse-borne" and that
"widespread horse-based mobility across Eurasia dates to about 2200 BC, several centuries after
these migrations" states one side of that dispute as settled fact. The wagon half is solid: Yamnaya
wagon burials are real and the traction was paired oxen, which the 2026 paper affirms as readily as
the 2021 one. The "not horse-borne" half is exactly what is being fought over.

## Phase 1's replacement prose

> Equipped with ox-drawn wagons and perhaps bronze weapons, they moved westward in successive
> waves. Widespread horse-based mobility came later, arising across Eurasia around 2200 BC.

This is both unnecessary and independently wrong, and the second defect is the serious one.

- The added sentence converts a contested question into a flat assertion in the *opposite*
  direction. "Widespread horse-based mobility came later, arising across Eurasia around 2200 BC" is
  the precise framing the 2026 rebuttal exists to attack: its abstract holds that domestication's
  "transformative effects on human mobility and social organization began as early as the fourth,
  if not the fifth millennium BCE." Phase 1 would trade a sentence true on both readings for one
  false on one of them. That is an unhedged claim about a contested question, the thing the brief
  defines as an error.
- It drops a fact that is true on every account, that the Yamnaya had horses, in order to avoid an
  implication the sentence never carried.
- It sits awkwardly against the essay's own line 86, which offers the Yamnaya as possibly "among
  the first to ride the horse." A reader who meets that on one page and "widespread horse-based
  mobility came later" on the next has to reconcile them unaided.
- Markup and style are clean: no delimiters, no em dashes, no new parentheticals. Length grows by a
  sentence, which is minor. The prose is not the problem; the proposition is.

## Strongest case for the finding, and my answer

The strongest case: Librado et al. 2021 is the most-cited paper in the field, published in Nature,
and it went out of its way to name and reject "the commonly held association between horseback
riding and the massive expansion of Yamnaya steppe pastoralists into Europe around 3000 bc." Line
126 dates the migration to "around 3000 BC" and puts horses first in the list of what the migrants
were equipped with. A general reader almost certainly walks away picturing riders. Popular coverage
of the Yamnaya reliably produces headlines like "Thousands of horsemen may have swept into Bronze
Age Europe," which is the misconception, and the essay does nothing at line 126 to block it. So
even if the sentence is defensible word by word, it feeds a reading that a major Nature paper
rejects, and a one-word change would cost the essay nothing.

My answer, in three parts. First, the essay does block that reading, at line 86, in the sentence
that introduces the horse: it says the riding reading is disputed and that the mobility claim rests
on the wheel. A reader arriving at line 126 has been told. Second, the Nature sentence rejects
riding *as the driver of the expansion and of the spread of Indo-European*, which is a causal claim
line 126 does not make; treating "equipped with horses" as equivalent to it is the finding's own
inference, not the source's. Third, and decisively for the grading, the rejection is no longer the
field's settled position. It was rebutted in detail two months ago in Science Advances by the
three archaeologists whose data is at issue, who hold that the CWC argument rests on four horses
from one atypical site and that "strong conclusions about this topic cannot be supported." A
finding whose entire force comes from treating a contested rejection as established cannot survive
that. If anything, the current literature makes the *original* sentence the safer of the two texts
on offer.
