# A7 — verdict: partly — grade: nitpick

Essay line 130 (English), line 96 (German). Phase 1 graded this a nitpick at medium confidence.
I agree it is a nitpick, I agree a one-phrase edit is worth making, and I reject two of the three
arguments Phase 1 used to get there, including its flat statement of "what is actually true".

## Why

**Question 1: is the sentence, as written, wrong?** No. It is indeterminate, and both of its
readings land on a published scholarly position.

The sentence reads:

> By the first millennium BC, a recognizable Proto-Germanic language had emerged in southern
> Scandinavia and along the North Sea and Baltic coasts. These Germanic-speaking peoples (known to
> the Romans as ~Germani~) developed a culture adapted to the forests and coastlines of northern
> Europe.

Read as "at some point within the first millennium BC", it is exactly the dominant handbook
position (Proto-Germanic from c. 500 BC). Read as "by the time of the first millennium BC", that
is c. 1000 BC, it matches the older and still-published periodization in which the Proto-Germanic
period runs from the Germanic separation onward: Lehmann's *A Grammar of Proto-Germanic* dates
PGmc "from approximately 2500 B.C. to the beginning of our era", and Euler and Badenheuer devote a
book to reconstructing the Germanic of the Bronze and Iron Ages, the stage between "Western
Indo-European (c. 2000 BC) and Urgermanic (c. 100 BC)". German Wikipedia's summary of that
tradition has Urgermanisch developing "möglicherweise im 2. Jahrtausend v. Chr."

The disagreement here is mostly terminological, about where Pre-Germanic stops and Proto-Germanic
starts, not about what anyone thinks happened. An essay for app users is not obliged to pick a
side, and this one does not misinform on either reading. The second half of the same sentence
("known to the Romans as ~Germani~") and the next section (9 AD, Teutoburg) anchor the reader at
the late end of the window anyway.

**Phase 1's two supporting arguments do not hold.**

*The German does not "resolve the ambiguity in the wrong direction."* Line 96 reads "Bis zum
ersten Jahrtausend v. Chr." That is the same ambiguity, transported. German "bis zum" plus a
period is read both as "up to the start of" and as "by the time of, and through", and in BC
counting neither reading is forced. The German is no worse in kind than the English, so the claim
that German readers get a flat early date that English readers only permit is not supported. If
the English changes, the German should change with it, but the German is not an aggravating
factor.

*The internal-arithmetic argument is weak.* Line 143's "over some two millennia" from a 2500 to
2000 BC separation gives a range with a five-hundred-year mouth at each end, which the phrase
"some two millennia" widens further. It is consistent with 500 BC and it is consistent with 1000
BC minus a bit of rounding. It does not adjudicate line 130.

**What survives.** One thing, and it is small: the surrounding paragraph gives hard numbers on
either side (3000 BC, 2500 to 2000 BC, then 9 AD), so a reader is primed to take "By the first
millennium BC" as another number rather than as a vague band. Naming the second half costs one
phrase, removes the priming, and puts both readings of "by" inside the range every handbook
accepts. Worth doing. Nobody is misled today, so it stays a nitpick and does not rise to
needs-hedging.

**Question 3: Phase 1's replacement.** Factually defensible, markup-safe, house-style clean, but
it makes two unnecessary changes and I would revise it. See below.

## What is actually true

The dominant modern position, Ringe and the Anglophone handbooks, is that Proto-Germanic proper
belongs to the last centuries BC and the first two AD, no earlier than about 500 BC, because the
First Sound Shift cannot be dated earlier than that: the Celtic and Scythian loanwords that
underwent it were borrowed only in the Iron Age. German Wikipedia states the constraint the same
way, "frühestens um 500 v. Chr. stattgefunden haben kann, wahrscheinlich jedoch erst danach."

Phase 1's summary of that is right. Its next sentence is not:

> Nothing recognizable as Proto-Germanic is reconstructed for 1000 BC.

That is false as stated. Lehmann's grammar dates the Proto-Germanic period from c. 2500 BC. Euler
and Badenheuer's book is precisely a reconstruction of the Germanic language of the late Bronze
Age, and they call that stage Protogermanisch (Frühurgermanisch in the second edition). Iversen
and Kroonen argue the Indo-European dialect that became Proto-Germanic was already developing in
situ in southern Scandinavia from the third millennium BC, picking up its non-Indo-European
agricultural vocabulary there. What is true is narrower: under the convention that reserves
"Proto-Germanic" for the post-Grimm common ancestor, the 1000 BC stage is called Pre-Germanic
instead. Phase 1 presented one terminological convention as the state of the evidence.

The honest version: the phase everyone agrees to call Proto-Germanic without qualification sits in
the second half of the first millennium BC and the first two centuries AD. Some scholars extend
the label backward over the pre-shift stage; none extend the essay's word "recognizable" backward,
since what makes Germanic recognizable is the shift.

## Phase 1's replacement prose

> In the second half of the first millennium BC, a recognizable Proto-Germanic language emerged in
> southern Scandinavia and along the North Sea and Baltic coasts.

No new error, no markup problem (the sentence carries no markers; the `~Germani~` pair and the
closing `The Battle of the Teutoburg Forest` heading are later on line 130 and must be left
untouched). Two objections, neither fatal:

1. It changes "had emerged" to "emerged". The past perfect was doing work: the following sentence
   moves to the Romans, and "had emerged" is what makes that a consequence rather than a jump.
2. "In the second half ... emerged" commits flatly to one dating convention. Under Lehmann's it is
   wrong, since he has PGmc emerging two millennia earlier. Keeping "By" makes the sentence true
   under both conventions, because "by 500 BC" and "by 1 BC" are both defensible.

## Revised replacement prose

English, line 130, first sentence only:

> By the second half of the first millennium BC, a recognizable Proto-Germanic language had emerged
> in southern Scandinavia and along the North Sea and Baltic coasts.

This keeps the original's shape and its verb, adds four words, and makes the earlier reading of
"by" land on 500 BC, which is Ringe's earliest bound, rather than on 1000 BC. Both readings are
now inside the accepted window, so the ambiguity stops mattering. No markup is touched.

German counterpart, `docs/verb_history_de.txt` line 96, first sentence: Phase 4 owns the wording. The
requirement is that a bare "Bis zum ersten Jahrtausend v. Chr." not survive, because in BC counting
it can be read as "up to 1000 v. Chr."; the German must name the second half of the millennium
explicitly, and should keep the German pluperfect ("war ... entstanden") that line 96 already has.
The rest of line 96, including `~Germani~` and the trailing `Die Schlacht im Teutoburger Wald`
heading, stays as is.

## Strongest case for the finding, and my answer

The strongest case is one Phase 1 did not make, and it targets a single word. The essay says
*recognizable* Proto-Germanic. What makes Germanic recognizable is Grimm's Law, and every
authority, including the ones I used to defend the early reading, puts that no earlier than about
500 BC. Euler's Bronze Age stage is titled "vor der Ersten Lautverschiebung", before the First
Sound Shift, which is to say by construction not yet recognizable; Lehmann's 2500 BC is a
period-start convention that swallows the pre-shift stage rather than a claim that anything sounded
Germanic then. So on the essay's own adjective, the early reading is unsupported even by the
scholars I cited against Phase 1, and if a reader takes "By the first millennium BC" as "by 1000
BC" the essay has told them something no handbook holds.

My answer, in two parts. First, "recognizable" in a popular essay reads as "identifiable as the
ancestor of the Germanic languages, distinct from its neighbors", not as "post-Grimm", and the
early-dating scholars do claim that much for 1000 BC: Euler's own label for the stage is
Protogermanisch. Second and more decisively, the sentence does not say 1000 BC. It says "by the
first millennium BC", a phrase whose likelier reading in ordinary English prose is "at some point
within", whose window is 999 years long, and whose bulk is mainstream-correct, in a paragraph that
immediately continues to the Romans. The gap between what a reader takes away and the truth is
close to zero. That is the definition of a nitpick, which is where Phase 1 filed it and where I am
leaving it. It is not zero, which is why I am not calling it refuted.
