# B12 · verdict: partly · grade: nitpick

Phase 1 graded this factual-error at high confidence. The underlying linguistics in its
"What is actually true" section is correct and I confirm it. But the grade does not survive
contact with the paragraph, and the one leg of the sentence that genuinely deserves a rewrite
is not the leg Phase 1 argues about, and is not fixed by Phase 1's replacement.

## Why

The sentence at line 133 reads:

> This had profound linguistic consequences: the Germanic languages developed free from the
> Romanization that transformed Gaulish into French, Iberian languages into Spanish and
> Portuguese, and Dacian into Romanian.

Phase 1's case is that "transformed X into Y" asserts descent, that all three legs assert it
falsely, and that in an essay whose subject is genetic descent the reader will build a false
family tree. Three things cut against grading that as a factual error.

**The grammatical agent is Romanization, not time.** A descent claim does not name an external
imperial process as the thing that did the transforming. "Latin evolved into French" is a
descent sentence. "The Romanization that transformed Gaulish into French" attributes the
outcome to the imposition of Latin, which is the correct cause of the correct event: the
language of Gaul in 50 BC was Gaulish and the language of Gaul in 900 AD was Old French, and
Romanization is what made the difference. That is a compressed description of a language shift,
not a genealogical assertion. The essay states genuine descent elsewhere with different syntax
("the branch that would become Germanic", "direct inheritances from Proto-Indo-European",
"As Proto-Indo-European evolved into Proto-Germanic"), and it never places French, Spanish,
Portuguese, or Romanian on a tree with Gaulish or Dacian.

**The next sentence carries the qualification.** Line 135, the immediately following paragraph:
"Had the Romans conquered Germania, the Germanic peoples might have adopted Vulgar Latin, as
did so many others within the Empire." That names the mechanism correctly, names it as
adoption, and applies it to precisely the peoples just listed. Phase 1 sees this and calls it
a point that "cuts both ways." It does not cut both ways evenly. A reader would have to stop
reading mid-section to acquire the false tree. The essay does not merely "know better two lines
later"; it tells the reader two lines later, in the same section, unprompted.

**Phase 1's flattening argument proves less than it claims.** "One error stated three times" is
rhetorically clean, but if the shared formulation is loose-but-causally-correct rather than
false, then repeating it three times repeats a looseness, not an error. The multiplication does
not upgrade the grade.

What does survive is smaller and different. **"Iberian languages" is the one leg that is
genuinely defective**, and for a reason Phase 1 raises only in passing and then does not fix.
Two problems. First, "the Iberian languages" in ordinary modern usage denotes Ibero-Romance,
Spanish and Portuguese and Catalan and Galician, so the phrase as written parses as
"Romanization transformed Spanish and Portuguese into Spanish and Portuguese"; and "Iberian"
on its own is also the name of one specific non-Indo-European pre-Roman language of the eastern
coast, so the phrase is ambiguous three ways. Second, Basque. Britannica calls Basque "the only
remnant of the languages spoken in southwestern Europe before the region was Romanized," which
means the plural "Iberian languages" sweeps in the single most famous counterexample to its own
claim. Gaulish and Dacian were in fact wholly displaced; the languages of Iberia were not.

That is a real blemish, worth a cheap fix, and it misleads essentially nobody who is reading
this essay for its account of German. Nitpick.

## What is actually true

Phase 1's account is correct and I reached it independently. French, Spanish, Portuguese, and
Romanian all descend from spoken Latin; Britannica's Romance-languages entry has them "all
derived from Vulgar Latin within historical times and forming a subgroup of the Italic branch."
Gaulish was supplanted by Vulgar Latin by roughly the end of the fifth century and survives in
French only as a substrate lexicon; the Gallo-Romance languages are derived from forms of
Vulgar Latin spoken by the descendants of Romanised Gaulish-speaking peoples, that is, from
Latin. The Romanian substratum is reckoned at roughly 90 to 140 words, and the identity of the
substrate language is itself disputed among Dacian, Thraco-Dacian, and Illyrian, with around 160
of the substratum words having Albanian cognates.

Two corrections to Phase 1's numbers, neither material to the verdict. Phase 1 gives 70 to 200
Gaulish words in French; the commonly cited range runs higher, "between some 200 and 400 words
of Gaulish origin appear to have survived in modern French or other Gallo-Romance dialects."
And Phase 1 mentions Basque as "a further reason not to write of Iberian languages turning into
Spanish and Portuguese" but does not carry that reason into its fix.

## Phase 1's replacement prose

> This had profound linguistic consequences: the Germanic languages developed free from the
> Romanization that replaced Gaulish, the languages of Iberia, and Dacian with the Latin that
> became French, Spanish, Portuguese, and Romanian.

Content: correct. Markup: safe, no delimiters involved, no asterisks, and the sentence sits
mid-paragraph so no heading-spacing question arises. House style: no em dashes, no new
parentheticals. Length: 36 words against the original 34.

Two objections, neither fatal.

1. **It breaks the pairing.** The original mapped three sources onto three outcomes, with
   Iberia's two named side by side, so the reader could zip the lists. The replacement asks the
   reader to map a list of three onto a list of four with no cue about which goes with which.
   Prose regression, not error.
2. **It preserves the defect that actually needed fixing.** "The languages of Iberia" were not
   all replaced by Latin. Phase 1 identified Basque as a problem and then wrote a fix that still
   asserts the thing Basque contradicts.

## Revised replacement prose

English, replacing the final sentence of line 133:

> This had profound linguistic consequences: the Germanic languages developed free from the
> Romanization that carried the Latin of French, Spanish, Portuguese, and Romanian into Gaul,
> Iberia, and Dacia at the expense of the languages already spoken there.

33 words after the colon against the original's 34. Naming regions rather than language names
dissolves the "Iberian languages" ambiguity entirely; "at the expense of the languages already
spoken there" states displacement without asserting that every one of them died, which keeps
Basque from being a counterexample; and "the Latin of French, Spanish, Portuguese, and Romanian"
puts Latin where the descent actually starts. No markup characters, no em dash, no parenthetical.

If Josh prefers to stay closer to Phase 1's shape, this is also sound and one word shorter than
Phase 1's:

> This had profound linguistic consequences: the Germanic languages developed free from the
> Romanization that replaced the languages of Gaul, Iberia, and Dacia with the Latin that became
> French, Spanish, Portuguese, and Romanian.

**German counterpart**, `docs/verb_history_de.txt` line 99, final sentence, for Phase 4. The
German mirrors the English exactly, with the same flat "die Gallisch zu Französisch ...
transformierte" construction, so there is no English-hedged / German-flat asymmetry here and no
extra German-only defect. What the German needs is the same structural change, not a word swap:
the relative clause hanging off "Romanisierung" has to stop taking language-to-language pairs as
its object and instead make Latin the thing that arrived and the local languages the thing that
gave way. The phrase "iberische Sprachen" should go, replaced by the region ("Iberien"),
alongside "Gallien" and "Dakien", for the same ambiguity reason as in English. "Romanisierung"
stays as the agent. Phase 4 writes the actual sentence.

## Strongest case for the finding, and my answer

The strongest case is contextual and Phase 1 states it well. This essay has spent four sections
teaching the reader a specific reading protocol: when it says X became Y, it means Y descends
from Y's ancestor X. It says PIE became Germanic, it says the perfect became the preterite, it
says vowel alternations are direct inheritances across five millennia. A reader trained on that
protocol for two thousand words arrives at line 133, meets "transformed Gaulish into French,"
applies the protocol, and walks away with a false family tree, produced by the one text in the
app whose whole job is family trees. The looseness would be harmless in a general history and is
not harmless here, because here the reader has been taught to take it literally.

My answer, in three parts. First, the protocol the essay taught does not have "Romanization" in
it. Every descent sentence in the essay has a language or a stage as its subject and no external
agent; this sentence has an imperial process as its subject, which is a different sentence shape
signalling a different kind of event. Second, the reader who applies the protocol wrongly has it
corrected before the section ends, by a sentence that names Vulgar Latin adoption explicitly and
attributes it to "so many others within the Empire." Third, nothing downstream in the essay
builds on the false reading; no later passage treats French as Celtic or Romanian as Thracian,
so the misreading has nowhere to go and nothing to reinforce it.

The residue I cannot fully answer: a reader skimming this section for the Teutoburg point alone
may take the one sentence and stop, and in isolation the sentence is imprecise. That residue is
exactly why my verdict is "partly" and not "refuted," and it is nitpick-sized, not
factual-error-sized. Phase 1 itself wrote that a downgrade to nitpick is defensible and that it
would not fight one. I am taking it up on that.

rested mine on Britannica and Wikipedia, because the one peer-reviewed item I went after was
paywalled. Both of us are on tertiary sources. That would be a serious exposure if the question
were contested, but it is not: that the Romance languages descend from Latin and that Gaulish
and Dacian are substrates is not a live question in any handbook, and neither Phase 1's account
nor mine turns on a disputed reading. Where the sources genuinely disagree, on the identity of
the Romanian substrate, both of us report the disagreement rather than resolving it. The part of
this verdict that a handbook would settle differently is not the linguistics; it is the judgment
about how a reader parses "the Romanization that transformed," and no handbook settles that.
