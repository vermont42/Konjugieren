# B7 · verdict: refuted · grade: none

## Why

The essay's sentence is:

> Three Roman legions under the command of Publius Quinctilius Varus (perhaps 20,000 soldiers) were ambushed and annihilated by an alliance of Germanic tribes led by Arminius (Hermann), a Romanized chieftain of the Cherusci.

Phase 1 concedes in its own "Why" that the sentence "asserts nothing explicitly" and grades the
finding a nitpick. That concession is the whole case. `X (Y)` after a personal name is an
alternate-name gloss, and the alternate name is real: Germans have called this man Hermann for
about five hundred years, the monument above Detmold is the Hermannsdenkmal, and German popular
writing still says Hermann der Cherusker. Nothing in the sentence claims Hermann is his Germanic
name, his earlier name, or the source of the Latin one. The false proposition Phase 1 is guarding
against, that Arminius is a Latinization of Hermann, is a proposition the essay never states.

Two further things cut against the finding.

First, the essay establishes its own convention for a parenthesized name, and it establishes it in
the same article, eleven lines later at line 142: "Wōðanaz (later Wōden (Old English) and Óðinn
(Old Norse)), Þunaraz (Thunor and Þórr), Tīwaz (Tiw and Týr)". In every one of those, the head is
the older form and the parenthesis holds the later reflexes. Applied to line 131 that convention
delivers precisely the right chronology: Arminius is the attested first-century name, Hermann the
later one. A reader who has internalized this essay's parenthetical habit is steered toward the
correct reading, not away from it.

Second, the gloss earns its place for this particular audience. This is an app for English speakers
learning German. A reader who meets Hermannsdenkmal, Kleist's Hermannsschlacht, or Hermann der
Cherusker will connect them to this paragraph only if the paragraph gave him the German name.
Phase 1 acknowledges this too.

Against that, the fix costs a scannable two-word gloss, buries the German name at the tail of an
already long sentence, and imports a date-stamped name-history claim into a paragraph whose subject
is the battle and its linguistic consequences. It buys no correction, because there was no
incorrect assertion to correct.

Phase 1's account of the underlying history is substantially right, so this is not a refutation of
its facts. It is a refutation of the claim that those facts convict this sentence.

## What is actually true

Established from my own research, then compared with Phase 1.

Arminius's Germanic name is not recorded. The only name the ancient sources give is the Latin one,
and since he held Roman citizenship and equestrian rank before the revolt, Arminius may simply be a
Roman name he acquired rather than a Latinization of anything Germanic. Where a Germanic source is
proposed, the candidates cluster on the Irmin- / *Ermin- element, "great, universal", including a
compound reconstruction of the *Ermin-mēraz shape. None of this is checkable.

Hermann is a separate word. Its first element is Old High German heri, which the Etymologisches
Wörterbuch des Deutschen dates to the eighth century and glosses "Streitmacht (eines Landes)",
plus man. Old High German did not exist in 9 AD, so the name cannot be his in any form. The
identification of Arminius with Hermann is a sixteenth-century move, associated with Martin Luther
and with Johannes Turmair (Aventinus), and it hardened into reference-work doctrine: the 1911
Encyclopaedia Britannica still opens its entry "ARMINIUS, the Latinized form of the name of
Hermann". Modern treatment has abandoned that equation and reports Hermann and Armin as
back-formations. Current Britannica frames it as an alternate name rather than an etymology
("also called Hermann"; page itself 403s, see Blocked sources).

Comparison with Phase 1's section: correct on every load-bearing point, with three imprecisions,
none of which changes the outcome.

1. "which is itself uncontested only in the weak sense that nobody can check it" is garbled. It is
   trying to render "not uncontested" and ends up saying the opposite of what it means.
2. "the likeliest source is ermunaz, 'great'" promotes one reconstruction to likeliest, and omits
   the live possibility that Arminius is a Roman nomen and not a Latinization at all.
3. The sourcing is Wikipedia only. Benario and Smith are cited at second hand through Wikipedia's
   footnotes rather than read. That is exposed sourcing for a finding, though on this particular
   question the secondary summary happens to be accurate.

## Phase 1's replacement prose

> ...led by Arminius, a Romanized chieftain of the Cherusci whom Germans have called Hermann since
> the sixteenth century.

Not wrong, and not needed. Verdict: unnecessary.

It carries no markup, so it cannot break the Info screen. It adds no em dash and no new
parenthetical, so it satisfies house style. Its history is accurate.

Two smaller objections, offered in case the sentence is touched for some other reason. "whom Germans
have called Hermann since the sixteenth century" reads as unbroken current usage, whereas German
scholarship and much current popular writing have moved back to Arminius; Hermann is the
traditional name, not the default one. And the relative clause now hangs off "Cherusci" and has to
be parsed back to "Arminius", in a sentence that already carries a parenthetical and two passive
verbs.

## Strongest case for the finding, and my answer

The strongest case is not the one Phase 1 makes. It is this: the false reading is not a reader's
private invention, it was published doctrine. The 1911 Britannica states flatly that Arminius is
"the Latinized form of the name of Hermann", and that framing is still in circulation in popular
history writing. A reader does not fill the parenthesis from nothing; he fills it from a folk
etymology that reference works taught for a century. Add the sentence's own "Romanized", which
invites the inference that the Latin name is the imposed one, and the essay's other habits, which
train the reader to care about which form came first (asterisked PIE reconstructions, Wōðanaz
before Wōden), and the ambiguity is being read by exactly the reader most likely to resolve it
wrongly.

My answer. That case shows the parenthesis is compatible with a wrong belief a reader may already
hold. It does not show the essay asserts it or causes it. The test for a factual error is whether
the text misinforms, and a true alternate-name gloss does not become false because some readers
carry a bad etymology to it. The same argument would condemn every "Charlemagne (Karl der Große)"
and "Confucius (Kongzi)" in print. Note also that the essay's parenthetical convention runs older
form first, later form in the parenthesis, which is the correct order here, so the essay's internal
signal points away from the folk reading rather than toward it. And "Romanized" is doing work about
his citizenship, equestrian rank, and auxiliary command, all of which the sentence's own subject
matter supports; it says nothing about the provenance of names. The residue is a hypothetical
misinference about a claim the essay declines to make, which is the definition of the grade
Phase 1 itself assigned, and a nitpick that misleads nobody does not earn a rewrite of the
sentence that carries the essay's only bridge to the Hermannsdenkmal.
