# C1 · verdict: refuted · grade: none

## Why

The essay's sentence at line 136 reads, in full:

> At the time of the battle at Teutoburg, the Germanic peoples lived in small villages and farmsteads scattered through the forests of northern Europe. They practiced mixed agriculture, growing barley, oats, rye, and wheat while raising cattle, pigs, sheep, and horses.

Phase 1 concedes the first two components ("small villages and farmsteads", "scattered") and objects only to the third, the forest matrix, on the ground that "by 9 AD the settled zones were largely open" and that "the forest framing comes from Tacitus rather than from the archaeology."

That is the part that does not survive. The best pollen-based synthesis of European land cover, Roberts et al. 2018, maps a forest-loss index across mid-latitude Europe and draws exactly the opposite regional line for the region Germania occupied. North central Europe is its example of *late* clearance, not early. Forest cover there was still around 60 percent as late as the 550 to 750 CE baseline the paper uses, with the collapse arriving in Carolingian times and after. Zanon et al. 2018 independently put the steep Central European decline after 1500 BP. At 9 AD the north German interior was a majority-wooded region containing cleared farmland, which is what "small villages and farmsteads scattered through the forests" describes.

The cases Phase 1 leans on are real but are the regional exceptions, and Roberts et al. separates them out by name in the same sentence: "coastal regions of northern Europe (e.g. southern Scandinavia) also experienced substantial forest loss during later prehistory." Denmark, the Drenthe Celtic-field sandy soils, and the Weser-estuary salt marsh where Feddersen Wierde sits are all in that coastal and southern-Scandinavian band. Feddersen Wierde in particular is a *wierde*, an artificial dwelling mound on tidal marsh. A marsh has no trees because it is a marsh, not because the Germani had felled them, so it is not evidence about clearance at all.

Two further points on reading the sentence rather than the quotation. First, "scattered through" already carries the clearings: farmsteads scattered through a wooded region are by construction not in the trees. Second, the very next clause tells the reader these people grew four cereals and pastured four kinds of livestock, which no reader can picture happening under a canopy. The paragraph supplies its own open ground.

Finally, the essay is internally consistent on this: line 130 has the Germani "adapted to the forests and coastlines of northern Europe", which pairs woodland and coast exactly as the evidence does.

## What is actually true

At around 9 AD, Germanic settlement north and east of the Rhine took the form of single farmsteads and small villages, dispersed rather than nucleated, each sitting in its own worked field system. The regional matrix, however, differed sharply by sub-region. In north central Europe, the northern German interior included, the majority of woodland was still standing and was not substantially removed until the medieval clearances; pollen-based forest cover there is estimated at roughly 60 percent even five centuries after Teutoburg. In southern Scandinavia, Denmark, and the North Sea coastal fringe, including the terp and wierde marshlands and the sandy Celtic-field zones, clearance had gone much further already in later prehistory, and some of the best-excavated settlements of the period stand on treeless salt marsh.

So Phase 1's settlement-form statement is correct, and its statement that "the settled zones were largely open" by 9 AD is correct only for the coastal and Scandinavian minority of the region, not for Germania generally. Its inference that the forest is a Tacitean literary residue with no archaeological backing is wrong for north central Europe, where the palynology supports the woodland picture rather than undercutting it.

## Phase 1's replacement prose

> At the time of the battle at Teutoburg, the Germanic peoples lived in small villages and farmsteads scattered across northern Europe, in a landscape of cleared fields, pasture, and coastal marsh as much as of forest.

No replacement is needed, and this one would make the line less accurate than the line it replaces. Three defects:

1. **"as much as of forest" is a new unhedged quantitative claim, and it is the wrong way round.** It asserts parity between cleared and wooded land at 9 AD. Roberts et al. puts north central Europe at majority forest for another eight centuries. The fix overcorrects the original into a different overstatement.
2. **"coastal marsh" promotes a narrow band to a defining feature of the whole region.** The wierde and terp marshes are a strip along the North Sea. Listing them beside "cleared fields" and "pasture" as one of the three things the landscape of northern Europe was made of misweights the geography, and it does so on the strength of a single site.
3. **It adds a three-item list to a sentence that is already followed by a four-cereal and four-animal list.** That is a voice problem on top of an accuracy problem, and the added length buys nothing the paragraph did not already say.

The markup is unaffected either way; the line carries no `…`, `~…~`, `$…$`, `‡…‡`, or `^…^` markers, so nothing here is a crash risk.

Because the essay is fine as written, the German counterpart at `docs/verb_history_de.txt` line 102 needs no change either.

## Strongest case for the finding, and my answer

The strongest case: a lay reader may take "in the forests" to mean the farmsteads themselves sat in woodland clearings hacked out of a wildwood, whereas each excavated settlement, Vorbasse, Wijster, Odoorn, Flögeln, sat in an established open field system its occupants had been farming for generations. Under that reading the sentence imports Tacitus's rhetorical Germania, "silvis horrida aut paludibus foeda", into a paragraph that is otherwise reporting archaeology, and Tacitus was writing about a country he had never visited.

My answer is that the sentence does not make that claim and the paragraph forecloses that reading. "Scattered through" places the settlements *within* a wooded region, which is a statement about the region, not about the farmyard, and it is the correct statement about north central Europe at that date on the pollen evidence. The mixed-agriculture clause that immediately follows supplies the cleared ground. And the objection would still leave the essay needing *some* word for the surroundings; "forests" is the one the palynology supports for the interior, where the battle in the essay's own preceding paragraph took place.

The one place the finding lands is that a Danish or Frisian reader could object that their corner was open heath and marsh by then. That is a specialist's regional footnote against a sentence that is already correct at the scale it is written, which is below the threshold even for a nitpick worth changing shipped text over.
