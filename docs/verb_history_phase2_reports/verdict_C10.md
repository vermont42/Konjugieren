# C10 · verdict: refuted · grade: none

Essay line 140, English: "They committed little to writing, though they did develop the runic
alphabet for short inscriptions and magical purposes."

## Why

Phase 1 raises two charges. Neither survives contact with the standard handbooks.

**Charge one, chronology.** Phase 1 says the paragraph places runes at 9 AD and that the script
"postdates the anchor by something like a century and a half." This rests on an equation the
runological literature explicitly refuses to make: that the date of the oldest undisputed
*surviving* inscription is the date the alphabet was *developed*. Düwel's Runenkunde, the standard
German handbook, separates the two in consecutive sentences: the script arose "at the latest in the
course of the first century AD," while the oldest monuments date "from around 200 AD." On the
handbook's own dating, runic writing existed in the century of Teutoburg. Nedoma dates the Meldorf
fibula to "the first decades AD," which is the decade of the battle, and while its runic status is
unresolved he keeps it on the table rather than dismissing it. Since 2021 the archaeology has moved
further in the same direction: the Svingerud/Hole rune-stone fragments have a radiocarbon context of
50 BC to AD 275 in a 2025 Antiquity paper, "the earliest known context for a runestone."

And the essay asserts no date at all. The concessive "though they did develop" reads naturally as
"though they did eventually develop." The section is not the tight 9 AD snapshot Phase 1 treats it
as, either: two paragraphs later the same section glosses Wōðanaz with Óðinn and Old Norse, forms a
thousand years downstream of Arminius. The section is a general characterization of Germanic
culture, and it already signals as much.

**Charge two, magic.** Phase 1 says "magical purposes" is an older strand of runology that modern
handbooks have walked back, and cites Barnes. Barnes's actual point, in the review Phase 1 itself
quotes, is that there is little evidence the *runes themselves* were held to be imbued with magic
power, which is a claim about esoteric character-mysticism, not about whether runic inscriptions
were used for magical ends. The essay makes the second claim, not the first. On the second claim the
handbooks are affirmative. Nedoma: "As far as can be discerned from the inscriptions, the runes
served both profane interpersonal communication and communication with the 'other' world (the
supernatural sphere)." Düwel: a general characterization of the older-futhark inscriptions that
either foregrounds the magical, in some cases cultic, aspect or stresses the profane content of the
messages "is hard to carry out," which is a statement that both are present and neither dominates.
Nedoma glosses one of the four oldest inscriptions, Øvre Stabu raunijaz, as "probably a poetic or
magical weapon name." MacLeod and Mees devote a Boydell monograph to runic amulets and magic
objects. The essay's two-item summary, short inscriptions and magical purposes, is structurally the
same two-item split Nedoma makes.

What is left is a matter of proportion: naming magic as one of two uses gives it more billing than
its share of a corpus dominated by personal names. That is a specialist's quibble, it misleads
nobody about anything checkable, and the handbook framing does not support even a hedge. Grade none.

## What is actually true

The runic script was probably devised at the latest during the first century AD, from a Mediterranean
model whose identity remains unsettled. The oldest undisputed inscriptions are later than that:
the Vimose comb and Øvre Stabu spearhead from the second half of the second century, joined recently
by the Funen knife of about 150 and the Svingerud stone, whose grave context radiocarbon-dates to
50 BC to AD 275. The Meldorf fibula of the first decades AD would be earlier still if its four
graphemes are runic, which is unresolved. Older-futhark inscriptions are short, often a single word,
usually a personal name, and the handbooks state that they served both profane communication and
communication with the supernatural, with the extent of the cultic horizon still openly debated.

Phase 1's "what is actually true" is therefore wrong in its central move. It reports the attestation
date as the development date, which produces the "century and a half" gap on which the whole finding
turns, and it describes the magical reading as a walked-back "minority possibility" when the two
current handbooks state it affirmatively and treat the debate as live. It also predates the Hole and
Funen material.

## Phase 1's replacement prose

> They committed little to writing, and only about a century and a half later developed the runic
> alphabet, used for short inscriptions naming owners and makers and, some argue, for charms.

Wrong, and worse than what it replaces. Three defects.

1. "only about a century and a half later developed the runic alphabet" asserts a development date
   that Düwel contradicts directly. It swaps an undated sentence, which cannot be wrong about a
   date, for a dated one that is.
2. "later" has no antecedent in its own paragraph. The nearest temporal anchor is two paragraphs
   above, so the sentence reads as later than the stone monuments they did not build.
3. "and, some argue, for charms" demotes to a minority view what the standard handbooks assert, and
   it is a new parenthetical aside, which house style excludes.

The markup is clean in both versions, so nothing here risks the Info-screen fatalError.

## Strongest case for the finding, and my answer

The strongest case: the section opens "At the time of the battle at Teutoburg," so the reader is
invited to read every sentence under that heading as a snapshot of 9 AD, and the earliest runic
inscriptions anyone can point to and read really are about 150 years later. A reader could leave
picturing Arminius's warriors carving runes, which the surviving evidence does not support.

My answer, in three parts. First, the section's frame is demonstrably loose on its own terms, since
it reaches forward to Old Norse Óðinn two paragraphs later. Second, the essay states no date, so
nothing false is asserted; the misdating would be the reader's inference, and inferences from
undated concessive clauses are weak ground for a hedging finding. Third and decisively, the fact the
reader would supposedly be misled about is not established. The handbook position is that the script
was devised at the latest in the first century AD, the Meldorf candidate sits in the first decades
AD, and the Svingerud context reaches back to 50 BC. A hedge is warranted when the essay states
flatly something the field contests. Here the essay states nothing flatly, and what Phase 1 wants
inserted instead is the thing the field contests.
