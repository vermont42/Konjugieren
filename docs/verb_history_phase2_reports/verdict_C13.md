# C13 · verdict: refuted · grade: none

## Why

The sentence at line 142 does not float free of a date. It sits in the section headed
`Lifeways of the Germanic Tribes`, whose first words are "At the time of the battle at Teutoburg."
Everything in that section is a statement about the free Germanic peoples of the early first
century AD, and the paragraph immediately above it makes flat period-bound claims in exactly the
same register: "They had no centralized states or cities. They built no stone monuments." The
temple sentence is one more of those.

For that period and that region, "worshipped in sacred groves rather than temples" is the
mainstream archaeological picture, not a Tacitean rhetorical flourish that scholarship has since
overturned. The Leibniz-Zentrum für Archäologie survey of sanctuaries in the Rhine region states
that pre-Roman sanctuaries were open-air sites that acquired architecture beyond a simple enclosure
only at the very end of La Tène or the beginning of the Roman period. Oberdorla, which the finding
offers as a complication at the early end, is an open-air bog sanctuary with hazel-branch
enclosures, sod and wicker altars, and wooden pole idols, and has no permanent roofed ritual
structure at all. It is evidence for the essay's sentence, not against it.

The finding's real weight is carried by Uppåkra, Tissø, Ranheim and Ose, and the finding itself
concedes those are third century and later and "do not bear directly on 9 AD." That concession is
fatal to the proposal. Worse for it, the scholarship on the earliest of those buildings runs the
other way: Sørensen's Gudme monograph dates the first cult building there to about 200 AD, roughly
seventy-five years after the Germania, and argues the dedicated cult building was inspired by
Roman practice encountered along the Rhine in the first and second centuries. On that reading the
appearance of Germanic cult houses presupposes that there were none before, which is the essay's
claim.

That leaves Tacitus's own templum Tanfanae, razed in 14 AD, which is the one genuinely
contemporaneous item. It does not carry the weight put on it, because templum in classical Latin
is first of all a marked-out consecrated space, not a roofed building. Lewis and Short give the
augural marked-out area and "an open, clear, broad space" ahead of the sense "a place dedicated to
some particular deity," and reference works on Germanic religion note the specific consequence:
a consecrated grove, or any other sacred place, was called templum by the Romans. A Marsian
sanctuary levelled to the ground is as easily an enclosed precinct as a building, and no
excavation has ever produced it. A source-critical ambiguity in one word is not a documented
Germanic temple.

One residual quibble, which the finding did not raise and which does not rise to a hedge: cult in
this period was also sited at bogs, lakes and springs, so "groves" is a synecdoche. It is Tacitus's
own synecdoche (lucos ac nemora) and the standard one, and it misleads nobody.

## What is actually true

Sacred groves and other open-air sites were the cult places of the free Germanic peoples in the
early Roman Iron Age, and no roofed cult building is securely attested among them at that date.
Enclosures, sacrificial pits, wooden altars and pole idols are the archaeological signature, as at
Oberdorla in Thuringia, which runs from the sixth century BC into the Migration period without a
roofed ritual structure. Purpose-built Germanic cult houses do appear later, from about 200 AD at
Gudme and in the third century and after at Uppåkra, with Tissø, Ranheim and Ose later still, and
at least one recent monograph reads their emergence as a response to Roman temple practice met
along the Rhine. Tacitus does use the word templum of a Marsian sanctuary destroyed in 14 AD, but
templum denotes a consecrated precinct before it denotes a building, and Romans applied it to
consecrated groves.

So Phase 1's "what is actually true" is half right and half misdirected. Correct: the grove/temple
contrast in Germania 9 is framed against Roman practice, and cult houses exist in the Germanic
world. Misdirected: those cult houses postdate the essay's stated moment by two to eight centuries,
which the finding admits and then argues from anyway, and the templum of Tanfana is a lexical
ambiguity rather than a counterexample.

## Phase 1's replacement prose

> worshipped chiefly in sacred groves, though Tacitus also reports a Germanic temple, and
> archaeology has since found roofed cult houses.

Unnecessary, and as written it would introduce an error the finding's own analysis rules out. The
clause "archaeology has since found roofed cult houses" carries no date. Dropped into a paragraph
whose governing sentence is "At the time of the battle at Teutoburg," it tells the reader that
roofed cult houses belong to the world of 9 AD, which is precisely what the finding says it is not
claiming. "Tacitus also reports a Germanic temple" is likewise flattening: it converts an ambiguous
Latin word, from the same author whose reliability the sentence is supposedly hedging against, into
a reported building. The replacement also doubles the length of a clause already trailing a heavy
four-name parenthesis, and it imports a historiographical caveat into a survey paragraph that
states cattle, kinship, runes and stone monuments flatly. Markup is unaffected either way, since
the sentence carries none.

## Strongest case for the finding, and my answer

The strongest case is the parenthesis. The sentence names Óðinn and Þórr in their Old Norse forms,
and in the Norse world roofed hof buildings are well attested, so a reader could carry "groves
rather than temples" forward into the Viking Age and be wrong. My answer: the Old Norse forms are
supplied as later reflexes of the Proto-Germanic theonyms, a diachronic gloss on the names, and the
section is datelined to Teutoburg in its first clause. Nothing in the sentence invites the reader
to project the cult practice forward, and the essay's later sections move the reader through Old
High German and Middle High German on an explicit timeline.

The second strongest case is the templum of Tanfana, because unlike the Scandinavian buildings it
is contemporaneous and roughly in the right region. My answer is the lexical one above: templum is
a consecrated space first and a building second, the Romans used it of groves, and no
identification of the site has ever been made on the ground. If that word were the only evidence
either way the sentence might deserve a hedge; against the open-air archaeology of the period it is
not enough to make the question contested.

If I could not answer either of those I would have said "partly." I can answer both, so the
sentence stands.
