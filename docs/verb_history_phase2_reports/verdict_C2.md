# C2 · verdict: partly · grade: nitpick

Cluster C (Tacitus country), essay line 136: "They practiced mixed agriculture, growing
barley, oats, rye, and wheat while raising cattle, pigs, sheep, and horses."

## Why

The archaeobotany behind Phase 1's objection is right, and I reached it independently: at the
Teutoburg horizon barley was the dominant cereal of the Germanic north by a wide margin, and rye
was still overwhelmingly a field weed rather than a crop. What does not survive is Phase 1's
reading of the essay's sentence and its proposed fix.

Three things push this down rather than up.

First, the sentence carries no proportional claim. It is a list of what grew in their fields
inside a one-sentence lifeways summary, coordinate with "cattle, pigs, sheep, and horses" in the
same sentence, which is an equally generic list nobody reads as four equal herds. Phase 1's
argument is that an alphabetical list "reads as neutral and therefore as coordinate, so a reader
takes four equal staples." That inference does not hold. A reader takes "they farmed grain and
kept livestock," which is what the paragraph is for. Phase 1 concedes in its own last sentence
that the sentence is not false.

Second, the literature is not unanimous that rye was absent as a crop at 9 AD. Behre 1992, the
standard reference, puts the change of status from weed to crop plant "probably in the course of
the early Iron Age," and the downstream literature summarising him traces the onset of rye
cultivation to the Pre-Roman Iron Age in central Europe. So "growing rye" among the Germani of 9
AD is defensible on the regional edge cases even though it is not the picture at the Teutoburg
site itself. That is exactly the shape of a specialist's quibble.

Third, and this is what moves the verdict off "upheld," Phase 1's replacement is not an
improvement. "and, where it had taken hold, rye" is a comma-fenced aside, which is the
parenthetical construction house style bans in new prose; it lengthens a background sentence by
half in an essay about verbs; and it spends the reader's attention on an archaeobotanical caveat
that the surrounding paragraph never earns. It also silently reorders wheat ahead of oats, which
is if anything backwards for the northwest German coastal record, where oats are the better
attested of the two.

The two-word fix does the whole job. "barley above all" and "some rye" restore the hierarchy
without adding a clause.

## What is actually true

Barley was the staple cereal of the Germanic north through the Iron Age, shifting from naked to
hulled barley around 1 BC/AD. Wheats (hulled emmer and spelt, naked bread wheat) and oats sit
below it as subdominant crops. Oats have independent first-century testimony: Pliny, writing
within two generations of Teutoburg, says flatly that the peoples of Germany sow oats and live on
no other porridge. Archaeobotanically oats are the weaker case of the two, because *Avena* in a
northern assemblage often cannot be separated from wild oat as a weed of barley; Pliny is what
makes oats safe in the list, not the seed counts. Phase 1's "wheat and oats regularly beside it"
is correct but glosses over that determination problem.

Rye is genuinely the outlier. It entered Europe as an arable weed of barley and wheat, with
earliest south Scandinavian occurrences at the end of the Bronze Age, and "led an existence as a
weed for millennia" before becoming a crop of its own. Behre dates the status change to the early
Iron Age and the onset of cultivation to the Pre-Roman Iron Age in central Europe, with the
increase in northern and northeastern Germany falling in the Roman Iron Age. Region by region the
threshold is later than that: in east-central Jutland secure evidence of rye cultivation exists
only from the end of the second century AD, with rye a possible co-dominant crop only from the
fourth; in the late Pre-Roman Iron Age settlements of Schleswig-Holstein rye and emmer occur only
sparsely and are read as weeds in the main cereals, with rye rising only slightly in the late
Roman Imperial period. The recent isotope work puts the shift to intensive, manured rye growing in
northern central Europe at roughly 2,000 years ago and established by the fourth century AD.

So 9 AD sits at the very start of that transition, and locally before it. Rye was in their fields
but mostly not on purpose.

Pliny is also worth noting as a negative check that Phase 1 did not run: the only place he locates
rye is among the Taurini below the Alps, not in Germania, and he calls it the worst grain, good
only for staving off hunger. A first-century Roman writing about grain does not associate rye with
Germans at all.

## Phase 1's replacement prose

> They practiced mixed agriculture, growing barley above all, along with wheat, oats, and, where
> it had taken hold, rye, while raising cattle, pigs, sheep, and horses.

Rejected. No markup is involved, so nothing crashes, and the factual content is accurate. The
problems are stylistic and proportional: the comma-fenced "where it had taken hold" is a new
parenthetical aside, the sentence grows from 23 words to 30 in a paragraph that is scene-setting,
and the wheat-before-oats reordering is unmotivated and mildly against the northwestern record.

## Revised replacement prose

English, essay line 136, second sentence only:

> They practiced mixed agriculture, growing barley above all, along with oats, wheat, and some
> rye, while raising cattle, pigs, sheep, and horses.

"above all" fixes the flattening Phase 1 correctly identified; "some" downgrades rye without
committing the essay to a date for its domestication. No markup, no aside, one word longer than
the original.

German counterpart, `docs/verb_history_de.txt` line 102, currently "bauten Gerste, Hafer, Roggen
und Weizen an." Phase 4 owns the wording. What it needs to carry: a "chiefly/above all" qualifier
attached to Gerste, which will force Gerste out of the alphabetical run to the head of the list,
and a small-quantity qualifier on Roggen. The German list is alphabetical in German as well as in
English, so the reordering is a real change to that line and not a translation artifact. No markup
is present in the sentence in either language.

## Strongest case for the finding, and my answer

The strongest case is regional precision. The essay does not say "the Germanic world in general";
it opens the paragraph "At the time of the battle at Teutoburg," which pins a date of 9 AD, and
Teutoburg is in northwestern Germany. In exactly that time and place the record is not "rye was a
minor crop," it is "rye was not a crop." The Schleswig-Holstein material has rye in the late
Pre-Roman Iron Age only sparsely and interprets it as a weed; Jutland has no secure cultivation
for another two centuries. On that reading the essay asserts cultivation of a plant that the
nearest evidence says was not being cultivated, which is a small false statement rather than a
disproportion, and false statements are not nitpicks.

My answer is that the sentence's subject is wider than its date. It reads "the Germanic peoples
lived in small villages and farmsteads scattered through the forests of northern Europe," and the
crop list belongs to that subject, not to the Cherusci specifically. Across northern Europe so
described, rye cultivation had begun in the Pre-Roman Iron Age in central Europe on Behre's
dating, and rye grains were physically present in northern German and Danish harvests from the end
of the Bronze Age onward, weed or not. "Growing rye" is therefore imprecise at the margin rather
than untrue, and the correction it deserves is one word, not a clause. If the essay had said "rye
was a staple" or had dated the four crops to the Cherusci alone, I would grade this a factual
error. It does neither.
