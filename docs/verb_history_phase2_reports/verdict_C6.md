# C6 · verdict: refuted · grade: none

## Why

Phase 1 makes three charges against essay line 138. Two fail on the essay's own usage, and the third fails on the Latin of the chapter Phase 1 cites.

**Charge one: the emphasis span makes `~comitatus~` look like a Germanic self-designation.** The essay's `~…~` span marks foreign and technical vocabulary, not native names, and it does so consistently. `~Germani~` at line 130 is introduced with the words "known to the Romans as", so the essay has already told the reader in this very stretch of prose that the Latin names here are Roman. `~limes~` at line 133 is a Roman frontier work named by its Roman name. `~kurgans~` is a modern Russian loanword used by archaeologists for burial mounds raised by people who certainly did not call them that. `~ablaut~` later in the essay is a nineteenth-century German coinage applied to Proto-Indo-European. Every member of the set Phase 1 invokes as the comparison class is an outsider's term for the thing. A reader who has absorbed the essay's convention will read `~comitatus~` as exactly what it is, the standard name for the institution, and nothing in the span asserts that the warriors used it. Naming an institution by the Latin term under which every reference work files it is not an error. Britannica and Oxford Reference both carry the entry under "comitatus".

**Charge two: "bound to their lord by oaths of loyalty" misstates Tacitus, who grounds the bond in shame rather than in a sworn oath.** This is the substantive charge, and it is wrong. Phase 1 quotes *Germania* 14's "iam vero infame in omnem vitam ac probrosum superstitem principi suo ex acie recessisse" and stops one sentence short. The next sentence is:

> Illum defendere, tueri, sua quoque fortia facta gloriae eius adsignare praecipuum sacramentum est.

*Sacramentum* is the Roman word for the military oath of allegiance. Tacitus, describing the comitatus bond, reaches for precisely that word. Translators divide on how literally to take it. Kline gives "is recognised in their primary oath of allegiance"; the Loeb gives "this is the gist of their allegiance". But on either reading the oath vocabulary is Tacitus's own, not the essay's invention, and "bound to their lord by oaths of loyalty" is a fair compression of a sentence whose Latin head noun is the word for an oath. Phase 1 asserted the opposite of what its own cited chapter says.

**Charge three: the comitatus is the following of a *princeps* and includes men of modest birth, so it is not simply "the warrior aristocracy".** *Germania* 13 does say the retinue has internal grades and that youths of distinguished birth are assigned a *princeps*'s rank early while others attach themselves to the seasoned men. But the class from which comites are drawn is the fighting elite: 14 has "plerique nobilium adulescentium" seeking out nations at war, and the standard summary is that the volunteers were the more well-to-do warriors. The essay's preceding sentence has already grounded chieftainly authority in "military prowess, generosity, and noble lineage", so the paragraph is not claiming a closed hereditary nobility. This is a shading, well below nitpick.

**On the Gefolgschaft skepticism.** It is real, and it targets two things the essay does not say: the postulated institutional continuity from Tacitus through the Carolingian material, and the legal-historical edifice built on *Germania* 13 and 14 by nineteenth- and twentieth-century German scholarship. The essay asserts no continuity. It describes how people lived around 9 AD, in a paragraph about villages, cattle, and gift-giving. Phase 1 itself concedes that "warrior retinues themselves are not in doubt" and that graded weapon deposits corroborate hierarchically graded war-bands. A hedge demanded for a claim the demander concedes is well attested is not an improvement to the essay.

One observation on the cited German line, outside the finding's claim and offered only because it sits on line 104: "eine ~comitatus~" takes feminine agreement for a Latin fourth-declension masculine. Whether German loan usage there should be recast is a translation-quality question for whoever owns the German pass, not a C6 issue.

## What is actually true

*Comitatus* is Tacitus's Latin word for the following of a Germanic *princeps*, and it is also the term under which the institution is universally catalogued in English-language reference works. It is not a Germanic word, but the essay does not claim it is, and the essay's own convention for `~…~` spans marks exactly this kind of outsider term.

*Germania* 14 does not ground the bond only in shame. It states the shame sanction and then states the positive obligation as *praecipuum sacramentum*, using the vocabulary of the Roman military oath. Phase 1's sentence "Tacitus grounds the tie in the disgrace of outliving one's chief rather than in a sworn oath" is contradicted by the chapter it cites.

The comitatus was a personal war-band of graded rank drawn from the fighting elite, maintained by the leader with food, weapons, horses, and a share of spoils, and reciprocated with service and glory. That is what the essay's sentence says.

What modern scholarship has dismantled is the Gefolgschaft construct as an engine of continuity into medieval lordship, together with the reading of Tacitus's picture as a neutral field report rather than an ethnography with a Roman moral purpose. Neither bears on a sentence that describes a war-band existing at the time of Teutoburg.

## Phase 1's replacement prose

> Around a chieftain gathered what Tacitus calls a ~comitatus~, a band of followers bound to their lord by loyalty and by the disgrace of outliving him.

This is worse than what it replaces, on four counts.

1. It rests on the false premise established above. It removes the oath, which is in Tacitus, and substitutes the disgrace, which is also in Tacitus but is not the whole of what Tacitus says.
2. "bound to their lord by loyalty and by the disgrace of outliving him" does not parse as a description of a bond. One is not bound *by* a disgrace. Disgrace is the sanction for breaking the bond, not the ligature that forms it. The sentence also reads, on a first pass, as though the followers had already outlived their lord.
3. It is self-defeating. The finding's own argument is that Tacitus's account of the bond is "framed in Roman moral terms" and should not be repeated flat. The replacement then takes the single most rhetorical and most moralizing detail in *Germania* 14, lifelong infamy for surviving one's chief, and promotes it to the essay's own unattributed characterization of the institution, while attributing only the word *comitatus* to Tacitus.
4. The inversion "Around a chieftain gathered" does not match the essay's plain declarative voice in this paragraph, and it cuts the sentence's link to the preceding clause about chieftains whose authority derived from prowess, generosity, and lineage.

The markup is balanced and the house style is respected, so the replacement would not crash the Info screen. It would simply make the essay less accurate.

No revised replacement prose is offered, because none is needed. The German at line 104 needs no change on this account either.

## Strongest case for the finding, and my answer

The strongest case has two parts. First, *sacramentum* in *Germania* 14 may be metaphorical, and the Loeb's "the gist of their allegiance" suggests exactly that; if so, "bound by oaths of loyalty" imports a formal Roman legal institution into a passage that only gestures at one. Second, the *Germania* is a rhetorical ethnography by a man who never crossed the Rhine, and the Gefolgschaft literature built on it has been substantially demolished, so any sentence drawing on it deserves attribution rather than assertion.

My answer to the first part: even on the metaphorical reading, the metaphor is Tacitus's, and it is a metaphor *from the oath*. An essay that renders *praecipuum sacramentum* as "oaths of loyalty" is following its source, not inventing against it; and translators of standing render it literally as an oath of allegiance. A claim cannot be an error for tracking the one word in the passage that names the bond.

My answer to the second part: the demolition is of the continuity thesis and of the legal-historical Gefolgschaft, and the essay asserts neither. Phase 1 grants that the war-bands themselves are attested and archaeologically corroborated. A one-sentence lifeways summary in a popular history of the German verb, in a paragraph flanked by cattle-as-currency and gift-exchange, is at exactly the altitude where "a band of followers bound to their lord by oaths of loyalty" is the right amount of detail. Attribution to Tacitus would be a defensible stylistic choice and Josh may take it if he wants it, but its absence misleads no one, and Phase 1's specific proposed attribution buys it at the price of a false premise.
