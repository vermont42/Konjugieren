# C9 · verdict: upheld · grade: factual-error

Essay line 140: "They built no stone monuments." German counterpart, `docs/verb_history_de.txt`
line 106: "Sie errichteten keine Steinmonumente."

## Why

I went in expecting to kill this one. The paragraph is Tacitus country in the most literal sense:
the sentence is a near-paraphrase of *Germania* 27, where the Germani are said to scorn
"monumentorum arduum et operosum honorem," and it sits beside *Germania* 16's denial that they used
quarried stone or tiles. So the obvious defense is that "stone monuments" here silently means
"monumental masonry in the Roman manner," and that the paragraph is anchored at Teutoburg, AD 9,
in the forests between Rhine and Elbe, where the burial record really is urn cremation in flat
cemeteries and nothing else.

That defense fails on two independent grounds, and the second one is the one Phase 1 did not find.

First, the essay's own scope is wider than Rhine-to-Elbe. Ten lines earlier, at line 130, it places
Proto-Germanic "in southern Scandinavia and along the North Sea and Baltic coasts" and says those
peoples "developed a culture adapted to the forests and coastlines of northern Europe." The
"Lifeways" paragraph then describes *those* peoples. Southern Scandinavia in the Pre-Roman and
Roman Iron Age is precisely where stone grave monuments are the normal form: the Ire cemetery on
Gotland alone preserves some 400 visible cairns and circular stone settings and was in continuous
use "from the Roman Iron Age to the late Viking Period." A sentence cannot name southern
Scandinavia as the homeland and then deny, without qualification, the most conspicuous thing its
inhabitants left behind.

Second, and decisively, the narrow continental reading fails too. The Wielbark culture of Eastern
Pomerania and the Lower Vistula, dated to the 1st through mid-5th century AD and "usually treated
as a material manifestation of the Goths, possibly also of the Gepids," raised stone circles,
stelae and stone-covered mounds from the second half of the 1st century onward. Odry preserves ten
intact circles between 15 and 33 metres across, of 16 to 29 upright boulders each, most with one or
two larger monoliths set near the centre, dated by Kostrzewski to the 1st and 2nd centuries AD.
These are Germanic-speaking people, on the continent, building in stone, in the century of
Teutoburg. There is no reading of the essay's sentence narrow enough to survive that and still be
about anything.

Note also that "monument" is not a word I am stretching. The Gotlands Museum scholarly edition
titles its own overview section "The Monuments" and classes an individual picture stone as "an
Early Type monument." Archaeologists apply the word to exactly these objects.

The German is, if anything, worse than the English. "Sie errichteten keine Steinmonumente" uses
*errichten*, to raise or erect, which is the precise verb for what one does to a bautasten or a
boulder circle. Both localizations ship as translated, so the flat claim reaches German readers
flat.

I part company with Phase 1 only on emphasis, not on outcome. Its case rested on Wikipedia for the
two load-bearing facts and on an academia.edu paper for a third, which is thin ground for a
factual-error grade at high confidence; and its counterexample set leans on runestones and Gotland
picture stones from the 4th and 5th centuries, which postdate the paragraph's frame by four hundred
years and are therefore not proper counterexamples to a claim about the age of Teutoburg. Strip
those away and the finding still stands, because the domarringar and the Wielbark circles are
contemporary with the paragraph. So: upheld, at the grade Phase 1 gave it, on partly different
evidence.

## What is actually true

The Germanic peoples built nothing in quarried, mortared or dressed stone. They had no masonry
architecture, no temples, nothing answering to a Roman arch or aqueduct, and Tacitus records both
the absence of building stone and a stated contempt for elaborate tombs.

They nonetheless raised stone grave monuments as a matter of routine, and did so during exactly the
period the paragraph describes. In southern Scandinavia, circular stone settings and cairns are the
characteristic Roman Iron Age burial form, with Gotland's Ire cemetery preserving roughly 400 of
them in continuous use from the Roman Iron Age onward. On the southern Baltic coast, the Gothic
Wielbark culture raised stone circles up to 33 metres in diameter, along with stelae and
stone-covered mounds, from the later 1st century AD. Ship settings belong to a long tradition
running from the Bronze Age into the Viking Age. Carved and shaped stone follows later: Gotland's
picture stones from about AD 400 in Lindqvist's Abschnitt A, and runestones from roughly the same
horizon.

Phase 1's account of the truth is materially correct. Two small overreaches: "constantly" is
rhetoric, and treuddar are predominantly a Late Iron Age elite form rather than a Pre-Roman one.
Neither affects the verdict. Phase 1 also missed the strongest single piece of evidence, the
Wielbark circles, which is the one that closes off the Tacitean defense.

## Phase 1's replacement prose

> They built nothing in dressed stone, though standing stones and stone settings marked their graves.

Sound. It is true, it keeps the Rome contrast the original sentence was reaching for, it introduces
no new error, it drops no hedge because the original carried none, it contains no markup and so
cannot unbalance any delimiter, and it does not collide with anything else in the essay. Line 142's
"worshipped in sacred groves rather than temples" sits comfortably beside it. House style is clean:
no em dash, no parenthetical, and the length matches the short declaratives on either side.

Two minor observations, neither disqualifying. "Dressed stone" is a term of art that a general
reader may parse only loosely, and it is technically in tension with the shaped and carved picture
stones of the 5th century, though those fall outside this paragraph's frame. "Stone settings" is
likewise archaeological jargon. A plainer wording would carry the same content, so I offer one
below as a preference rather than a correction.

## Revised replacement prose

Either of these works. Phase 1's stands as written; the second is my preference for a
general-audience app essay.

> They built nothing in dressed stone, though standing stones and stone settings marked their graves.

> They built nothing in cut stone, though standing stones and stone circles marked their graves.

**German counterpart**, `docs/verb_history_de.txt` line 106, replacing "Sie errichteten keine
Steinmonumente." Phase 4 owns the translation. What it needs to carry: the concessive shape of the
English, a term for worked or quarried building stone rather than for stone in general, and grave
markers named with the ordinary German archaeological vocabulary rather than a calque of "stone
settings." The verb *errichten* should move to the concessive half of the sentence, where raising
stones is what actually happened, instead of being negated in the main clause.

## Strongest case for the finding, and my answer

The task asked for the strongest case *for* the finding; since I uphold it, what is owed is the
strongest case *against* my verdict. It is this: the sentence is transparently a compression of
Tacitus, sitting in a paragraph built entirely from *Germania* material (comitatus, sacred groves,
cattle as wealth, human sacrifice), addressed to a reader who is being told what the Germani were
not, by comparison with Rome. On that reading "stone monuments" means temples and arches, the
claim is exactly what Tacitus says at 16 and 27, and objecting that a boulder ring in Götaland is
also technically a stone monument is the kind of literalism that would gut half of any popular
history.

My answer is in two parts. The reading defense would be strong if the counterexamples were only
Scandinavian, because one could argue the paragraph's foreground is continental. But the Wielbark
circles are continental, Germanic, and dated to the 1st and 2nd centuries AD, which removes the
geographic escape hatch entirely. And the essay is not attributing this to Tacitus. It asserts it
flatly in its own voice, in an app whose readership includes people who will visit Skåne or Gotland
or the Kashubian lakes and stand inside a monument the sentence says does not exist. The fix costs
one clause and loses nothing the sentence was actually for.
