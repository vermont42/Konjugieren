# D12 · verdict: refuted · grade: none

Essay line 153 (German counterpart line 119). Phase 1 graded this nitpick at medium confidence.

## Why

The sentence under review is:

> This is the origin of the "-te" ending in German preterites (machte, sagte, spielte) and the "-ed" ending in English ($mAde$, $saId$, played).

Phase 1's objection is orthographic: only *played* is spelled with the letters `e` + `d`, so two of the three examples do not display the ending the sentence names them for. That observation about spelling is correct. It does not make the sentence wrong, for three reasons.

**First, the sentence asserts an origin, not a spelling.** Its subject is the Proto-Germanic dental suffix named in the preceding sentence, and its predicate is that this suffix is where the English past-tense ending comes from. Phase 1 concedes that "the sentence's actual assertion ... is true of all three." A sentence whose assertion is true of all its examples is not in error.

**Second, all three examples do carry the ending, at the level the sentence is operating on.** "The -ed ending" is the conventional name of the English weak-preterite suffix, whose three allomorphs are /d/, /t/ and /ɪd/. *Played* is /pleɪd/ and *made* is /meɪd/: the two words end in the identical segment, the /d/ allomorph of that suffix, and differ only in whether English spelling writes it as `ed` or lets a preceding silent `e` absorb it. *Said* /sɛd/ likewise ends in the suffixal /d/. The dental in *made* and *said* is not a stem consonant that happens to look like the suffix; it descends from the Old English preterite endings `-ode` and `-de`, with the stem consonant (`c` of *macode*, `g` of *sægde*) lost or absorbed and the suffixal dental intact. So the examples exemplify exactly the thing the sentence says they exemplify, and only the digraph is missing.

**Third, the essay's own markup already tells the reader that these two are not plain regular forms.** `$mAde$` and `$saId$` redden a letter apiece, which in this essay means "irregular", while `played` is unmarked. A reader is thereby told, in the same breath, that *made* and *said* are not the tidy case. Phase 1 reads that markup as the essay "half-knowing" the problem. I read it as the essay handling the distinction it is accused of eliding. (Whether `A` is the best letter to redden in *made*, given that the departure from a hypothetical regular *maked* is the lost `k` rather than the vowel, is a separate question about a claim nobody reported, and I leave it alone.)

What remains is the asymmetry Phase 1 noticed: the German trio satisfies its label letter for letter and the English trio does not. That asymmetry buys something real, and Phase 1 says so. *Machen/make*, *sagen/say* and *spielen/play* are cognate pairs, so the parenthetical pair demonstrates that the same three verbs took the same dental preterite on both sides of the North Sea. Trading that for orthographic tidiness would be a bad trade, and Phase 1 agrees, which is why its own fix keeps the examples.

So the sentence misleads nobody, asserts nothing false, and flags its own exceptions. That is below nitpick. No change.

## What is actually true

Phase 1's "What is actually true" section is factually accurate line by line and misleading in emphasis.

Accurate: *made*, *said* and *played* all descend from the Germanic dental preterite. *Made* goes back to Old English *macode* (preterite of *macian*, weak class 2), through Middle English *makede*, *maked*, *makde* to *made*. *Said* goes back to Old English *sægde* / *segde* (preterite of *secgan*, weak class 3, a class that by definition has no vowel between root and dental suffix), through Middle English *seide*. Both are listed among the irregular verbs in modern reference works.

Misleading in emphasis: Phase 1 writes that *made* and *said* "exemplify the dental suffix ... rather than the spelling the sentence names," as though the spelling were the sentence's subject. The sentence's subject is the origin of the ending; the spelling is how the ending is named, and naming the English past suffix "the -ed ending" is standard usage that no linguist would gloss as a claim about how any particular verb is spelled. Phase 1 also frames the contraction as loss of the medial vowel, which is right for *macode* > *makde*, but for *said* the class-3 preterite never had a medial vowel to lose: *secgan* belongs to the small class (*habban*, *libban*, *secgan*, *hycgan*) that attaches the dental directly to the root. Neither point changes the outcome, but the second means the tidy "both are syncopated the same way" story is not quite the story.

## Phase 1's replacement prose

> This is the origin of the "-te" ending in German preterites (machte, sagte, spielte) and of the English dental preterite, plain in played and worn down in $mAde$ and $saId$.

Do not apply it. Setting aside that no change is needed, this introduces a new inaccuracy and loses something.

**"Worn down" attaches to the wrong element.** What wore down in *made* and *said* is the stem, plus in *made* the linking vowel: *macode* lost its `k` and its medial vowel, *sægde* lost its `g` into the preceding diphthong. The dental suffix itself did not wear down at all. It is fully present and fully audible, the same /d/ that ends *played*. So the replacement tells the reader that the suffix eroded in exactly the two words where it survived intact, which is a worse error than the one it was written to fix.

**It drops the "-ed" label.** For an audience of English speakers learning German, "the -ed ending" is the one handle they already own for this suffix, and it is what makes the German "-te" comparison land. "The English dental preterite" is a term of art that the essay would be introducing where it previously offered recognition.

**Markup and house style are fine.** `$mAde$` and `$saId$` stay balanced, no em dashes, no new parentheticals. Length is comparable. The defect is in the linguistics, not the mechanics.

## Strongest case for the finding, and my answer

The strongest case is the parallelism argument. The sentence sets up a template with the German half, and the German half honors it perfectly: "-te" ending, then *machte*, *sagte*, *spielte*, three words each visibly ending in `te`. Having trained the reader on that pattern in the first half of the sentence, the essay then supplies "-ed" and three words of which only one visibly ends in `ed`. A reader running the template will trip. Worse, an English-learning or ESL reader could take the sentence as saying *made* and *said* are regular `-ed` verbs, which every classroom in the world teaches them are irregular. The essay is a teaching text on an Info screen, not a monograph, and teaching texts owe their readers a working template.

My answer has three parts. The markup is the first: the essay does not present the three as equivalent, it reddens two of them, and red means irregular throughout this essay, so the reader who trips is immediately caught. The second is that the trip is orthographic only, and the thing the reader would carry away from stumbling is that *made* and *said* are irregular in some way, which is true and which the essay wanted them to know. Nothing false survives the stumble. The third is that the sentence is doing history, and in history *made*, *said* and *played* are the same object: three cognate-paired weak preterites whose final /d/ is the same Proto-Germanic suffix. Charging the sentence with picking bad examples of the ending requires reading "the -ed ending" as a spelling rather than as the ordinary English name for a suffix, and the sentence gives no sign of inviting that reading.

If Josh nonetheless wants the template to hold, the minimal honest touch is to say the ending is written out in *played* and hides behind the spelling of *made* and *said*. That is a stylistic preference, not a correction, and it costs a clause to buy nothing a reader needed. I am not proposing it as a fix.
