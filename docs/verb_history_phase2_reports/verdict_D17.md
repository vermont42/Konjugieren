# D17 · verdict: refuted · grade: none

## Why

The finding rests on two objections, and neither survives contact with the sentence as it
actually stands in its paragraph.

The paragraph (line 155) runs: "This innovation was enormously productive: new verbs entering
Germanic languages almost always followed the weak pattern, and many originally strong verbs
eventually became weak. The King James Bible provides one example of this pattern. In that book,
the phrase 'the cock $crEw$' appears. Speakers of Modern English are aware that the conjugation
later became 'crowed'."

**Objection one is a misreading.** Phase 1 says the sentence "makes a claim about what speakers of
Modern English know ... which is very likely false in the plain reading: crew is far more available
to a modern reader as the noun than as the preterite of crow." But the essay does not claim that
speakers recognize *crew* as a preterite. It claims they know the modern conjugation is *crowed*,
which every competent speaker of Modern English does know: asked for the past tense of *crow*,
they produce *crowed*. Phase 1 substituted a different proposition (speakers know the diachrony of
*crew*) and then refuted that one. Even on the more literal diachronic reading, the sentence is a
rhetorical device of the ordinary "as we all know" kind, and the only fact it delivers to a reader
is that Modern English has *crowed*. That fact is true. Nothing false is conveyed, so nobody can
be misinformed.

**Objection two attacks a claim the essay does not make.** "Crew is not simply gone," Phase 1
writes, and therefore "'later became' overstates a replacement that is not quite complete." The
essay never says *crew* is gone or extinct. It says the conjugation "later became 'crowed'", which
describes the standard outcome, and the standard outcome is exactly that. My own lexicographic
check in fact cuts harder against the finding than for it: the Oxford Advanced Learner's
Dictionary, a British-published reference, lists the verb's forms as crow / crows / crowed /
crowed / crowing and does not mention *crew* at all; the American Heritage and Century entries
likewise give no alternative preterite. The residual *crew* that Phase 1 wants credited is a
marginal, chiefly-British, largely biblical-literary fossil that at least two major dictionaries
no longer print.

Phase 1 graded this a nitpick itself, that is, by its own account it misleads nobody. A nitpick
whose two supporting arguments are a misreading and an objection to an unmade claim does not
deserve to reach the edit. Additionally, its proposed fix would *increase* risk: it replaces an
unfalsifiable flourish with a falsifiable and shakier lexicographic assertion (see below).

## What is actually true

*Crow* descends from Old English *crāwan*, a class VII strong verb with preterite *crēow*. In the
general drift of English strong verbs toward the weak (regular) pattern, it went weak: standard
Modern English is *crowed*, in both British and American usage. The old strong preterite *crew*
has not been formally abolished. Merriam-Webster still records it, labelled chiefly British, and
only for the cock-crowing sense; it is otherwise met almost exclusively in the King James
"the cock crew" and its literary echoes. But it is marginal enough that the Oxford Advanced
Learner's Dictionary and the American Heritage Dictionary do not list it among the verb's forms
at all.

So Phase 1's "What is actually true" section is directionally right on the lexicography and wrong
about what follows from it. Where it says "dictionaries still list it as a British past tense," the
truer statement is "some dictionaries still list it; others have dropped it." And its closing line,
"whether ordinary speakers recognize crew as a preterite of crow at all is not something the essay
can assert," is a criticism of a sentence the essay did not write. Note too that the finding's
lexicographic backbone is Wiktionary plus a teaching website; on the question it is trying to
settle, that is thin ground.

## Phase 1's replacement prose

> Modern English writes "crowed" instead, and the old strong preterite survives only in literary use.

The markup is fine and the house style is fine. The content is not an improvement:

1. It adds a new, checkable, and not quite correct claim. "Survives only in literary use" is a
   stronger and narrower assertion than the sources support. Merriam-Webster's label is *chiefly
   British*, a regional label, not *literary*; a British writer using *the cock crew* is not
   necessarily writing literature. The replacement trades an unfalsifiable flourish for a
   falsifiable overstatement, which is the wrong direction of trade in a fact-check.
2. It smuggles *crew*'s survival into an essay that had no need to mention it. The paragraph's
   subject is the productivity of the weak pattern. A parenthetical rescue of the strong form
   works against the illustration it is attached to.
3. "Modern English writes 'crowed' instead" loses the light forward motion of the original and
   reads as a correction of the King James rather than as a continuation of the argument.

If the sentence were ever touched for voice rather than fact, the minimal move would be to drop the
appeal to speakers and let the fact stand alone, for instance "The conjugation later became
'crowed'." But that is a copy-edit, not a fact-check finding, and I do not recommend it as an
output of this pass.

## Strongest case for the finding, and my answer

The strongest case: read strictly, "Speakers of Modern English are aware that the conjugation
later became 'crowed'" asserts awareness of a historical change, not merely knowledge of the
current form. Most speakers have never once considered that *crowed* displaced an older strong
preterite, and this essay is precisely a diachronic essay, so the diachronic reading of "later
became" is the natural one in context. On that reading the sentence asserts something about a
population that is probably false.

My answer: the assertion carries no informational payload that could mislead. Strip the clause and
the reader retains "the conjugation later became 'crowed'", which is exactly what the essay wants
them to retain and is true. The awareness clause is a register marker, addressing the reader as a
fellow English speaker, and popular linguistic writing is built out of such markers. A fact-check
that fires on them will fire on dozens of sentences in any essay of this kind. The test that
matters is whether a reader finishes the sentence holding a false belief, and no reader does.
Meanwhile the finding's second leg, the survival of *crew*, is refuted outright by the fact that
the essay makes no claim about *crew*'s extinction and that two major dictionaries have dropped the
form the finding wants protected.
