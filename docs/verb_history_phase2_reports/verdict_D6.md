# D6 · verdict: upheld · grade: factual-error (replacement needs revision)

## Why

I went looking for a way to kill this one and could not find it. The essay's sentence carries no
hedge at all: "The ~subjunctive and optative moods merged~ into a single Germanic subjunctive."
There is nothing in the surrounding paragraph that softens it, and the paragraph it sits in is a
run of formal-descent statements, not functional ones: the old imperfective *became* the Germanic
present, the old perfect *was repurposed* as a preterite, the aorist *was largely lost*, the augment
*was lost entirely*. In that company "merged" reads as a claim about where the Germanic form came
from, and the bullet list at lines 106 to 107 has already installed the subjunctive and the optative
in the reader's head as two separate PIE categories with two separate jobs. The reader's takeaway is
that the German Konjunktiv descends from both. It descends from one.

The current handbook is flat about this. Fulk (2018) lists among the defining Germanic innovations
the "Reduction of the verb moods of PIE to three," with "the etymological optative assuming the
functions of the subjunctive," and says of Proto-Germanic that it has "no subjunctive mood (since
the Gmc. subjunctive reflects the PIE optative)." That is not a contested question with a minority
view the essay could be resting on, so "needs-hedging" is the wrong grade: there is no hedge that
would rescue "merged," only a rewrite. And it is more than a nitpick, because the false half of the
sentence is a descent claim in an essay whose whole subject is descent, and because the true version
is the more interesting fact: the German Konjunktiv is an optative wearing a subjunctive's name.

I do think the harm is at the mild end of factual-error. The sentence's second clause, that modal
options were reduced, is true on either account, and a reader who retains only "PIE had two moods
here, Germanic has one" has retained something correct. But the retained mechanism is wrong, and it
is wrong in the one direction a Latin-trained reader is already primed to go, since in Latin the
subjunctive genuinely does continue both PIE moods.

## What is actually true

Phase 1's account is correct, with one refinement.

Correct as stated: the Germanic subjunctive is formally the continuation of the PIE optative, and
PIE's subjunctive mood left no mood-level reflex in Germanic. The Proto-Germanic present subjunctive
continues the PIE present optative; the preterite subjunctive continues a perfect or aorist optative.
The surviving optative took over the functional territory of the lost subjunctive, which is exactly
why Germanic grammars of Gothic call the category the optative while grammars of Old High German and
Modern German call the same inherited category the Konjunktiv. That naming split is a terminological
fact about two traditions describing one category, not evidence of two categories.

The refinement, which bears on the replacement prose rather than on the essay: "lost outright" is a
shade strong. PIE subjunctive *forms* are argued to survive in Germanic after reanalysis as
indicatives, most prominently the West Germanic *ƀi-/*ƀeu- stem of "to be" (Old English bēo), which
is analyzed as continuing an aorist subjunctive *bʰuH-e- rather than a j-present. What was lost is
the subjunctive as a *mood category*. Handbook prose ("no subjunctive mood," "reduction of the verb
moods of PIE to three") is careful to put the loss at the level of the category. The essay does not
need the debris of the copula, but its sentence should not assert more than the handbooks do either.

Line 178's summary, "a distinction between indicative, subjunctive, and imperative moods," is
consistent with all of this and with the correction, so nothing downstream has to move.

## Phase 1's replacement prose

> The ~optative survived as the Germanic subjunctive~ and took over the work of the PIE subjunctive,
> which was lost outright, reducing the modal options available to speakers.

Substantively right and markup-safe: one balanced `~…~` pair, no `$…$`, no asterisks, no em dash, no
new parenthetical. Two things I would change.

1. "which was lost outright" overstates in the way described above. The mood was lost; the forms are
   not all gone, and no handbook I found says "outright." The essay already spends "entirely" on the
   augment two lines later, where it is deserved, so the flat word is also slightly repetitive.
2. The sentence stacks a relative clause inside a coordination and then hangs a participle off the
   whole thing, so "reducing the modal options" now attaches to a longer and vaguer antecedent than
   it did in the original. Minor, but the essay's list paragraphs are single-clause and punchy.

There is also a cosmetic point: the paragraph's siblings put the emphasized span on the *name of the
change* ("~collapse of the aspect system~", "~two tenses~", "~augment~", "~Verb classes
proliferated…~"). "The ~optative survived as the Germanic subjunctive~" starts the emphasis one word
in, mid-noun-phrase. Easily fixed by moving the article inside the sentence's shape rather than the
marker's.

## Revised replacement prose

English, for line 148:

> The ~optative became the single Germanic subjunctive~, taking over the jobs of the PIE subjunctive,
> which Germanic did not keep, and reducing the modal options available to speakers.

One balanced `~…~` pair, no other markers, no em dash, no new parenthetical, and the original's
closing clause survives verbatim. "Did not keep" is true at both levels: Germanic kept no subjunctive
mood, and the claim no longer forecloses the reanalyzed remnants in "to be."

German counterpart, `docs/verb_history_de.txt` line 114, currently:

> ~Konjunktiv und Optativ verschmolzen~ zu einem einzigen germanischen Konjunktiv, was die modalen
> Optionen für Sprecher reduzierte.

Phase 4 should note a trap the English does not have. German uses *Konjunktiv* for both the PIE mood
and its Germanic successor, so a literal rendering of the corrected sentence becomes circular, saying
that the Konjunktiv took over the jobs of the Konjunktiv. The German needs the two to be distinguished
explicitly, along the lines of *der indogermanische Konjunktiv* against *der germanische Konjunktiv*,
using the same distinguishing label the essay's earlier German mood list at that section already
establishes. The emphasized span must remain a single balanced `~…~` pair, and the existing trailing
clause "was die modalen Optionen für Sprecher reduzierte" can be carried over unchanged.

## Strongest case for the finding, and my answer

Since my job is to refute, the case I have to answer is the one *against* the finding, and it is this:
"merged" is ordinary loose usage for a functional merger, historical linguists say it all the time
about categories where only one form-set survives, and the essay's own second clause makes clear it
is talking about the speaker's modal options rather than about morphological descent. On that reading
the finding is a specialist's quibble and the grade should be nitpick.

My answer has three parts. First, the immediate context is descent, not function: every other item in
this "Losses from Proto-Indo-European" list names what became what, so the reader has no cue to switch
readings at line 148. Second, the essay itself has just spent lines 105 to 108 teaching the reader
that these are two distinct PIE moods with distinct semantics, which makes the joint-descent reading
the natural one rather than a perverse one. Third, the loose reading is not available to a reader who
has learned Latin or Greek, where "the subjunctive and optative merged" is a true statement of formal
history, and that reader is exactly the reader this essay attracts. A phrase that is harmlessly loose
in one branch is a false analogy in another.

What I will concede, and what keeps me from calling this a severe error: nobody is going to conjugate
a verb wrongly because of it, and the sentence's practical claim about reduced modal options is sound.

## Sources

- R. D. Fulk, *A Comparative Grammar of the Early Germanic Languages* (John Benjamins, 2018), §1.3
  and §1.4 <https://ebin.pub/a-comparative-grammar-of-the-early-germanic-languages-2018030754-2018031862-9789027263124-9789027263131.html>
  > "Reduction of the verb moods of PIE to three" ... "the etymological optative assuming the functions
  > of the subjunctive" ... "no subjunctive mood (since the Gmc. subjunctive reflects the PIE optative)"

  This is the decisive source, and it is a current handbook rather than a tertiary reference. It settles
  both halves: the Germanic subjunctive *is* the PIE optative, and Proto-Germanic has no continuation of
  the PIE subjunctive as a mood.

- Rolf Noyer, "The PIE Verb," Linguistics 051 course materials, University of Pennsylvania
  <https://www.ling.upenn.edu/~rnoyer/courses/51/PIEVerbs.pdf>
  > The PIE optative "survived as such in Greek and Indo-Iranian; it developed into forms called
  > 'subjunctive' in Latin and Germanic," while the PIE subjunctive "survived in Indo-Iranian, Greek and
  > Celtic; it developed into a future in Latin; and disappeared in the other branches."

  Independent confirmation of the branch-by-branch picture, and it makes the Latin contrast explicit,
  which is the contrast that makes the essay's error tempting.

- Wikipedia, "Indo-European copula," for the uncontroversial background that West Germanic has an
