# D7 · verdict: upheld · grade: needs-hedging (confidence high)

## Why

I went looking for a reason to kill this one and could not find it. The sentence at line 150 makes
three assertions, all flat, and every one of them is a live question:

1. that PIE had an augment,
2. that the augment marked past tense at the PIE stage,
3. that Germanic *lost* it, which presupposes Germanic once had it.

Assertion 3 is the one that does the most damage, and it is the one a Germanic handbook denies
outright. Lehmann's *A Grammar of Proto-Germanic* uses the augment's absence as a positive argument
about Germanic's position in the family: the augment is a common innovation of Indo-Iranian, Greek
and Armenian, introduced after the community began breaking up, and Germanic's not having it is
evidence that Germanic left early. On that account Germanic did not lose the augment. It never had
one. The essay's sentence puts a morpheme into the ancestor of German that a mainstream Germanicist
says was never there, in a paragraph headed `Losses from Proto-Indo-European`.

Assertion 2 is shaky even for scholars who do reconstruct *h₁e- for the proto-language. The standard
account of its origin is a deictic particle meaning roughly "then, at that time", which is an adverb
before it is a tense marker; Willi's 2018 monograph, the most recent book-length treatment, derives
it instead from standardized reduplication and argues it was originally a marker of perfectivity, not
of past time and not a narrative connective. So "had marked past tense in PIE" is not merely
unhedged, it is the reading of the PIE stage that the recent literature specifically argues against,
independently of the archaism-versus-innovation question.

Reading the sentence in its paragraph does not rescue it, which is the test I was asked to apply. The
neighbouring items in that list (aspect collapse, two-tense reduction, subjunctive-optative merger)
are uncontested and carry no qualifying language that could spill onto the augment. The essay's own
hedge sits forty-eight lines earlier at line 102, "whether it goes back to Proto-Indo-European at all
is disputed", and it is not a nearby qualification so much as a direct contradiction of line 150. The
same pair exists in German: line 68 carries the hedge, line 116 carries the flat claim. Both ship as
translated.

Grade. I considered downgrading to nitpick and rejected it: a reader is told a specific morpheme
existed in the ancestor language and was lost on the way to German, and a Germanicist handbook says
the opposite about the only half of that a Germanic essay needs. I considered upgrading to
factual-error and rejected that too: the archaism reading is respectable, plenty of teaching grammars
print the augment in PIE paradigms, and under that reading line 150 is simply true. A live question
stated flatly is exactly needs-hedging. Phase 1 graded it right.

One correction to Phase 1's reasoning, which does not change the outcome. Its "Why" says the
optionality of the prefix in the oldest texts is "the point pressed against the archaism reading".
That is one-sided. The same fact is standardly deployed for the archaism reading: if the augment was
a loosely attached, not-yet-grammaticalized particle in the proto-language, dropping it in the
peripheral branches costs nothing, and the eight-independent-losses objection dissolves. Optionality
is evidence that whatever the augment was, it was not yet a mandatory tense marker. It does not by
itself pick a side. Phase 1's bottom line, that the question is open, is unaffected.

## What is actually true

The augment is attested as a verbal prefix in Indo-Iranian, Greek, Armenian and Phrygian, with
Albanian sometimes added, and nowhere else. In the branches that have it, it prefixes to
secondary-ending indicative forms (imperfect, aorist, pluperfect) and marks past time. In the oldest
texts of those branches, Vedic and early Greek, it is optional rather than obligatory.

Whether it is inherited from Proto-Indo-European and lost everywhere else, or is an innovation of
that one contiguous southeastern area, is an open question, and specialists state it as open. Where
scholars do reconstruct a PIE *h₁e-, they generally reconstruct it as a deictic particle "then, at
that time" or, on Willi's account, as an aspectual marker of perfectivity, not as a past-tense
prefix; the tense-marking function is what the prefix acquired in the branches that grammaticalized
it. Germanic-side handbooks tend to the innovation view precisely because the augment's absence is
one of the isoglosses used to argue Germanic separated early.

The negative half is not in doubt: Germanic reflects no augment anywhere, at any stage.

## Phase 1's replacement prose

> The ~augment~ (*e-), the past-tense prefix of a few branches, left no trace at all in Germanic.

Sound. I checked it for the usual failure modes and it survives all of them.

- Markup: one balanced `~…~` pair, no `$`, no `‡`, no `^`, and the single asterisk in `*e-` is
  correctly left unpaired as a PIE reconstruction mark. No crash risk.
- House style: no em dash, and the parenthetical `(*e-)` is carried over from the original rather
  than newly introduced.
- Length and voice: sixteen words against the original's fifteen, same register as the surrounding
  list items.
- No new error. "The past-tense prefix of a few branches" is exactly right about the attested
  branches and takes no position on PIE. "Left no trace at all in Germanic" states the observable
  fact without presupposing prior possession, which is the whole repair.
- No contradiction with the rest of the essay. It echoes line 102's "A few branches also added a
  prefix called the ~augment~" closely enough to read as deliberate, which is the opposite of the
  present situation.

The one thing I would flag and then let stand: the sentence still sits under the subheading
`Losses from Proto-Indo-European`, so the loss framing survives in the heading even though the
sentence no longer asserts it. That heading is out of this finding's scope, the item legitimately
belongs in a list of what Germanic lacks relative to the reconstructed parent, and a reader is not
misinformed by the placement once the sentence itself is neutral. Not worth a larger edit.

## Strongest case for the finding, and my answer

Since my instructions are to state the strongest case against my verdict, and my verdict upholds, the
strongest case *against* upholding is the one I have to answer. It runs: the augment is printed in
PIE verbal paradigms in a great many reference works, including ones students meet first; the essay
is a popular history inside a conjugation app, not a contribution to Indo-European subgrouping; and
line 102 already tells the attentive reader the thing is disputed, so line 150 is shorthand for a
qualification the essay has already made. On that reading D7 is a specialist's quibble and should be
downgraded to nitpick or killed.

My answer has two parts. First, line 102 does not qualify line 150, it contradicts it; an essay that
says "disputed" in one section and "had marked past tense in PIE" in another has not hedged, it has
made a mistake in one of the two places, and the mistake is in the later one. A hedge only functions
as a hedge if the reader can tell which sentence is the essay's position. Second, and this is what
decides it for me, the sentence is not merely repeating a textbook convenience about PIE. It adds
"was lost entirely in Germanic", which is a claim about Germanic specifically, in a German-language
essay, and it is the claim the Germanic-side literature declines to make. Lehmann does not say
Germanic lost the augment; he says Germanic never reflects it and treats that as a reason to believe
Germanic split off early. The essay is asserting, in its own subject area, the side of a live
question that its own subject area's handbooks argue against. That is more than a quibble, and the
fix costs one sentence.
