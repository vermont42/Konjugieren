# E1 · verdict: upheld · grade: nitpick

Essay line 156, German counterpart line 122. I tried to kill this finding and could not. The
omission is real, it is larger than a map correction, and it leaves a tension inside the very
paragraph that contains it. I keep Phase 1's grade rather than raising it, and I endorse its
replacement prose unchanged.

## Why

The sentence under review:

> The Germanic dialects spoken in what is now southern Germany and Switzerland developed into Old
> High German (roughly 750–1050 AD), distinguished from other Germanic languages by the ~High
> German consonant shift~.

Read literally, nothing here is false. Alemannic and Bavarian were spoken in what is now southern
Germany and Switzerland, and they did develop into Old High German. The sentence says nothing about
exclusivity, and it carries no hedge that could misrepresent a consensus, because there is no
contested question here to misrepresent. That is why this is neither a factual error nor a
needs-hedging case.

What it is, is a list that stops one item too early, and the missing item is the largest one. Old
High German is not a latitude, it is participation in the second sound shift: the Old High German
dialect landscape is every area with any share of that shift, with the share thinning steadily from
south to north. That area is Upper German plus Franconian, or in the German teaching tradition three
Sprachlandschaften, Alemannisch, Bairisch and Fränkisch. Franconian is the central band running from
the Moselle and the Rhineland across Hesse to Franconia proper, and it is where most of the surviving
Old High German was actually written: the Tatian at Fulda in East Franconian, Otfrid's Evangelienbuch
at Weissenburg in South Rhine Franconian, Isidor, the Lorsch and Mainz material. An essay that
introduces Old High German by naming only the Alemannic and Bavarian homelands has told the reader
that the language grew somewhere other than where it was written down. That is substantive, not
cartographic, and it is what stops me from dismissing the finding as "no change needed."

Austria is the second omission and the cheaper one to fix, since Bavarian Old High German covers what
is now Upper and Lower Austria and Salzburg as well as Bavaria. Harbert names Austria explicitly
among the areas where the shift went furthest.

Phase 1's internal-tension argument also survives my checking. Three sentences later the essay
writes:

> By contrast, more northerly dialects did not experience certain sound changes associated with the
> shift. For example, in Kölsch, spoken in Cologne, the /t/ in "et", English "it", never shifted to
> /s/, as it did in German "es".

That sentence is coherent only if Cologne sits inside the shift-affected area, which it does. Cologne
lies south of the Benrath line, and Ripuarian shifted the k of machen while leaving the t of et, dat
and wat alone. The paragraph is describing a gradient within High German. But its opening sentence
has already told the reader that High German grew in the south and in Switzerland, so "more northerly
dialects" reads most naturally as dialects outside the picture just drawn. Adding the central band to
the first sentence is what makes the Kölsch example land as the essay plainly intends it.

I decline to upgrade past nitpick. The essay's subject is the verb system, nothing in the strong-verb
or perfect sections depends on the map, the sentence states no falsehood but only an incomplete list,
and the fix is a three-word insertion. The reader gets a picture that is too small, not one that is
false.

One thing about Phase 1 that deserves saying plainly: its evidence base is Wikipedia and nothing else.
That is thin sourcing for a finding. It happens not to matter here, because the handbooks say the same
thing, and I went and got them rather than re-reading its citations.

## What is actually true

Old High German is conventionally dated from roughly 750, the first substantial texts, to roughly
1050, which the essay already has right. It is a cover term rather than a single language. Its dialect
base is Upper German (Alemannic in southwest Germany, Alsace, Switzerland and Vorarlberg; Bavarian in
Bavaria and Austria) plus Franconian or Central German (Middle Franconian along the Moselle and Rhine,
including Trier, Echternach, Luxembourg and Cologne; Rhine Franconian around Lorsch, Worms and Mainz;
South Rhine Franconian at Weissenburg; East Franconian at Fulda, Würzburg and Bamberg). The northern
edge is the Benrath line, which runs from Aachen past Benrath south of Düsseldorf and on to the
northeast, so Cologne falls inside it. In modern terms: southern and central Germany, Austria,
Switzerland, plus Alsace and Luxembourg, with South Tyrol as a later Bavarian extension.

Phase 1's "What is actually true" section matches this on every point I checked: the dates, the Upper
plus Central dialect base, the region list, the Benrath boundary, and Cologne's inclusion. Its one
peripheral assertion I could not confirm from a handbook is that some scholars begin Old High German
at 500 or 600 on the strength of the shift's onset. The framing is common and the shift is indeed
dated to about the middle of the first millennium, but nothing in the finding or the fix rests on it,
and the essay's 750–1050 stays either way.

## Phase 1's replacement prose

> The Germanic dialects spoken in what is now southern and central Germany, Austria, and Switzerland
> developed into Old High German (roughly 750–1050 AD), distinguished from other Germanic languages by
> the ~High German consonant shift~.

Sound. I checked it for the usual failure modes and it survives them:

- Markup: one balanced `~…~` pair, preserved intact, nothing nested, no `$…$` or `‡…‡` touched, no new
  crash surface.
- No hedge dropped, because the original carried none.
- No overcorrection: it does not claim Old High German covered all of Germany, and the consonant-shift
  clause is verbatim.
- It contradicts nothing later in the essay and actively repairs the Kölsch tension.
- House style: no em dash, and the only parenthetical is the one the sentence already had. Five words
  added.

Two imprecisions I considered and decided to leave. "Central Germany" as an English geographic phrase
reaches somewhat further east and north than the Old High German area did in 750, since Old Saxon held
the north German plain; "what is now" absorbs most of that, and the alternative is a dialectological
term the essay has not introduced. And the list still omits Alsace and Luxembourg, which genuinely
belong to the area. I would keep them out: the three-country gloss is the standard orientation for a
general reader, and a five-item list with two exclaves would cost the sentence its shape to buy
accuracy the paragraph does not need.

## Revised replacement prose

None needed. I endorse Phase 1's English verbatim:

> The Germanic dialects spoken in what is now southern and central Germany, Austria, and Switzerland
> developed into Old High German (roughly 750–1050 AD), distinguished from other Germanic languages by
> the ~High German consonant shift~.

For the German counterpart at `docs/verb_history_de.txt` line 122, which currently opens "Die
germanischen Dialekte, die im heutigen Süddeutschland und der Schweiz gesprochen wurden", and which
Phase 4 owns: the region list needs the same two additions, central Germany and Austria, and nothing
else in the sentence changes. One caution, because the obvious rendering is a trap. Modern German
"Mitteldeutschland" is commonly read as Saxony, Thuringia and Saxony-Anhalt, which would leave the
Rhineland and Hesse out again and so fail to repair the Cologne problem that motivates the finding.
The dialectological term that actually covers Cologne, Trier, Mainz and Fulda is westmitteldeutsch; a
neutral geographic phrasing along the lines of "im mittleren und südlichen Deutschland" avoids the
misreading without dragging a technical term into the sentence. The date parenthesis and the
~hochdeutsche Lautverschiebung~ clause are unaffected, and the marker count must stay at one balanced
`~…~` pair.

## Strongest case for the finding, and my answer

The case I was hunting for, against the finding: the sentence states no false thing, and the paragraph
supplies the missing north by itself, since it goes on to speak of "more northerly dialects" and names
Kölsch, so an attentive reader already learns that the High German area reaches Cologne.

My answer is that the paragraph gestures north without ever repairing the definition. "More northerly
dialects" sits inside a discussion of how completely the shift applied, and a reader told that Old
High German is southern Germany plus Switzerland will most naturally take those northerly dialects to
be something outside Old High German rather than a constituent part of it. The self-correction the
counterargument depends on does not actually occur.

The strongest case for the finding, correspondingly: the Central German half is not a fringe. East
Franconian at Fulda gives the Tatian, South Rhine Franconian at Weissenburg gives Otfrid, Rhine
Franconian gives the Ludwigslied. Several of the largest surviving Old High German texts come from
outside the area the essay names. I cannot answer that, which is why the verdict is upheld rather than
refuted. What it does not do is misinform a reader about the verb system, which is why the grade stays
at nitpick.
