# E12 · verdict: refuted · grade: none

Essay line 157 (English), `docs/verb_history_de.txt` line 123 (German).

> Today, German strong verbs must largely be memorized individually, their ablaut patterns, while still systematic, are no longer predictable from the infinitive.

## Why

The essay's sentence is not merely defensible. It is close to a paraphrase of the standard
scholarly summary of the modern German strong-verb system, and the pairing it makes (still
systematic, no longer predictable from the infinitive) is exactly the pairing the literature makes.

Mailhammer's peer-reviewed article on precisely this question states that the loss of the link
between a verb's phonological root shape and its ablaut pattern produced "a complicated array of
inflection classes whose predictability is no longer based on the citation form stored in the
mental lexicon," and then adds that "it is exactly the remnants of the old system that keep the
German strong verbs together as a group." The citation form of a German verb is its infinitive.
The first half of that abstract is the essay's second clause; the second half is the essay's
"while still systematic." Phase 1 cited Mailhammer's book as evidence against the sentence; the
same author's article in *Morphology* is evidence for it, in nearly the essay's words.

Phase 1's grading rationale also rests on a premise that does not hold. It says the two halves of
the sentence "argue with each other." They do not. Systematicity and predictability are different
properties. A system can be organized into coherent recurring series while still requiring the
class of any given lexeme to be stored rather than derived. That is the whole content of
Mailhammer's "however" clause, and it is what the essay says.

Phase 1's positive case is that i plus nasal plus consonant reliably yields a and u, and that ie
reliably yields o. Both generalizations are real, and both are conditional on something the
infinitive does not tell you. See the answer below.

Two smaller notes. Phase 1's evidence is Wikipedia's "coherence of the strong verb system is still
present in modern German" line, which is about coherence, not predictability, and which the essay
already concedes with "while still systematic." And Phase 1's one genuinely unpredictable pair,
*leiden* / *litt* against *meiden* / *mied*, is an argument for the essay's sentence, not against
it: those two infinitives are segmentally identical from the stressed vowel onward.

Out of scope, and I am not reporting it as a finding: the English sentence joins its two clauses
with a comma splice where the German at line 123 uses a colon. That is punctuation, not fact, and
the brief excludes it.

## What is actually true

German strong verbs cannot be identified as strong from the infinitive at all, and among the verbs
that are strong, the ablaut class is not derivable from the shape of the infinitive. It is stored.
Partial regularities survive and are strong enough that an experienced speaker guesses well, which
is what "still systematic" concedes, but they are sub-regularities over a lexically specified
class, not rules that take an infinitive as input.

Three facts settle it.

1. Strength itself is unpredictable. *kaufen* is weak (kaufte), *laufen* is class 7 (lief),
   *saufen* is class 2 (soff). Same rhyme, three outcomes. Likewise *blinken* (blinkte) beside
   *sinken* (sank), and *reisen* (reiste) beside *reiten* (ritt). Any rule keyed to the infinitive
   has to survive this first, and none does.
2. Given strength, identical infinitive rhymes still land in different classes. *graben* / grub
   against *braten* / briet; *laden* / lud against *raten* / riet; *beißen* / biss against
   *heißen* / hieß; *biegen* / bog against *liegen* / lag; and Phase 1's own *meiden* / mied
   against *leiden* / litt. Phase 1's "ie takes o just as reliably" fails on *liegen*, one of the
   most frequent verbs in the language.
3. The class inventory has fragmented past the point where "pattern" and "rule" mean the same
   thing. Grammis (IDS Mannheim) counts about 170 simple strong verbs and notes that "die Hälfte
   aller starken Verben lassen sich den sechs häufigsten Ablautreihen zuordnen, viele Ablautreihen
   (ca. 25) sind nur ein- oder zweimal belegt." Half the class sits outside the six common series,
   scattered across roughly 25 series with one or two members each. "Must largely be memorized
   individually" is a fair description of that distribution.

Phase 1's formulation, "the infinitive constrains the pattern without determining it," is not
false, but it is a specialist's refinement of a claim the essay already qualified, and it omits the
larger fact that the infinitive does not even tell you whether ablaut applies.

## Phase 1's replacement prose

> Today, German strong verbs must largely be memorized individually. Their ablaut patterns remain
> systematic, and the shape of the infinitive often points to the pattern, but it no longer settles
> it.

The markup is clean (there is none in this sentence in either localization), and the replacement
would not crash the Info screen. But it should not be adopted, for three reasons.

- It weakens a true statement into a vaguer one. "Often points to the pattern" is the claim that
  actually needs hedging, since it holds only after you already know the verb is strong, and it
  fails on high-frequency members (*liegen*, *heißen*, *braten*, *raten*, *leiden*).
- It half-contradicts its own first sentence. If the infinitive often points to the pattern, the
  reader is entitled to ask why the verbs must be memorized individually. The original avoids this
  by keeping systematicity and predictability distinct, which is the correct distinction.
- It costs a German retranslation at line 123, where the existing rendering ("Ihre Ablautmuster,
  obwohl noch systematisch, sind nicht mehr aus dem Infinitiv vorhersagbar") is accurate, for a
  change Phase 1 itself grades a nitpick.

## Strongest case for the finding, and my answer

The strongest case is not the Wikipedia line Phase 1 leaned on. It is the German Wikipedia
observation, which I found independently, that with some experience of the language one can
predict the inflections in very many cases, because recognizing an infinitive of the shape
K₁eiK₂en tells you the class and the voicing of K₂ then tells you whether the preterite has ie or
short i. That is a real generalization, it covers a large and frequent set of verbs, and it does
take the infinitive as its input. If it holds, "no longer predictable from the infinitive" is too
strong.

My answer has two parts. First, that rule is stated for a speaker who already knows the verb is
strong. *reisen*, *heizen*, *kreisen*, *reizen* all fit K₁eiK₂en phonologically, and none of them
ablauts. The rule is a description of an existing class, not a predictor over infinitives, and the
essay's clause is about the latter. Second, even within that class the rule is a tendency with a
resident exception: *heißen* has the class 1 shape and the class 7 outcome. So the honest reading
of the German Wikipedia passage is "an experienced speaker guesses well," which is a statement
about learned sub-regularities. That is what "while still systematic" already grants in the essay,
and it is what Mailhammer's "remnants of the old system ... ensure a limited degree of
productivity" grants in the literature. Neither one restores predictability from the infinitive.

The residual case is that a reader might take "no longer predictable" to mean "random," which the
neighboring "while still systematic" is there to prevent. Since the qualification sits inside the
same sentence, no reader reaching the end of it is misinformed. That puts the finding below even
nitpick.

  > "Using a principal parts approach, this paper brings out the drastic changes the system of the

  > of the paradigmatic connection between the phonological root structure of a verb and its ablaut
  > pattern is the key alteration that transformed the strong verbs from a highly homogenous and
  > learner-friendly system to a complicated array of inflection classes whose predictability is no
