## Why

**Question 1: is the sentence as written wrong?** No, and not marginally. The sentence is

> This series of sound changes transformed voiceless stops into fricatives or affricates
> (p → pf or ff; t → ts or s; k → ch)

Its grammatical subject is what the shift did to voiceless stops, and about voiceless stops it is
both accurate and complete. It does not open an enumeration of the shift's phases and abandon it.
Phase 1's own metaphor, that the essay's account "stops halfway", is the claim I reject: a sentence
scoped to one series of consonants is not a truncated sentence about all of them. Phase 1 concedes
"Nothing in this sentence is false" in its second line, and in a fact-check that concession is close
to dispositive. What remains is a request for an additional fact, which is an enrichment proposal
wearing a finding's clothes.

Nor is the reader left with a wrong model. Every arrow in the parenthesis is right, and the three
examples silently do the positional work the arrows leave implicit: ~Pfund~ is the word-initial
affricate outcome, ~Wasser~ and ~machen~ are the post-vocalic fricative outcomes. The paragraph
then spends four further sentences insisting the shift was uneven, with Swiss ~Chuchi~ at the
southern end and Kölsch ~et~ at the northern. A reader closes that paragraph believing the shift
was a bundle of related changes, strongest in the south, thinning northward. That belief is
correct. Adding d > t would make it richer; nothing about it would make it truer.

There is also a scope argument that Phase 1 does not weigh. Line 156's whole job is to say what
made Old High German diverge, in one sentence, inside an essay about the verb system. The
mainstream one-sentence summary of the second sound shift is the Tenuisverschiebung, which is why
Grimm's name for the whole bundle and every encyclopedia lead sentence I checked start there. An
essay that gives the headline of a phonological change it mentions in passing has not erred by
declining the footnotes.

## What is actually true

The High German consonant shift is conventionally described in successive phases. The voiceless
stops p, t and k become long fricatives after a vowel (ff, ss, xx) and affricates word-initially,
after a nasal or liquid, and in gemination (pf, ts, kx). The affricate outcome of k is the narrowest
of these geographically and belongs to Upper German, which is why standard German has *Kind* beside
*machen*; the post-vocalic k > ch change runs all the way to the Benrath line and is the isogloss
the whole shift is usually named for.

A later phase, the Medienverschiebung, devoices the voiced stops. Only the dental established
itself: germ. *d* > *t* throughout the High German area and into the standard language, while
*b* > *p* and *g* > *k* survive only in Highest Alemannic and South Bavarian. The relative
chronology places t > ts before d > t, and d > t before þ > d.

Phase 1's account of all this is right, and the two examples it picks are exactly the textbook ones.
DWDS/Pfeifer confirms both independently: germ. *daga- yields ahd. *tag* against got. *dags* and
aengl. *dæg*; westgerm. *dō- yields ahd. *tuon* against aengl. *dōn* and engl. *do*.

One wrinkle I checked because the essay's own next sentences raise it, and which does not damage
Phase 1: Kölsch says *Daach* for *Tag* and *Dür* for *Tür*, which looks like unshifted d in a High
German dialect. It is not. *Tür* goes back to germ. *dur-, so its standard-German t is itself a
product of the shift, and Ripuarian *Dür* shows the later West Central German lenition of t back to
d rather than a failure of the shift to arrive. The claim "across the whole High German area"
survives, and I note it because Phase 1's Citizendium sentence about "even the most northern
dialects" is the one link in its evidence that a skeptic could have pulled on.

## Phase 1's replacement prose

> This series of sound changes transformed voiceless stops into fricatives or affricates (p → pf or
> ff; t → ts or s; k → ch after a vowel), giving German words like ~Pfund~, ~Wasser~, and ~machen~
> their characteristic sounds where English has ~pound~, ~water~, and ~make~. A second phase
> hardened the voiced stop d into t, which is why German has ~Tag~ and ~tun~ where English has
> ~day~ and ~do~.

Unnecessary, and imperfect if adopted anyway. Three observations, in descending order of how much
they matter.

1. "A second phase" is wrong against the standard numbering. Handbook enumerations separate
   fricativization from affrication, which makes the Medienverschiebung the third phase, with
   þ > d fourth. The relative chronology is right, so only the ordinal misfires, and the indefinite
   article softens it toward "another phase". Still, an essay that names a number should name the
   right one, and "a later stage" says the same thing with nothing to get wrong.
2. "k → ch after a vowel" is accurate and I would keep it if any edit lands here, which puts me at
   odds with the earlier pass on this path. Its objection is that qualifying one arm of a three-arm
   list implies position matters for k and not for p and t. The implicature it warns about is true
   as far as it goes: k is the one arm whose outcome is positionally restricted in the standard
   language, and the qualifier is what stops a reader from generalizing the pattern to *Chind*.
   The objection that the qualifier collides with the Swiss sentence two lines later runs backwards,
   in my reading: with the qualifier, "in Swiss German, the /k/ sound became guttural /x/" is a
   genuine southern extension, which is what the essay says it is; without it, the Swiss claim looks
   like a restatement of something already granted for standard German. Either way this clause is
   ground E5 and E6 also stand on, so it is not E3's to settle.
3. "which is why German has ~Tag~ and ~tun~ where English has ~day~ and ~do~" reuses the
   "German … where English has …" frame from the clause twelve words earlier. Markup is balanced,
   house style is respected, no em dashes, no new parenthetical, so this is only cadence.

Salvage form, recorded as an option and not as a recommendation, since my verdict is that no change
is warranted. Leave the parenthesis exactly as it stands and append one sentence after the ~make~
clause: "A later phase hardened Germanic d into t, which is why German has ~tun~ where English has
~do~." That keeps the essay's own subject in view, since ~tun~ is the very verb the preceding
paragraph names as the source of the weak dental suffix, and it drops the ~Tag~ pair, a noun that
earns nothing in a verb essay. If Josh takes it, the German counterpart is the matching sentence
appended at `docs/verb_history_de.txt` line 122 after the ~make~ clause, with ~tun~ and ~do~ left
unlocalized the way the essay already leaves its English glosses, and with the established German
term for the change rather than a calque of "hardened".

## Strongest case for the finding, and my answer

The strongest case is not the one Phase 1 argues. It is this. The essay's stated reason for
mentioning the shift at all is that it is what distinguished Old High German "from other Germanic
languages", and the single change that reaches every High German dialect without exception is
absent from the list. Worse, the essay has a private motive for wanting it. One paragraph earlier,
at line 155, the essay derives the weak dental suffix from the verb "to do", *dō-. A German reader
who knows that verb as *tun* is handed a d and a t with no bridge between them, and the bridge is
sitting right there in the next paragraph, unmentioned. That is a real loss, and it is the kind of
buried connection this essay elsewhere goes out of its way to surface.

My answer is that it argues for an enrichment, not a correction, and Phase 2's question is whether
the essay is wrong. It is not. The *dō-* to *tun* link is a pleasure the essay currently declines
to offer, not a debt it fails to pay, and no reader is misled by its absence: the connection is
invisible to anyone who has not already been told the shift had a second half. A fact-check that
promotes "this passage could say more" to a finding will find such a case in every paragraph of any
essay, which is why I decline this one and record the salvage sentence above instead.
