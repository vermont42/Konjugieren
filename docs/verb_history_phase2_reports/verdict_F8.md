# F8 · verdict: partly · grade: nitpick

Essay line 164. Phase 1 graded this needs-hedging at medium confidence. The defect it points at
is real but it is an omission, not a hedging failure, and it is smaller than Phase 1 claims. Its
replacement prose needs revision on two points.

## Why

The sentence under review does not stand alone. Read in its paragraph:

> Old High German expressed future time primarily through the present tense with temporal adverbs.
> Beginning in Middle High German, the verb ~werden~ (to become) was grammaticalized as a future
> auxiliary:

The operative words are "Beginning in". The sentence makes an onset claim, not a completion claim.
It does not say that Middle High German had a working future tense, does not say the construction
was frequent, and does not say the grammaticalization finished inside the period. A reader is
underinformed about where the process ended, not misinformed about it. That is the difference
between a nitpick and a factual error, and it is also the difference between an omission and the
category Phase 1 assigned. "needs-hedging" is defined as a contested question stated flatly. The
part of this sentence that is genuinely contested is the onset, and the essay hedges it correctly
with "Beginning in". The part Phase 1 wants added, the terminus, is not contested at all: everyone
puts it in Early New High German, culminating in the sixteenth century. Omitting a settled endpoint
is not an unhedged contested claim.

On the onset the finding concedes, correctly, that the essay is if anything conservative. Bech's
old view put the origin in the thirteenth century, squarely inside Middle High German. Concu's
corpus study pushes attestations back into Old High German and says the construction was "already
well established in the first two centuries of the Middle High German period". "Beginning in Middle
High German" sits between those two positions and is defensible against both.

What survives is smaller and worth fixing anyway. The essay names Old High German with dates,
Middle High German with dates, and then goes straight to modern German; Early New High German
appears nowhere in the article. Six lines earlier, at line 158, the perfect gets the two-endpoint
treatment: "began in Old High German and became fully established in Middle High German
(1050–1350)". Against that immediately preceding parallel, a reader can take "Beginning in Middle
High German" as the same shape of claim with the endpoint left implicit inside the period being
narrated. That is a soft misdirection produced by the essay's own local convention rather than by
anything the sentence asserts, and one clause fixes it. Cheap fix, small defect: nitpick.

Two things I checked before downgrading. First, whether any handbook actually dates the
establishment of the werden future to Middle High German, which would have made the essay's flat
reading correct and refuted the finding outright. No: the sources converge on late Middle High
German consolidation and a sixteenth-century explosion. Second, whether the essay elsewhere
contradicts the added terminus. It does not; line 181 simply lists werden + infinitive as the modern
future.

## What is actually true

Phase 1's "What is actually true" paragraph is correct, and my own research reproduces it. werden +
infinitive is attested from Old High German, occurs through Middle High German but is marginal
there, and in the thirteenth century is outnumbered by werden + present participle (39 against 143
in the ReM corpus). Middle High German normally expressed futurity with the simple present, with
explicit periphrases built on sol, wil and muoz as the standing alternatives. The construction
consolidated across the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries and expanded sharply in the sixteenth,
inside Early New High German (conventionally 1350–1650), which is when it became the regular future
auxiliary. Concu's revision moves the origin earlier, not the completion.

One qualification Phase 1 does not make, and which matters for its own replacement wording: the
werden future was never purely temporal. Paul's grammar is cited in the secondary literature for the
observation that in Middle High German the future use of werden was never free of modal meaning, and
a substantial modern strand (Vater, Leiss, Diewald) argues that werden + infinitive in present-day
German still carries epistemic rather than purely temporal force. Any fix should therefore avoid
promising the reader a "true" future auxiliary.

## Phase 1's replacement prose

> Middle High German began to pair the verb ~werden~ (to become) with an infinitive, but the pattern
> was rare there and competed with periphrases built on ~sollen~ and ~wollen~; it hardened into a
> true future auxiliary only in Early New High German:

Markup is clean: three balanced `~…~` pairs, no `$…$`, no asterisks, and it keeps the colon that
leads into the "Ich werde singen" example line. The substance is right in direction. Three problems.

1. **It introduces Early New High German with no dates**, in an essay that dates Old High German
   (750–1050 AD) and Middle High German (1050–1350) at first mention. Since the whole complaint is
   that the reader cannot place the change on the timeline, handing them an undated new period name
   only half fixes it.
2. **"a true future auxiliary" overshoots.** It reads as a claim that the construction became a pure
   temporal future, which is exactly the point the modal-versus-temporal literature disputes for
   Early New High German and for modern German alike. "the regular future auxiliary" makes the same
   point without the exposed adjective.
3. **It loses the essay's own hedge word.** "Middle High German began to pair" states the onset as
   Middle High German more flatly than "Beginning in Middle High German" did, which is awkward given
   that Phase 1's own headline source puts attestations in Old High German. Minor, but the original
   was the better-hedged of the two.

Also, "the pattern was rare there" is defensible but sits in visible tension with Concu's "already
well established"; the two are talking about attestation versus frequency. Naming the competitors
with the modern infinitives ~sollen~ and ~wollen~ rather than MHG sol and wil is a fair
simplification for this essay's register and not a defect.

## Revised replacement prose

English, replacing only the second sentence of line 164 and leaving the Old High German sentence
before it untouched:

> Beginning in Middle High German, the verb ~werden~ (to become) was paired with an infinitive,
> though the simple present and periphrases with ~sollen~ and ~wollen~ still carried most future
> reference; ~werden~ became the regular future auxiliary only in Early New High German (1350–1650):

This keeps the original onset hedge, supplies the terminus the finding correctly asks for, dates the
new period the way the essay dates the other two, and avoids "true". The date range is a period
gloss of the same kind the essay already uses at lines 156 and 158, not a new parenthetical aside.
If a shorter version is wanted, the clause "and periphrases with ~sollen~ and ~wollen~" can be cut
without loss to the correction.

German counterpart, `docs/verb_history_de.txt` line 130, second sentence, currently:

> Beginnend im Mittelhochdeutschen wurde das Verb ~werden~ (to become) als Futurauxiliar
> grammatikalisiert:

Phase 4 owns the translation. What it needs: the same two-stage structure, the existing English
gloss "(to become)" left as it is per that file's convention, the German period name
Frühneuhochdeutsch with the same 1350–1650 range so both localizations date it identically, and
~sollen~ and ~wollen~ carried over with their emphasis markers balanced. Note that the German file
already writes "Mittelhochdeutschen (1050–1350)" at line 124, so the dated-period habit is
established on that side too.

## Strongest case for the finding, and my answer

The strongest case is the parallelism argument, and it is a good one. Line 158 tells the reader
explicitly that the perfect "began in Old High German and became fully established in Middle High
German (1050–1350)". Six lines later the future gets a "Beginning in Middle High German" with no
matching second clause. A reader who has just been taught the essay's two-stage template reads the
missing stage as absent because the change completed within the named period, and the essay never
mentions Early New High German anywhere to correct that inference. On that reading the reader walks
away placing the arrival of the German future roughly two centuries early, which is the finding's
charge exactly.

My answer: this is an inference the reader may draw, not a claim the sentence makes, and the words
that would license the inference are the ones the essay conspicuously does not use. "Beginning in X"
is a standard onset formula and it is the correct one here. Compare a genuine failure of the same
kind, which would read "In Middle High German, werden was grammaticalized as a future auxiliary" or
"By Middle High German, German had a future tense". Neither is what line 164 says. The gap is real
enough to be worth one clause, which is why I do not refute the finding, but a reader who is
underinformed about an endpoint the essay never asserts has not been misinformed, and the question
the essay allegedly flattens is not contested. Hence partly, and hence nitpick rather than
needs-hedging.
