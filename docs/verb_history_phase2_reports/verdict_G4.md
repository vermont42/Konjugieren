# G4 · verdict: partly · grade: nitpick

Phase 1's defect is real and its grade is right. Two things do not survive: its evidentiary
claim about what the Duden page says, and its replacement, which repairs one label of a
two-label contrast and leaves the contrast broken on a different axis.

## Why

**Question 1: is line 174 as written wrong?** Only mildly, and the finding's own grade
captures that. The paragraph at line 172 sets up a genuine diachronic development, so a
reader can take `Old:` as "the older of the two constructions" rather than "the obsolete
one". But the pairing `Old:` / `Modern alternative:` is temporal on both sides, and the
example chosen is the single worst instance the essay could have picked: käme is one of the
handful of strong-verb Konjunktiv-II forms that never went anywhere. A learner meeting
`Old: Wenn ich käme` will conclude that käme is a form to recognize rather than produce,
which inverts what German usage guidance actually says about this verb. Set against line
177, which exempts "common verbs" from the würde takeover, the essay labels as old a form
its own next sentence protects. That is an internal tension, not a falsehood, and line 175
says "alternative" rather than "replacement", so nobody is actively misinformed. Nitpick,
and I would not upgrade it.

**Question 2: is Phase 1's "What is actually true" correct?** Substantively yes, with one
sourcing correction. käme is current German, it is one of the distinct (non-syncretic)
Konjunktiv-Präteritum forms that IDS grammis names explicitly, and the forms Duden's
Sprachratgeber actually singles out as archaic are hülfe and gälte/gölte. Google Books
German shows käme flat across 120 years and still roughly 2.5 times more frequent than
"kommen würde" in 2019. So the contrast at lines 174 to 175 is indeed synthetic versus
periphrastic rather than dated versus current.

The correction: Phase 1 attributes to the Duden Sprachratgeber page a list of frequent
synthetic forms "bliebe, brächte, gäbe, ginge, hätte, käme, könnte, wäre, würde and wüsste"
naming käme explicitly. I fetched that page twice and could not find such a list; what the
page carries is the syncretism rule, the ambiguity examples (ich liebte, wir riefen, sie
gingen), and the two archaic examples hülfe and gälte/gölte. Phase 1's rendering is a
paraphrase, not a quotation, and the list may have come from a different Duden surface.
The conclusion still holds on the evidence I gathered independently, but the claim
"Duden names käme" should not be repeated without a verified locator.

Phase 1 also understates the counterweight. In spoken German the würde-form carries the
overwhelming majority of Konjunktiv-II tokens, and popular teaching sources do describe
synthetic strong-verb forms as gehoben or veraltet. That does not make käme old, but it
means the finding's "not true" is stronger than the evidence supports for the spoken
register. The essay's own register clause at line 177 already handles this.

**Question 3: is Phase 1's replacement correct?** No, it is half a fix. Changing line 174
to `Synthetic:` while leaving line 175 as `Modern alternative:` puts a typological label
opposite a temporal one, and the temporal one still carries the implicature the finding
objects to: if the second is the modern one, the first is not. The pair has to move
together. Markup, gloss handling and vocabulary are all fine in Phase 1's line ("synthetic"
already appears in the surrounding prose at line 172 and 177), so the revision is small.

## What is actually true

German Konjunktiv II has two live exponents: the inherited synthetic preterite-subjunctive
form and the periphrasis with würde plus infinitive. Which one a speaker reaches for is
governed by syncretism and register, not by date. Where the synthetic form is homophonous
with the preterite indicative (all weak verbs, and the wir/sie forms of strong verbs whose
stem vowel takes no umlaut), the würde-form is the ordinary repair. Where umlaut keeps the
form distinct, the synthetic form survives: sein, haben, werden, the modals except sollen
and wollen, and a set of high-frequency strong verbs of which kommen is a member. IDS
grammis lists kamen/kämen among its examples of umlaut blocking syncretism. Duden's advice
to substitute würde for a synthetic form on stylistic grounds is aimed at hülfe and
gälte/gölte, not at käme. In written German käme has been stable for over a century and
remains commoner than the bigram "kommen würde". In speech the würde-form dominates across
the board, which is a fact about register and medium, not about käme's age.

## Revised replacement prose

> 🇩🇪 Periphrastic: Wenn ich kommen $wÜrde$... (If I $wOUld$ come...)

Both labels are true of both lines in every register, the pair is parallel, the vocabulary
matches the prose above and below (line 172 "periphrastic constructions", line 177

and their internal capitalization, and the English glosses are all carried over verbatim.

## Strongest case for the finding, and my answer

Strongest case for: käme is not merely surviving, it is the recommended form. German usage
advice routinely warns against "wenn ich kommen würde" where a distinct synthetic form
exists, so `Old:` on käme does not just misdate the form, it points a learner at the weaker
of the two options. On that reading the finding is underdrawn rather than overdrawn, and
the grade should rise to needs-hedging. My answer: the essay never tells the reader to
prefer either line, it calls the second an alternative rather than a replacement, and three
lines later it exempts common verbs by name. A learner who reads the paragraph rather than
the label alone gets the right picture. That is the definition of a nitpick.

Strongest case against the finding, which is the case for refuting it outright: in a
diachronic essay whose whole subject is what came before what, `Old:` naturally reads as
"the older construction", and popular German-teaching material does describe synthetic
strong-verb Konjunktiv II as gehoben or veraltet, with würde-forms at something like 98
percent of spoken Konjunktiv-II tokens. On that reading the label is defensible shorthand.
My answer: the sources that call these forms dated reach for hülfe, nähme, ginge, never for
käme, and the corpus record for käme in writing is flat, not declining. Frequency in speech
is not age. And the label the essay chose is not "older construction", it is "Old" opposite
"Modern", which is a claim about the form on the page rather than about the construction
type. The defect is small, but it is there.
