# R1 — verdict: refuted (grade: none)

Essay line 90, English; German counterpart line 56.

## Why

The sentence under attack does not stand alone, and the finding quotes only its second half. The pair reads:

> The Yamnaya spoke what linguists reconstruct as ~Proto-Indo-European~ (PIE), the ancestor of languages spoken by nearly half of humans alive today. These languages include German, English, Ukrainian, Hindi, Persian, Cornish, Greek, and Latin.

Phase 1's case is that "These languages" inherits the modifier "spoken by nearly half of humans alive today", so the list is a list of currently spoken languages and Latin does not belong in it. Three things defeat that.

**First, the modifier is collective and cannot distribute.** No single language is spoken by anything like half of humanity. English tops the table at roughly 1.5 billion speakers of all kinds, under a fifth of the species, and Mandarin sits near 15 percent. A reader therefore cannot parse "spoken by nearly half of humans alive today" as a property held by each member; it is only intelligible as a property of the set taken together, which is to say a characterization of the Indo-European family. The sentence that introduces it says so in as many words: PIE is "the ancestor of" that set. Once the set is the family, Latin is a member of it, and the finding's mismatch evaporates.

**Second, even on the finding's own parse, no false proposition results.** Grant the strictest reading, that the set is exactly {languages spoken today} and the aggregate speaker share is the collective property. The essay still never asserts that Latin has speakers, has native speakers, or is anyone's mother tongue. And "Latin is spoken today" is not flatly false in the first place. Ethnologue, which is as unsentimental about this as any reference gets, lists Latin as a language *of Vatican City* used only as a second language; the Holy See treats it as the authentic language of its most solemn acts, and Benedict XVI created a Pontifical Academy for Latin in 2012 specifically to keep it in use. What Latin lacks is native speakers and a heritage community. The essay claims neither. The residue is a looseness of set membership, not a claim a reader could be wrong about.

**Third, the finding grades itself out of existence.** Its own text says "no reader will conclude Latin is a living language". A defect that misinforms nobody, in a sentence that asserts nothing false under either available parse, is not a defect the essay needs to carry a change for. Its two supporting citations are both Wikipedia articles, which is thin ground on which to move prose that is already true.

I did not reopen "nearly half of humans alive today", which the brief records as dismissed twice, and I take Cornish's inventory row as settled elsewhere.

## What is actually true

Phase 1's factual section is mostly right and slightly overstated in the one place that matters to its argument.

Right: all eight named languages descend from Proto-Indo-European, across six branches, so the list is sound as a list of descendants. Right: Latin has had no native speakers for many centuries and no heritage speech community. Right: Cornish is spoken today. It died as a community language in the eighteenth century, was revived from Jenner's 1904 handbook, was moved by UNESCO from "extinct" to "critically endangered" in 2010, and the 2021 census of England and Wales recorded 563 people giving Cornish as their main language, up from 557 in 2011. Fluency estimates in the several hundreds are consistent with that.

Overstated: the finding treats "spoken today" and "has native speakers" as the same test, and they are not. Latin fails the second and passes a restricted form of the first. Ethnologue's own taxonomy makes exactly this distinction, noting that some languages with no L1 users "may continue to be used as second-languages only for specific, restricted, often vehicular, functions", and files Latin there. So the sentence "Latin is not a member of the set of languages spoken today", which is the load-bearing sentence of the finding, is itself a claim that needs hedging.

## Phase 1's replacement prose

> Its descendants include German, English, Ukrainian, Hindi, Persian, Cornish, Greek, and Latin.

Not wrong, and not needed. It contains no factual error, breaks no markup (the sentence carries none), and matches house style. Two small costs if it were adopted anyway. The pronoun "Its" has to reach back past "languages spoken by nearly half of humans alive today" to find PIE, whereas "These languages" picks up the nearest plural and reads without a stutter. And the substitution trades a sentence that ties the family to living humanity for a bare genealogical list, which is a small loss of the paragraph's rhetorical arc into the *tewtéh₂* passage that follows. Neither cost is large. Neither is worth paying for a correction that corrects nothing.

## Strongest case for the finding, and my answer

The strongest case is a parse I have to concede is available. Read "languages spoken by nearly half of humans alive today" as denoting the set of languages that are spoken today, with the head count aggregated over that set rather than predicated of each member. That parse is grammatical, it is arguably the most precise one, and under it "These languages include ... Latin" does put an extinct language inside a set defined by present-day speakerhood. A parallel makes the jar audible: "Old Norse is the ancestor of languages spoken across Scandinavia today. These languages include Danish, Swedish, Norwegian, and Old Gutnish."

My answer has two parts. The parallel is not parallel: "spoken across Scandinavia today" distributes over each member, so Old Gutnish is directly and falsely predicated of. "Spoken by nearly half of humans alive today" cannot distribute over anything, because no language on Earth satisfies it, which forces the collective reading and with it the family reading. And even where the strict parse survives, the worst it yields is that Latin has been included in a loosely bounded set, not that anything false has been said about Latin, whose continued restricted use as a second language means even the membership test is not cleanly failed. A fact-check exists to catch propositions a reader would be wrong to believe. There is no such proposition here.
