# R2a · verdict: partly · grade: nitpick (downgraded from needs-hedging)

Essay line 92 (English), line 58 (German).

## Why

Phase 1 is right that the derivation of *tewtéh₂ from the root for swelling is contested,
and it identifies the right objectors in outline. It is wrong about how the contest stands,
and its fix pushes the sentence past the truth in the other direction.

The decisive fact is one Phase 1 never quotes, because it never read the paper that started
the dispute. Beekes 1998 is the modern attack on the derivation, and Beekes opens his own
attack by conceding the field: "As far as I see it is generally accepted that *teuta is
derived from the root *teuH- 'to swell'." He then argues the derivation is "semantically
problematic and formally impossible" and concludes the word is a substrate loan. So the
state of play is a traditional derivation that the standard etymological dictionaries carry
(Pokorny IEW 1080, Walde-Hofmann LEW, Mayrhofer EWAia, and with LIV 639-640 glossing the root
"schwellen, stark werden", which is word for word the essay's "to swell, be strong"), one
serious specialist attack on it, and two subsequent specialists who reject the attack
(Delamarre 2003: 296; Meini 2012, whose whole paper is a defence of the semantic path from
"swelling, fullness" to "people"). Matasović's Leiden Proto-Celtic dictionary still
reconstructs *teu̯-t-eh₂, with the root written laryngeal-free exactly as the essay writes it.

The essay's sentence is "Linguists reconstruct PIE *tewtéh₂, from the root *tew- ... as a
word meaning ...". The brief's own test for a hedging failure is a claim that misrepresents
where the consensus sits. Here the consensus sits where the essay puts it, on the testimony
of the man who set out to overturn it. A popular essay that reports the majority handbook
etymology of an etymology, in a subordinate phrase, in a paragraph whose load-bearing claim
about the Yamnaya self-designation is already hedged twice in the following two sentences
("If the Yamnaya or their linguistic heirs used something like *tewtéh₂", "may trace back"),
is not misinforming anyone. The most that survives is that a reader who went on to read
Beekes would find a live dispute the essay gave no sign of. That is a specialist's quibble,
so: nitpick, and a small optional softening is the right response, not a rewrite.

Two of Phase 1's supporting particulars do not hold up:

- "Beekes calls the substrate theory speculative" is backwards. Beekes *is* the substrate
  theory's proponent for this word. What he calls "very speculative" is one sub-argument
  inside it, the idea of a non-IE eu/au vowel interchange. His summary judgment is the
  opposite: the loanword conclusion "is the most probable solution."
- "Mallory and Adams float a different root of the shape *tew-, possibly the one behind
  Latin tueor" I could not confirm from anything but Phase 1's Wiktionary source. The Latin
  word the literature actually argues about here is *tōtus*, not *tueor*, and Beekes devotes
  a section to rejecting that connection. Meini cites EIEC s.v. "people" on the side of the
  traditional swelling/fullness account, not against it.

Phase 1's evidence base is two Wiktionary pages. On a question where a peer-reviewed paper
and the primary article are both freely downloadable, that is exposed, and it shows in the
garbled attributions above.

## What is actually true

PIE *tewtéh₂ "people, tribe" is securely reconstructed from Italic (Osc. touto, Umbr. totam),
Celtic (OIr. túath, Gaul. Touto-), Germanic (Goth. þiuda, OHG diot) and Baltic (Lith. tautà,
OPr. tauto). Nobody disputes that, and the essay does not need a hedge for it.

The further derivation from *teuh₂- / tuh₂- "to swell, to become strong" is the traditional
and still generally accepted account, carried by Pokorny, Walde-Hofmann, Mayrhofer and, as a
root gloss, LIV. It is not unopposed. Beekes 1998 argues that the root's final laryngeal
would have yielded an acute in Baltic where the attested root is circumflex, and *tautā- in
Italic and Celtic against the Gaulish names in Teut-/Tout-, and that no proposed base meaning
("power", "plenitude", "wholeness") gets convincingly to "people"; he concludes the word is a
loan from the non-IE language of Krahe's Old European hydronymy. He also concedes he has "no
hard, formal evidence" for that. Delamarre 2003 and Meini 2012 both find his arguments
uncompelling, and Meini defends the semantic development as ordinary metaphor and metonymy.
Matasović 2009 continues to reconstruct *teu̯-t-eh₂.

So: contested, yes; overturned, no; and the essay's side of it is the side the dissenter
himself describes as generally accepted. "The full community" is also well founded as a
rendering, not a liberty: Bader posits "social whole", Meid "Ganzheit", Prosdocimi "the
fundamental civic unit".

## Phase 1's replacement prose

> Linguists reconstruct PIE *tewtéh₂ as a word meaning "the full community" or simply "the
> people", usually traced to a root *tew- ("to swell, be strong"), though that step is
> disputed and the word may be a borrowing.

The markup is safe: no paired markers are involved, the two asterisks stay unpaired, the
curly quotes are unchanged. The content is the problem. "The word may be a borrowing" hands
a minority hypothesis the same grammatical footing as the majority one, in an essay that
gives the reader no way to weigh them, and it does so on the strength of a proposal whose own
author says there is no hard formal evidence for it and which the two specialists who
responded in print rejected. Trading a mild overstatement for a larger one is not a fix. The
sentence is also visibly longer and more clause-ridden than its neighbours, which in this
paragraph run short and declarative.

The first half of the replacement, "usually traced to", is the good part and worth keeping.

## Revised replacement prose

English, line 92, optional and low-priority:

> Linguists reconstruct PIE *tewtéh₂ as a word meaning "the full community" or simply "the
> people", and usually trace it to the root *tew- ("to swell, be strong").

This keeps the sentence's length and rhythm, keeps both existing glosses, adds no
parenthetical and no em dash, leaves the asterisks unpaired, and moves the derivation from a
flat apposition to a reported majority view, which is exactly what it is. It does not import
the borrowing hypothesis.

German counterpart, `docs/verb_history_de.txt` line 58: the sentence currently reads
"Linguisten rekonstruieren PIE *tewtéh₂, von der Wurzel *tew- („schwellen, stark sein“), als
ein Wort für ...". If the English change is made, the German needs the same single move, the
appositive "von der Wurzel *tew-" becoming a reported-usual clause, with the reconstruction
itself left flat and the two glosses „schwellen, stark sein" and „die gesamte Gemeinschaft"
untouched. No new markup, and the asterisks stay unpaired. Phase 4 owns the wording.

## Strongest case for the finding, and my answer

The strongest case: this is not a fringe objection. Beekes was a Leiden professor, the
argument ran in the Journal of Indo-European Studies, and his verdict on the derivation is
not "doubtful" but "formally impossible". The laryngeal problem is concrete and testable, not
hand-waving: a root-final *h₂ predicts a Baltic acute and Italo-Celtic *tautā-, and neither is
what we find. An essay that says "from the root *tew-" with no qualifier tells the reader a
question is closed that a named specialist regards as decided the other way. By the letter of
the rubric, contested question stated flatly, that is needs-hedging.

My answer: the rubric's second clause is what settles it. A hedge fails when it misrepresents
where the consensus sits, and the essay's does not, on Beekes's own account of the consensus.
The dispute is also asymmetrical in a way the bare word "contested" hides. One side is the
handbook tradition plus two published rebuttals of the attack; the other is a single article
whose author concedes he has no hard evidence and whose positive proposal, Old European
substrate, is itself among the more contested frameworks in the field. Reporting the
handbook derivation in a subordinate phrase of a popular history, in a paragraph that hedges
the claim the phrase actually serves, misleads nobody. It is worth a two-word softening and
not worth a clause about borrowing.
