# R6 · verdict: partly · grade: nitpick

## Why

Three questions, three answers.

**1. Is the essay wrong as written?** Narrowly, yes. The bullet prints `*n̥-péh₂-tōr` as a
reconstruction, and no such form can have existed. The PIE word for "father" is `*ph₂tḗr`, and
its root syllable is in **zero grade throughout the paradigm**; the ablaut in that word lives
entirely in the agent suffix (`*-tér-` / `*-tḗr` / `*-tor-` / `*-tōr` / `*-tr-`). There is no
`*peh₂-` variant of the root anywhere in the daughter languages. The Greek reflex settles it by
itself: `ἀπάτωρ` has a **short** alpha in the `-πα-` syllable. A root full grade `*péh₂-` would
have given Attic-Ionic `πη-` and Doric long `πᾱ-`, exactly as `*méh₂tēr` gives Attic `μήτηρ`
against Doric `μᾱ́τηρ`. Short `ᾰ` is the regular Greek outcome of a vocalized `*h₂` with no
preceding vowel, i.e. of `*ph₂-`. So the defensible PIE-side shape of `ἀπάτωρ` is `*n̥-ph₂-tōr`,
not `*n̥-péh₂-tōr`. The bullet has no hedge to save it: it is a bare form offered as fact.

**2. Is Phase 1's "What is actually true" correct?** Only half. Its central evidentiary claim is
false. Phase 1 states that "`*n̥-péh₂-tōr` is not a reconstruction found in the reference
literature," that "targeted searches for the string return only the unrelated root `*peh₂-`," and
that the form is most likely a Proto-Indo-Europeanization invented by the essayist. It is not.
The string appears verbatim, with that exact hyphenation and that exact acute accent, in the PIE
column of the five-grade table in English Wikipedia's "Indo-European ablaut" article, alongside
`*n̥-péh₂-tor-m̥` for the accusative. That is the article Phase 1 itself cites, and it quoted only
the Greek column of the table without noticing that the PIE column is the essay's source. The
essay's whole bullet list, five grades in that order with `*mḗh₁n̥s` for the lengthened e-grade,
mirrors that page. So the essay did not invent a form; it copied the standard tertiary
presentation, which happens to carry a slip in one column. The finding's diagnosis is wrong even
though its verdict on the form is right.

Phase 1's secondary observation, that the `*ō` sits in the suffix while the accent sits on the
root, is true of the form but not much of an objection: hysterokinetic and amphikinetic
nominatives with retracted accent and lengthened suffix are a real type, and Wikipedia's table is
deliberately illustrating suffix ablaut in that word.

**3. Is Phase 1's replacement correct?** Yes, substantively. `*bʰṓr` "thief" is a real
reconstruction, a root noun of `*bʰer-` "to carry", continued by Greek `φώρ` and Latin `fūr`. It
puts the `*ō` in the root, where bullets one through three put their vowels, and it reuses the
root the list opens with. The replacement makes no claim about Latin, which is prudent, since
`fūr` has been argued to be a Greek loan. Markup is clean: tildes balanced, one unpaired asterisk
per form, no `$…$`, no `‡…‡`, gloss in ASCII quotes exactly as bullet one already does. One small
wording problem survives, addressed below.

**Why nitpick and not factual-error.** The bullet is illustrating one thing, `*ō`, and the `*ō` is
genuinely there in `-tōr`. The defect is a spurious `e` in a *different* syllable of an ornamental
example that carries no gloss and no argument. No reader of a German-verb app is misinformed about
ablaut, about lengthened grades, or about anything the essay goes on to say; the only false belief
available is the segmental shape of a Greek compound's preform, and it is a belief the reader would
equally have got from the encyclopedia article the essay followed. Note also the severity
inconsistency inside the finding itself: Phase 1 saw the larger issue, that Wikipedia flags these
very lengthened grades as products of Szemerényi's and Stang's laws rather than of ablaut proper,
and declined to raise it as "defensible but awkward." That objection touches bullets four and five
both, and it is the one a linguist would actually make. Grading the vocalism slip as
factual-error while waiving the mechanism objection inverts the real severities. The fix is cheap
and correct and should be taken. It is a correction, not an error report.

## What is actually true

- `*bʰer-`, `*bʰor-`, `*bʰr-` are the standard illustration of e-, o- and zero-grade. The essay's
  first three bullets are right.
- The PIE word for "father" is `*ph₂tḗr`, oblique stem `*ph₂tr̥-`. The root is zero grade in every
  case form; the ablaut is in the suffix. Greek `πατήρ`, `πατρός`, `ἀπάτωρ` all show short `ᾰ` from
  vocalized `*h₂`.
- `*n̥-péh₂-tōr` is therefore not a well-formed reconstruction. It is what English Wikipedia's
  ablaut table prints, and the essay took it from there. The defensible shape is `*n̥-ph₂-tōr`,
  matching Greek `ἀ-πά-τωρ` "fatherless".
- `*bʰṓr` "thief" is a real root noun of `*bʰer-`, from earlier `*bʰórs`, continued by Greek `φώρ`
  and Latin `fūr`. Its long `ō` arises by Szemerényi's law, i.e. by compensatory lengthening on
  loss of final `*-s`, and the same caveat holds for the essay's `*mḗh₁-n̥s` and for `*n̥-ph₂-tōr`:
  most textbook lengthened grades are not root ablaut proper. Swapping the example does not fix
  that, and the essay is not obliged to raise it.

## Phase 1's replacement prose

> 🐑 ~lengthened o-grade~: *ō (as in *bʰōr, "thief", from the same root)

Sound in substance, markup-clean, right length. One defect: "from the same root" sits immediately
below bullet four, whose example `*mḗh₁-n̥s` is from a *different* root, so the nearest antecedent
for "the same root" is the wrong one. Name the root instead. Naming it also lets the reader feel
the connection the essay is good at: one who carries off is a thief.

## Revised replacement prose

English, replacing line 117:

> 🐑 ~lengthened o-grade~: *ō (as in *bʰōr, "thief", from *bʰer-)

German counterpart, `docs/verb_history_de.txt` line 83, currently
`🐑 ~gedehnte o-Stufe~: *ō (wie in *n̥-péh₂-tōr)`. Phase 4 owns the translation. What it needs: the
same substitution of the reconstructed form, the gloss "thief" rendered in German, and the root
`*bʰer-` named rather than referred to as "the same root". The German bullet one already glosses
`*bʰer-` as "tragen", so the German bullet five should echo that gloss's register. Reconstructed
forms `*bʰōr` and `*bʰer-` are not localized. Tildes stay balanced; asterisks stay unpaired.

## Strongest case for the finding, and my answer

The strongest case is that this is a list of reconstructions presented as fact, in an essay whose
whole authority rests on getting reconstructions right, and one of the five is a form that cannot
have existed. If the essay wrote a German verb form that no German speaker has ever produced, we
would not call it a nitpick. Ablaut is the essay's central concept, this list is the reader's first
and only formal statement of the grade system, and the form is wrong in the very kind of detail the
list is teaching the reader to attend to, namely which vowel sits in which slot.

My answer: the analogy breaks on what the bullet is asserting. Each bullet asserts one thing, that
grade X looks like vowel Y, and every one of the five assertions is true, this one included: the
lengthened o-grade is `*ō`, and `-tōr` has it. The wrong segment is in a part of the form the
bullet neither points at nor explains, in a word from a language the essay never returns to, with
no gloss attached for a false meaning to attach to. And the counterfactual reader who checks the
form finds it printed identically in the standard encyclopedia treatment, which means the essay is
wrong in the way its own source is wrong rather than wrong in a way that singles it out. That is a
correction worth making, at nitpick weight. What would have been factual-error weight here is the
mechanism claim Phase 1 explicitly chose not to press.

**Source-quality note, stated plainly.** My sources here are tertiary, as were Phase 1's. I did not
get a handbook page in front of me. The load-bearing argument, however, is not an appeal to
authority but the Greek vowel quantities, which are checkable in any lexicon: `μήτηρ`/`μᾱ́τηρ` with
long vowels from `*méh₂-` against `ἀπάτωρ` with short `ᾰ`. Braune is irrelevant to this claim and
the Greek-side control is decisive without Fortson.
