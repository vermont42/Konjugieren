# R8 · verdict: refuted · grade: none

Essay line 122 (German line 88): `🇩🇪 nehmen, $nahm$, $genOMmen$ (take, $tOOk$, taken)`

## Why

Phase 1's etymology is right. Its reading of the essay is not, and the finding has nothing left
once the reading goes, since it concedes in its own text that "Nothing false is asserted."

The finding's case rests entirely on the premise that the parenthesis asserts a cognate pair, so
that `nehmen` beside `take` sets a class IV verb next to a class VI Norse loan and "marks both
sets of vowels red as though they illustrated the same inheritance." The essay makes no such
assertion, and its own convention forecloses it. Parentheses after a 🇩🇪 bullet in this document
are English translations, and elsewhere they gloss German words with English words that are
demonstrably not cognate. The decisive instance is line 153, in the very next section, which uses
the identical construction for the weak preterite:

> This is the origin of the "-te" ending in German preterites (machte, sagte, spielte) and the
> "-ed" ending in English ($mAde$, $saId$, played).

The list is positionally aligned: machte / made, sagte / said, spielte / played. The first two
pairs are cognate; the third is not remotely so. English `play` is from Old English `plegan`,
whose Continental relative is `pflegen`, while `spielen` continues Proto-Germanic *spilōną. Line
153 does with `played` precisely what line 122 does with `take`, in a passage with the same
inheritance thesis, and Phase 1 does not propose to fix it, because in that slot the translation
reading is obvious. The same reading is available at line 122 and is the correct one. Two further
parentheses confirm the convention: line 175, `Wenn ich kommen $wÜrde$... (If I $wOUld$ come...)`,
where `würde` continues `werden` and `would` continues `willan`, and line 161, `Ich bin gekommen
(I have come / I $cAme$)`, which offers two competing English renderings, something only a
translation slot would do. The word "cognate" appears nowhere in the essay.

The framing sentence is also narrower than the finding treats it. Line 119 ends "the ablaut
patterns became the vowel alternations we still see in ~German strong verbs~ today." German strong
verbs are the scope. `nehmen / nahm / genommen` is class IV throughout and is exactly what that
sentence promises. The English column is a courtesy so an English speaker can feel the same
phenomenon in a language they already have, and `take / took / taken` does that job as well as any
verb in English. Nor is line 125's closer falsified: English borrowed the lexeme from Norse, but it
borrowed it with an ablaut paradigm Norse had inherited from Proto-Germanic, and English slotted it
in beside its own inherited `shake / shook / shaken` and `draw / drew / drawn`. The pattern is a
direct inheritance even though the lexeme took a detour, and the sentence is about the pattern.

Two smaller inaccuracies in the finding's description of the line. `$nahm$` carries no uppercase
letter at all, so the German preterite vowel is not reddened, and English `taken` is unmarked
likewise; the row is not the symmetric pair of highlighted alternations the finding describes. And
the finding's own criterion would disqualify its control case. It offers `geben / give` as a clean
cognate pair whose alternations "really do continue the same inherited series." The lexeme is
inherited, but the modern English form is not phonologically inherited: Old English `giefan`
regularly yields Middle English `yiven`, and the hard initial of `give` is owed to Old Norse
`gefa`. If Norse involvement is what spoils the middle row, two of three rows are spoiled and the
fix is not a one-line swap. That inconsistency is a sign the criterion was invented for the middle
row rather than applied to the display.

I decline even the nitpick grade. A nitpick is a specialist's quibble that misleads nobody; this
one requires the reader first to adopt a misreading the essay does not license, and only then to
be misled by it.

## What is actually true

Phase 1's factual claims about the words all check out against my own research:

- English `take` is a late Old English borrowing, `tacan`, from a Scandinavian source, Old Norse
  `taka`, preterite `tók`, participle `tekinn`. It spread from the Danelaw, competed with the
  native verb through Middle English, and had displaced `nimen` by the 1200s.
- `tacan` entered Old English as a class VI strong verb, `tacan / tōc / tōcon / tacen`, the a - ō -
  ō - a series that also gives `shake`, `draw`, `slay`.
- Old English `niman` is class IV, the direct cognate of German `nehmen` from Proto-Germanic
  *nemaną, and `nehmen` is class IV.
- German `brechen` and English `break` are cognate, both from Germanic *brekan and PIE *bhreg̑-,
  and both are class IV. Phase 1's proposed substitute is therefore a genuine cognate class IV pair.

What is not true is the inference Phase 1 draws from those facts, that the essay's row asserts a
cognate parallel or a shared class. It asserts a German paradigm and supplies an English
translation with its own principal parts. Phase 1 also omits that modern `give` owes its initial
/g/ to Norse, so under its own criterion the row it treats as the standard is not clean either.

## Phase 1's replacement prose

> 🇩🇪 brechen, $brAch$, $gebrOchen$ (break, $brOke$, $brOken$)

Linguistically sound and mechanically safe. The `$` delimiters balance at eight, the capitalization
follows the surrounding rows exactly as `$sAng$` and `$gAb$` do, marking both English past forms
matches line 121 which marks both `$sAng$` and `$sUng$`, and `brechen / break` really are cognate
class IV verbs continuing *brekan.

It is nonetheless the wrong change, for three reasons beyond the absence of a defect. It trades one
of the highest-frequency verbs in German, and the canonical class IV teaching example, for a less
frequent one in a display whose purpose is recognition; this is an app for learners. It removes the
row's useful asymmetry, where unmarked `taken` beside marked `$genOMmen$` shows the reader that the
two languages did not preserve the same amount. And accepting it means editing a shipped German
localization at line 88 to match a row that was not broken.

Verdict on the replacement: unnecessary.

## Strongest case for the finding, and my answer

The strongest case is not the one Phase 1 argues. It is this: the paragraph closes with "These
vowel changes are direct inheritances from Proto-Indo-European," and `take` is the one word on
screen that did not reach its language by direct inheritance. A reader who takes "direct
inheritances" as a claim about every word displayed would pick up something false about English
lexical history and would have no way to know it. A layout can imply what prose does not state.

Three answers. First, the referent of "these vowel changes" is fixed two sentences earlier and it
is German strong verbs; the sentence is about the alternations, not about the transmission history
of each glossed English word. Second, the alternation in `take / took / taken` genuinely is
inherited from Proto-Indo-European by way of Proto-Germanic and Norse, so a reader who takes the
English column at face value learns something true; the only false belief available is the narrow
one that `nehmen` and `take` are the same word, which the essay never says and which the
`spielte / played` precedent at line 153 positively argues against. Third, if this objection were
allowed to bite it would bite `give` too, whose modern shape is equally Norse-touched, and the
honest consequence would be rewriting two rows and adding a caveat about Scandinavian borrowing to
a paragraph that is about PIE ablaut. That is a worse and longer essay in exchange for a
distinction no reader of this display is drawing.

I cannot construct a reader who ends up misinformed by line 122 as written. That is what "none"
means here.
