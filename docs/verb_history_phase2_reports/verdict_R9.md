# R9 · verdict: refuted · grade: none

## Why

Phase 1's linguistics is sound. Its reading of the sentence is not, and the reading is what the
finding rests on.

The sentence does not say "these vowels." It says "these vowel changes," and its antecedent is
the clause immediately before it, which the finding quotes but then argues against: "When PIE *e
and *o later developed into different vowels in the daughter languages, the ablaut patterns
became the vowel alternations we still see in German strong verbs today." So the essay has, in
the same paragraph and in the very sentence that sets up the triads, already said (a) that the
PIE vowels turned into different vowels and (b) that what carries over is the pattern. "These
vowel changes are direct inheritances" then asserts exactly what Phase 1's own "What is actually
true" section asserts: the alternation is inherited. The finding reads the sentence as though it
stood alone and as though "vowel changes" meant "vowels."

The German is less ambiguous than the English, not more. `Vokalwechsel` means vowel alternation,
and the preceding sentence spells out the chain: `die Ablautmuster` became `den Vokalwechseln,
die wir noch heute in deutschen starken Verben sehen`, and then `Diese Vokalwechsel sind direkte
Erbschaften`. A German reader cannot take the referent to be the individual vowels. Phase 1's
worry that a hedged English claim ships flat to German is inverted here.

Second, "direct" is not doing the damage the finding attributes to it. In historical linguistics
"inherited" is the term of art whose contrast class is *borrowed*, and inherited material is
precisely the material that undergoes regular sound change. English *five* is a direct inheritance
of PIE *pénkʷe. Saying a feature is directly inherited does not claim it is phonetically
unchanged, and the essay's own trailing phrase, "across five millennia of linguistic evolution,"
concedes the change explicitly rather than concealing it.

Third, the grade is misapplied on the project's own rubric. `needs-hedging` is for a contested
question stated flatly. That Germanic strong-verb ablaut is inherited from Indo-European ablaut is
not contested by anyone; it is the standard handbook statement (Zurich's Ad fontes tutorial puts
it in one sentence: "Die germanischen Sprachen haben den Ablaut aus ihrer ie. Vorstufe ererbt").
What Phase 1 actually objects to is imprecision about *what* is inherited, and the paragraph
already supplies that precision.

I would leave line 125 and German line 91 alone.

## What is actually true

Everything in Phase 1's factual account checks out, and I confirmed it independently:

- PIE *o, *a and *ə merged as Proto-Germanic *a, so the *a* of *sang* continues a PIE *o.
- PIE syllabic nasals gave Proto-Germanic *um / *un, so the *u* of *gesungen* continues *n̥.
- Proto-Germanic *e was raised to *i before nasal plus consonant, so the *i* of *singen* is not
  a preserved PIE *e.
- Analogy worked the results over again. Middle High German still opposed preterite singular
  *sanc* to plural *sungen*, and modern *sangen* is a rebuilding; *nehmen / nahm / genommen*
  likewise reflects levelling in both directions on top of Old High German *neman / nam / nāmun /
  ginoman*.

What that adds up to, though, is Phase 1's own conclusion: the alternation is continuously
inherited, the sounds are not preserved. The essay says the alternation is inherited. The two
statements agree. There is a residual softness in the word "preserved," which reads a shade more
static than "transmitted," but a sentence that ends "across five millennia of linguistic
evolution" is not inviting a reader to think nothing happened.

The one place where "direct" is genuinely generous is at the level of the *system* rather than
the alternation: Proto-Germanic did not merely receive PIE ablaut, it systematized it, converting
an accent-conditioned alternation spread across aspect stems into a seven-class tense-marking
paradigm with a preterite singular/plural split that PIE did not have. The same Ad fontes page
that says the ablaut was inherited says in the next breath that "Im Germanischen wird der Ablaut
bei den Verben systematisiert." But the essay's sentence is scoped to the alternations, not the
class system, and one sentence later the essay is off to the Yamnaya. Nothing here misinforms a
reader.

## Phase 1's replacement prose

> These vowel changes descend from Proto-Indo-European ablaut, reshaped by five millennia of sound
> change and still recognizable through all of it.

Not needed, and slightly worse than what it replaces on two counts.

"Reshaped by five millennia of sound change" names only sound change, when Phase 1's own argument
leans heavily on analogical levelling, which is not sound change and is the mechanism behind the
*sangen* and *nahm* rebuildings it cites. The replacement therefore drops the part of the story
that most justified writing it.

"Still recognizable through all of it" is the overcorrection in the other direction. PIE
*e / o / zero surfacing as German i / a / u is recognizable to a comparativist, not to the reader
of an app's Info screen, who has just been shown *singen, sang, gesungen* and told the vowels
changed. "Recognizable" invites the same misreading Phase 1 set out to prevent, only about the
sounds rather than the pattern.

Markup and mechanics are fine: no `~`, `$`, `‡`, `^` or heading backticks are disturbed, no
asterisks are introduced, house style is respected, and the splice against the following heading
is preserved. The prose is not wrong. It is a lateral move made to fix a defect that the
paragraph had already handled.

## Strongest case for the finding, and my answer

The strongest case: "direct" and "preserved" are the two words in that sentence a non-specialist
will weight most, they sit immediately below three triads whose changed vowels are printed in red,
and a reader who skims the setup sentence walks away believing PIE had something like i / a / u in
those slots. Essays are read at a skim, and the qualification lives in a subordinate clause of the
previous sentence, which is the easiest thing in a paragraph to lose. On that reading the essay
does not misstate the linguistics, but it arranges the page so that the wrong impression is the
cheap one to form.

My answer is that the qualification is not merely nearby, it is grammatically load-bearing. "These
vowel changes" has no referent at all except "the vowel alternations we still see in German strong
verbs today," and that phrase is the direct object of the clause that says the PIE vowels became
different vowels. A reader who skips the setup sentence does not get a wrong impression; he gets
no referent. And the German, which is the localization Phase 1 was worried about, spells the chain
out in three linked noun phrases. To sustain the finding I would have to hold that "direct
inheritance" implies phonetic identity, and it does not: inheritance is the category that regular
sound change operates *within*. That is the premise the finding needs and cannot have.

If someone still wanted the sentence tightened, the honest grade for it is `nitpick`, not
`needs-hedging`, because nothing about the claim is contested. I do not think it clears even that
bar.

  > vowel of the base," with "-o- as deflected grade of -e-, the normal grade; -ē- and -ō- as

- Wikipedia, "Sound change" <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound_change>

  > two exceptions to regular sound change: analogy and dialect borrowing." Cited only for the

- Wiktionary, "singen" <https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/singen>, cross-checked against the Old High

Note on Phase 1's own sourcing: its two citations are Britannica and Wikipedia. Both are correct
on the sound changes, and I reached the same phonology from the Texas grammar. The exposure is not
in the sources but in the inference drawn from them.
